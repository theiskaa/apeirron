# Apeirron's editorial standard

An Apeirron node is a focused essay about a question worth understanding. It should feel written by someone who has read carefully, decided what matters, and taken the trouble to make it clear.

The chosen voice is **plain, thoughtful, concrete, and open to doubt**. These are substantial essays, with enough space to understand the subject and its evidence. There is no routine word ceiling. The first roughly 1,100-word rewrites were much too short: they gave the outline while removing too much of the explanation. Cut repetition and loose tangents; preserve the reasoning, context, worked examples, and complications that make an essay worth reading.

## One thread, developed properly

The title names the topic. A governing question gives the essay a thread, and supporting questions supply its depth. An essay on consciousness needs room for the relationship between experience and the brain, the experiments that make it investigable, the competing explanations, and their limitations. Linking to the hard-problem node does not excuse explaining the hard problem poorly here.

Write that question, the supporting questions, and the essay's provisional answer before drafting. They are working notes, not required opening lines. Keep a paragraph when it supplies context, evidence, an explanation, a consequence, or an objection needed for that answer. Send a tangent to its own node when it would pull the essay off course. A link should offer further depth after a sufficient explanation here.

Cut repeated context before cutting the explanation a newcomer needs. The reader should never have to know the field already to follow why a detail matters.

## What depth actually adds

An experiment deserves more than its name and a verdict. Explain what the participant did, what was measured, what stayed the same, and why the result changed the question. In binocular rivalry, for example, the images remain fixed while perception alternates; saying that the brain stays unchanged would erase the very process being investigated.

A historical case needs the sequence that makes responsibility intelligible. A funding decision, a treatment, a later disclosure, and a family's challenge are different events. Give each enough space for the reader to understand how one changed the meaning of another. Listing their dates is not the same as telling that history.

A philosophical view needs a case it can explain and an objection it has to answer. A label followed by a link leaves the work to the reader. An archaeological interpretation needs the find, its context, and the steps connecting it to a claim about the past. A description of a carving and a proposed translation of it are different achievements.

Depth can require a long passage. It should bring the reader closer to the material, rather than repeat that the material is complex. If an essential section feels thin, add the missing explanation. If a passage repeats a conclusion, remove or rebuild it. These are separate editorial decisions.

## A structure that follows the thought

A useful starting shape is:

1. Orient the reader: what happened, or what is the problem?
2. Develop it through a few well-chosen details, explaining their significance.
3. Examine the difficulty: what objection, alternative, or missing evidence changes the reading?
4. Arrive somewhere: what can we reasonably say now, and where does that answer stop?

These are moves, not compulsory headings. History may need chronology and several closely examined episodes. Philosophy may need worked examples, competing accounts, and experimental evidence. Use as many specific sections as the material needs. Avoid a mandatory “origins / theories / implications / deeper question” assembly line.

The first few paragraphs should make the subject and focus clear. Do not withhold the premise to manufacture suspense. End when the argument is finished; the last paragraph does not need to announce that everything we know has changed.

## How the voice sounds

Use familiar words when they are exact. Keep necessary technical terms and explain them in place. Let sentences vary with the thought. Short sentences can clarify a turn; a string of dramatic fragments makes even serious material sound like a trailer.

Prefer paragraphs with one job, often two to five sentences. Break at a change of thought. Do not force every paragraph or sentence to the same size. Dates, full names, institutional titles, and numbers belong when the reader needs them, not as a display of research.

Have a point of view. Say which interpretation is better supported and why. An essay can be curious without being credulous, and decisive without pretending that every question is settled. Treat people fairly. Evidence determines how much space a claim deserves.

Keep the sense of discovery. Explain a compelling proposal well enough that the reader understands its attraction before weighing it. Repeatedly saying that something is unproven can become as mechanical as repeatedly calling it revolutionary. Identify the specific limitation once, then let the account move forward.

The sense of a person on the page comes from judgment: noticing a distinction, choosing a revealing example, admitting a limit. Do not manufacture it with typos, slang, invented memories, fake conversations, or an authorial “I” with no real author behind it.

## Evidence carries the weight

Keep three things distinguishable in ordinary prose: what the record establishes, how someone interprets it, and what remains conjecture. Name who is making a contested claim. Place the limitation near that claim rather than burying it in a closing disclaimer.

A missing file cannot establish its contents. A correlation cannot establish a mechanism. A similar date cannot establish contact. A failed explanation does not validate its rival. A documented abuse does not authenticate every later allegation made in its name.

These distinctions belong in descriptions, graph reasons, and tooltips as well as body text. An essay that qualifies a claim while its connection reason calls it proven has not been edited successfully.

Use direct links to evidence where the reader might reasonably ask “how do we know?” End with a compact `## Sources` bibliography. Keep a source because the essay uses it. A long reading list does not make unsupported prose reliable.

## Habits to remove

| Habit | Why it fails | Editorial move |
| --- | --- | --- |
| “The question beneath all questions” | Advertises significance before establishing it. | Name the actual question. |
| “This is not X. It is Y.” repeated through a draft | Substitutes a familiar cadence for reasoning. | State the distinction once, then explain it. |
| “Devastating,” “unprecedented,” “the most consequential” | Tells the reader how impressed to be. | Supply the fact that earns the judgment. |
| A parade of theories or famous names | Broadens coverage while weakening the thread. | Develop the few the argument needs; link to the rest. |
| “Both sides” regardless of evidence | Confuses fairness with equal credibility. | Explain serious claims and weigh their support. |
| “Perhaps… or perhaps…” as a final insinuation | Leaves an unsupported claim hanging as a revelation. | State the evidence and the specific unresolved question. |
| The same conclusion in every section | Makes length feel like progress. | Let each section add something. |
| An ominous connection appended to every topic | Converts the graph into a theory of everything. | Make the relationship explicit and proportionate. |

These are revision prompts, not banned strings or an AI detector. A phrase can be useful in context. Fix the reasoning before substituting vocabulary.

## Examples from the first review

**Before:** “Every answer rewrites the world. And the honest truth is: we do not have the answer yet. We barely have the right question.”

**Possible revision:** “The disagreement is partly about what an explanation would have to explain. Finding the brain processes involved in an experience may settle that question for one researcher and leave it open for another.”

The revision identifies the difficulty instead of asking uncertainty to sound profound.

**Before:** “Too sophisticated, too early, too sudden to fit any other explanation.”

**Possible revision:** “The buildings show that these communities could organize substantial work. They do not establish that the builders inherited their skills from a lost civilization.”

The revision separates the observation from the additional claim. It also gives the builders credit for what the evidence attributes to them.

## The release bar

An essay is ready when an editor can answer yes to all seven:

- Can a new reader identify its question and follow the argument without a glossary or neighboring essays?
- Can the reader explain how the important evidence was obtained, how the main accounts work, and what makes their objections serious? A readable outline is not enough.
- Does each section add something needed, with tangents handed to other nodes?
- Are the important claims traceable, with uncertainty and attribution kept beside them?
- Does the essay make a reasoned judgment without mocking people or inflating weak evidence?
- Does it sound natural aloud, without repeated rhetorical scaffolding or a manufactured finale?
- Do its description, links, and graph reasons describe what the essay actually supports?

Do not replace these judgments with a readability score. A useful word count or a long paragraph can flag a place to look; neither can tell whether an essay is good.
