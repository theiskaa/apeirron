On 5 December 2005, Milton Friedman argued that the destruction of New Orleans’s schools should become an occasion for permanent reform. His [proposal in the Wall Street Journal](https://miltonfriedman.hoover.org/internal/media/dispatcher/214456/full) was specific: give parents publicly funded vouchers, allow different kinds of providers to compete and make clear that the arrangement would continue after the emergency.

For Friedman, the storm had exposed a failing system and created a chance to replace it. For Naomi Klein, that way of describing a disaster revealed a political danger. People whose homes and institutions had been damaged were being offered a new arrangement while struggling to regain the capacity to decide what they wanted.

Her 2007 book *The Shock Doctrine* asks us to notice that imbalance. A crisis can make action urgent while leaving some participants much better prepared than others to shape it. The fight then concerns decisions that may outlast the emergency: what must happen immediately, what becomes permanent and who gets to distinguish between the two.

## What the doctrine claims

In the [introduction excerpt published on her website](https://tsd.naomiklein.org/shock-doctrine/excerpt/index.html), Klein connects market restructuring after coups, wars and natural disasters. She argues that organized advocates use disruption to advance policies that would encounter greater resistance under ordinary conditions. Her term “disaster capitalism” joins a political method to the commercial opportunities created by reconstruction.

A hurricane can create an opening for people who had no role in causing it. Deliberately creating a crisis, exploiting an existing one and recommending a poor response are different accusations. Evidence for one cannot simply be transferred to the others.

Nor is having ideas ready inherently sinister. A government should have plans for restoring water, reopening schools and providing temporary housing. Organizations outside government can have useful proposals too. The conflict begins when preparedness gives one group the power to attach its preferred long-term settlement to relief that others cannot wait to receive.

“Neoliberalism” needs care in this argument. It is often used for a family of policies favoring private ownership, competition, trade liberalization and limits on direct state provision. Those policies are not interchangeable. Selling a public company changes ownership; allowing new providers changes entry; issuing a voucher changes how a subsidy reaches a household. Each can leave the state with substantial responsibilities for funding, rules and enforcement.

Public responsibility can move rather than disappear. The state might stop running a service while continuing to pay for it and decide who qualifies. That leaves questions about the provider’s authority and the public’s means of challenging it.

The political loss can occur when alternatives disappear from consideration. A reform might already have supporters before a crisis. That does not establish a mandate for its entire design, but neither does it make the supporters intruders who invented their concerns afterward. The record should show what had been proposed, where it had met resistance and what the emergency changed about the ability to approve it.

An old proposal followed by a disaster is therefore the beginning of an explanation. It becomes more persuasive when there is a decision tying urgently needed resources to that proposal, a change in who has authority or a timetable that affected people cannot meaningfully use. Those decisions show how the opening was used.

A crisis can expose failures that had been hidden or tolerated, giving people good reasons to reconsider an institution. Their change of mind need not depend on manipulation. To distinguish a reassessment from an imposed settlement, we need to know whose judgment changed and how it could be expressed. Widespread suffering is not itself evidence of either consent or opposition.

## Chile: the content of the advice

Chile is a central case because market-oriented recommendations were made under a dictatorship, with ordinary democratic opposition suppressed. A [decree-law listing preserved by Chile’s National Library](https://www.memoriachilena.gob.cl/archivos2/pdfs/MC0053666.pdf) records the dissolution of Congress in September 1973. Economic decisions in that setting did not pass through a functioning elected legislature.

Friedman met Augusto Pinochet in March 1975 and wrote to him on 21 April. An [excerpt of the letter reproduced by the Université d’Artois](https://www.univ-artois.fr/sites/default/files/2024-09/Guide%20AIA%202024-2025.pdf) separates two aims: ending inflation and strengthening a private market economy. Friedman argued for rapid stabilization despite transitional unemployment, expecting a relatively short period of severe difficulty and a quick recovery. He also praised measures already taken, favored further trade liberalization and acknowledged an argument for allowing producers time to adjust.

The proposed speed varied with the policy. Stabilization was to be rapid; trade reform could allow adjustment, though Friedman wanted it to go further. A prediction that pain will be brief carries a burden of justification when those expected to bear it cannot freely replace the government making the decision.

Friedman’s [1976 reply to critics](https://miltonfriedman.hoover.org/internal/media/dispatcher/215090/full) denied that he held the role of economic adviser to the junta. He acknowledged the visit and meeting and defended giving technical advice on inflation despite his opposition to the authoritarian political system. His comparison was to medical advice that could relieve a country’s suffering.

His objection was to how the relationship was described, not to the fact of the meeting. Inflation can harm people under an authoritarian government as well as a democratic one. An economist can sincerely believe that useful advice should not be withheld from the population because its rulers are oppressive.

The economic recommendation still involved judgments about timing, losses, support for people affected and the obligations the state should retain. Expertise could inform those judgments; it could not supply the missing political consent. A prescription can be sincere and still help a government carry out a program its opponents cannot safely challenge.

The reproduced excerpt establishes the recommendations it contains. It does not, by itself, establish which recommendations Chilean officials adopted, how each affected the economy or whether repression was undertaken to secure a particular policy. Those questions require the decisions and outcomes inside Chile. Calling the whole period a laboratory can obscure the Chilean officials, institutions and citizens whose actions would have to be examined.

## A different kind of opening in New Orleans

The New Orleans case involves a natural disaster and decisions made within a constitutional system. It should not be treated as an interchangeable instance of military rule. Its relevance lies in the uneven ability to participate while institutions were being rebuilt.

The change had a legal route and a timetable. Louisiana’s [Act 35](https://www.legis.la.gov/Legis/ViewDocument.aspx?d=329650), enacted in the 2005 extraordinary session, expanded the transfer of schools into the state Recovery School District. Its early provisions changed the criteria for transfer and gave the recovery district authority over operation and management. The [legislative record](https://www.legis.la.gov/Legis/BillInfo.aspx?i=103804) gives an effective date of 30 November 2005.

Friedman’s voucher article appeared five days later. The takeover law was already in effect when he publicly advocated his voucher plan; the publication cannot establish that the article set the law in motion. His article itself referred to the state takeover. Treating him as the author of every subsequent change would confuse influence through ideas with control over particular decisions.

The proposal and the eventual system also differed. Vouchers help families pay for schooling through participating providers, including private schools. The New Orleans charter reforms gave operators responsibility for publicly funded schools under contracts. Both use choice and competition, but their governance, obligations and funding arrangements need separate examination.

The shift in control did not depend on withdrawing public funding. The relevant question is who can make decisions about staffing, enrollment and the conditions of a school’s continued operation—not simply whether a public building was sold.

## What changed, and what the research can measure

The Education Research Alliance for New Orleans’s [2025 synthesis](https://educationresearchalliancenola.org/files/publications/Key-Conclusions-Digital-Version-July-28th-Revised_2025-07-28-201830_bzxl.pdf) describes state takeover, the eventual conversion of nearly all public schools to charter operation, dismissal of district educators and the expiration of the union contract. It also describes decisions made with limited community input while residents and leaders were dispersed. The reforms drew on policies and organizations that predated the storm.

Displacement altered the practical terms of participation. A family did not have to be literally incapable of thought for displacement and immediate need to weaken its ability to attend meetings or influence a policy timetable.

Student outcomes complicate the indictment. In a [2018 study summary](https://educationresearchalliancenola.org/publications/what-effect-did-the-new-orleans-school-reforms-have-on-student-achievement-high-school-graduation-and-college-outcomes), Douglas Harris and Matthew Larsen reported improvements in test scores, high-school graduation and college outcomes. Their analysis examined outcomes through 2014 and used comparisons with similar students and schools elsewhere in Louisiana, including other hurricane-affected districts, rather than relying on a before-and-after description alone.

A before-and-after average would leave much of the upheaval out of view. If the students returning to a city differ from those who left, a rise in average scores need not show that schools became more effective. A comparison group and adjustments for student characteristics help address that problem. They do not turn a large social upheaval into a randomized experiment or identify the effect of every separate component of reform.

In a simple illustration, suppose average scores rose by ten points in the reformed system and four in a suitable comparison system. The first number alone would credit reform with changes that also occurred elsewhere. Subtracting the second leaves six points to explain. That is a more informative comparison, provided the groups would otherwise have followed similar paths. It is not a guarantee that every remaining difference came from the policy. These are invented figures, not the New Orleans estimates.

The [2018 policy brief](https://educationresearchalliancenola.org/files/publications/071518-Harris-Larsen-What-Effect-Did-the-New-Orleans-School-Reforms-Have-on-Student-Achievement-High-School-Graduation-and-College-Outcomes.pdf) explains the two approaches: following returning students over time and comparing different cohorts before and after the changes. Each reaches some outcomes more readily than others. The authors assess the overall reform package; the comparison does not isolate a single feature as the cause of every improvement.

The later synthesis also finds costs and cautions: changes to the teaching workforce, continuing problems of trust and limits on copying the results elsewhere. It distinguishes charter results from less favorable findings for the state’s voucher program. Market-oriented reforms in the same state did not all yield the same outcomes.

Governance continued to change. The 2025 account describes the 2018 return of state-controlled schools to local district governance while operators retained day-to-day responsibilities. A return of local authority did not simply restore the old arrangement. Residents could again direct complaints to local elected representatives whose power over staffing and instruction differed from what it had been before the storm.

That division helps explain why the name of the governing body is not enough. A school board can have responsibility for contracts without employing teachers or choosing a school’s curriculum. A parent trying to resolve a problem needs to know which body can actually act. Restoring a local institution can leave unresolved arguments over what that institution should be able to do.

Improvements for students matter. So do the losses imposed on educators and the authority residents could exercise over public institutions. A finding about one cannot quietly stand in for a finding about all the others.

## Results do not settle who should decide

A policy can produce a measurable benefit and still have been adopted through an unjust process. A policy can also have broad support and fail at its stated purpose. Democratic authority and effectiveness are connected standards, not substitutes.

Suppose a school becomes more effective at helping students complete their education. That is a real benefit, including to families with little money or political influence. It would be wrong to dismiss it merely because the change was promoted by people whose motives one distrusts. But the improvement does not tell us whether teachers were treated fairly, whether families had meaningful alternatives or whether residents should have had greater control over the transition.

The reverse mistake is to assume that consultation would have required preserving the prior system. Residents can want change. Some may favor a different operator, others better support for the existing school, and others a different admissions arrangement. Giving them a voice means allowing those differences to affect the decision, rather than treating “the community” as a single position already known to an outside critic or reformer.

Preparation and access give some participants an advantage. Who has staff, access to officials and a complete proposal? Who is looking for a place to live or a school their child can reach? A formal opportunity to comment can coexist with a large difference in the ability to use it.

## Urgency is a reason to specify the decision

Emergencies create genuine time constraints. Waiting to restore a basic service can deepen a disaster. Yet urgency is often uneven across the components of a proposal.

Consider a hypothetical city whose drinking-water system has been damaged. It may need to hire emergency crews immediately. That does not make a fifty-year operating concession equally urgent. The crews, the temporary management arrangement and the future ownership structure are decisions with different time horizons, even if they appear in one package.

The distinction is not between helpful public action and suspect private activity. A private firm can supply indispensable pumps or skilled workers. A public authority can use an emergency to expand its own power. In either case, the terms determine what happens after immediate relief: who sets prices, who can end the arrangement and who bears the cost if service is poor.

Emergency crews could start work while the city reviews its longer commitment. Sometimes the immediate work and longer arrangement will be technically or financially connected. Then officials should explain the connection: why a shorter arrangement will not work, what a delay would cost and which options would disappear once the contract is signed.

The same scrutiny applies to an advocate’s claim that its preferred reform was inevitable. Damage creates problems that need answers; it does not select one answer from all the feasible alternatives. An institution that failed before a disaster need not be restored unchanged, but the failure does not grant unlimited authority to whoever arrives with a plan.

Prepared proposals gain force because others have less time to assess them. Publishing the assumptions, identifying beneficiaries and preserving a point at which the choice can be reconsidered can change that imbalance. A deadline should state what must be done by then, rather than make every provision in a package urgent by association.

## Where the comparison stops

A coup, hyperinflation and a hurricane do not weaken opposition in the same way. In one case a government can use force against opponents; in another, households face an economic emergency; in another, physical displacement obstructs participation. A shared name should not conceal those differences.

The word “shock” cannot establish a shared chain of command between people working in different settings. Nor can a general pattern identify the purpose of every reform that follows a crisis. A case needs the proposed change, the people able to advance it, the altered constraints and the decision by which it took effect.

The New Orleans documents give the argument a firm subject: an explicit appeal for permanent change, a legal transfer of authority and later research identifying both gains and costs. There is enough to argue about without treating every reconstruction project as the same operation.

The lasting issue is what happens to the choices that a crisis compresses. People can need help now while retaining a claim to decide how their institutions will be governed later. Rebuilding should leave them with more than a completed arrangement they had no practical chance to shape.
