In November 2009, the Defense Intelligence Agency considered a request from Senator Harry Reid to put an aerospace research effort under tighter secrecy. Reid believed its findings deserved the protection of a restricted special access program. The agency's written assessment was much less excited. Its reviewers could not find adequate grounds for the designation. The reports delivered so far were unclassified, and the agency could not identify the damage that releasing their publicly available information would cause.

That memo is an awkward document for a simple story of Pentagon disclosure. A powerful senator was asking for more secrecy; intelligence officials were saying the material did not justify it. Eight years later, reporting about the same research effort would help revive public interest in [[ufos]], with Reid among those arguing that the subject deserved attention.

The history contains both movements. People inside and outside government sought to investigate unusual encounters, protect research, obtain funding, and publicize selected findings. Their purposes sometimes overlapped and sometimes conflicted. Following those decisions tells us more than imagining a single institution gradually admitting what it had always known.

## Before the famous acronym

On December 16, 2017, Helene Cooper, Ralph Blumenthal, and Leslie Kean reported in *The New York Times* on a Pentagon effort known as the Advanced Aerospace Threat Identification Program, or AATIP. The article connected $22 million in funding, Reid's support, contractor Robert Bigelow, Navy encounters, and intelligence officer Luis Elizondo. Elizondo had left the Defense Department and argued that the encounters were not receiving the attention they warranted.

For many readers the surprise was institutional. A subject usually encountered through witness stories and disputed photographs now had a budget, an intelligence-agency connection, and named military observers. Those details made questions about the work answerable: who paid, what was commissioned, who received the results?

The paperwork complicates the name. The contracted research was the Advanced Aerospace Weapon System Applications Program, generally abbreviated AAWSAP. A [2008 solicitation and statement of objectives](https://documents2.theblackvault.com/documents/dia/AAWSAP/SOW_Aerospace.pdf) survives in the DIA's released records. It sought studies of possible breakthroughs in aerospace weapons, looking ahead to 2050. Its subjects included propulsion, power, materials, signatures, and human effects. The procurement notice was publicly posted, although prospective work could involve classified information.

The 2008 procurement also gives us a date attached to a defined action. The 2017 account traced the effort's beginnings to 2007, while the released notice records the solicitation the following year. Political sponsorship, obtaining funds, awarding a contract, and producing the first deliverables need not happen in the same year. Treating one date as the birthday of everything called AATIP creates a continuity the records then have to be made to fit.

The contract went to Bigelow Aerospace Advanced Space Studies, or BAASS. Bigelow's interest was broader than ordinary aerospace forecasting. He had funded research into UFO reports and purported paranormal events, including work at [[skinwalker-ranch]] in Utah. James Lacatski, the DIA program manager, and BAASS researcher Colm Kelleher later described that wider effort with George Knapp in *Skinwalkers at the Pentagon*. Their account is a participant history: valuable for understanding what they thought they were doing, while still requiring comparison with the contract and the work delivered.

These layers matter because the familiar description “the Pentagon's secret UFO program” makes them sound identical. A contract can authorize one task while a contractor pursues a wider interpretation of it. A manager can encourage work that the agency's leadership later considers outside the intended scope. A public notice can exist without the public understanding the project behind it. None of those situations is especially easy to reconstruct from an acronym alone.

Reid supported the funding with Senators Ted Stevens and Daniel Inouye. The amount was small beside the defense budget but substantial for a concentrated research effort. Their involvement gives the program a political history as well as an intelligence history. This was not simply a bureaucracy acting beyond elected officials' knowledge: prominent legislators helped make it possible. Whether oversight was adequate is a further question about who knew which parts, what reporting was required, and how performance was evaluated.

## The request for greater secrecy

Reid's June 2009 request offers a closer view of those relationships. A special access program imposes restrictions beyond the ordinary handling of classified information. Such protection might matter if a project exposed a sensitive capability or a vulnerable source. Requesting it did not establish that the research had produced such a result.

The [DIA review dated November 13, 2009](https://documents2.theblackvault.com/documents/dia/AAWSAP/U-09-2660CE-IM-Review_of_Special_Access_Program_Request.pdf) addressed that point directly. It identified the contract behind Reid's AATIP terminology and examined the deliverables. The first year's material was unclassified. Some later reports were expected to incorporate classified information about foreign research, but the reviewers did not see a basis for restricting the entire program in the way Reid requested.

The disagreement was about the work in hand. Reid described sensitive, unconventional findings; the reviewers could not locate reports whose contents warranted the proposed protection. That leaves a specific historical question about what Reid believed he was protecting and how it related to what DIA had received. It gives us no reason to silently promote his expectation into a finding accepted by the agency.

A reader can also see how misleading the word “secret” becomes when it covers every part of this history. An obscure appropriation, an unclassified report marked for official use, a classified technical annex, and a restricted special access program describe different arrangements. Knowing which applied changes who could see the work and what could lawfully be released.

The reviewers were writing before the later publicity, to advise an administrative decision. They could have underestimated the project's value. Their memo nevertheless records what they had received and why it failed to persuade them. Put beside Reid's confidence, it lets us see the disagreement as it stood in 2009, before either position became part of a public argument about disclosure.

## What the research produced

A [DIA letter to congressional leaders](https://irp.cdn-website.com/a8004d23/files/uploaded/aatip-list.pdf), dated January 9, 2018, listed 38 associated reports. The subjects ranged from nuclear propulsion and high-energy lasers to invisibility cloaking, traversable wormholes, and warp-drive concepts. The list helps explain why the program continues to attract attention. It is unusual to find such topics gathered beneath an intelligence-agency letterhead.

The titles can also create a false impression of what a technical report contains. A survey of a possible propulsion concept can examine equations, obstacles, published experiments, and speculative extensions. Commissioning it does not mean the agency built the proposed device, saw one flying, or recovered one from elsewhere. The argument, calculations, and evidence inside a report have to bear that weight.

Suppose an analyst is asked how a competitor might move a vehicle without the familiar exhaust of a jet. Surveying unconventional propulsion could be a reasonable response to the assignment. The resulting paper would still be a study of possibilities. To connect it to a particular observed object would require evidence about that object's motion, energy use, and interaction with its surroundings. The paper's existence cannot supply those missing measurements.

The report list also records a more ordinary distinction inside the supposedly uniform black program. Most listed products were unclassified and marked for official use; one was a secret version of a laser-weapons report. A subject as unfamiliar as a wormhole did not automatically make a document classified. Classification depended on the contents and their handling, not on how startling a title sounded to a reader encountering it years later.

The distinction becomes especially important when the reports are presented beside Navy footage. A clip provides an observation from a particular instrument. A technical study explores a class of mechanisms. The two can motivate questions about each other without forming a demonstrated explanation. Placing them in the same news story does not close the gap.

The contractor's broader investigations create a second issue. Accounts of encounters, interviews, databases, and reported effects on people are research activities, but their value depends on methods and records. How was a case selected? Was an interview recorded before witnesses could influence one another? Were alternative explanations tested? Could another investigator reproduce an analysis? A budget and a government customer answer none of those questions automatically.

AARO's [2024 historical review](https://www.aaro.mil/Portals/136/PDFs/AARO_Historical_Record_Report_Volume_1_2024.pdf) criticized the program's utility and described paranormal investigations that it regarded as outside DIA's intended task. Participants dispute the dismissive reading of their work. The disagreement is best pursued through the deliverables and decisions, rather than by granting either later account unrestricted authority. Some documents are now available; a participant's claim about a much larger body of research still needs the corresponding material if the public is to evaluate it.

## What continued under AATIP?

The publicly reported end of dedicated funding in 2012 did not mean everyone associated with the subject lost interest. Elizondo has described continuing work on military encounters and has presented himself as AATIP's director. His memoir *Imminent* supplies his account of the work and the resistance he says he encountered.

Later official descriptions have drawn a sharper administrative boundary. AARO's historical review says that, after AAWSAP ended, people used AATIP for an informal community of interest inside Defense, without a dedicated budget or staff. That account permits people to have investigated reports while denying that their activity constituted a separately authorized program. It differs from the impression of a continuously funded office conveyed by many retellings.

This can sound like a dispute over office labels, but the labels carry responsibility. Who could task personnel? Who held the records? Did someone have authority to request data from another service? Was an investigation an assigned duty or work pursued alongside one? Those differences affect both what could be accomplished and what records an agency should be able to produce.

Elizondo's departure in 2017 brought the dispute into public view. He said bureaucratic resistance prevented the issue from reaching senior decision-makers. That is his explanation for leaving, and it deserves to be read as a claim by a participant with access and a stake in the outcome. Accepting that he was frustrated does not require accepting every inference he drew from the encounters.

He then joined To The Stars Academy of Arts and Sciences, founded by musician Tom DeLonge. Christopher Mellon, a former senior defense official, and physicist Hal Puthoff were among the people associated with the venture. It combined public advocacy, scientific ambitions, and commercial entertainment. That mixture gave the story reach while leaving readers to distinguish its research claims from its promotional purposes.

Puthoff's earlier involvement in government-funded remote-viewing research supplies a real personnel connection with [[project-stargate]]. It explains something about the network through which unusual subjects found sponsors and audiences. It does not make Star Gate, AAWSAP, and To The Stars one continuously authorized organization. A person can carry interests and relationships from one setting into another without carrying the same program with them.

## Three videos, several different claims

The public footage became easier to describe accurately on April 27, 2020, when the [Defense Department officially released three Navy videos](https://www.defense.gov/News/Releases/Release/Article/2165713/statement-by-the-department-of-defense-on-the-release-of-historical-navy-videos/). One was recorded in 2004, two in 2015. The release confirmed their Navy provenance and said the phenomena remained unidentified. It also explained that the department was clarifying misconceptions about whether the circulating footage was genuine and authorized.

That was useful authentication. It established a source for the clips, rather than endorsing every speed estimate or technological interpretation attached to them. A genuine instrument recording can still be difficult to interpret, especially when a short public extract lacks the surrounding observations.

The 2004 FLIR clip is associated with the *Nimitz* encounters. David Fravor described seeing a white, elongated object near a disturbance on the ocean surface. His [2023 written congressional testimony](https://oversight.house.gov/hearing/unidentified-anomalous-phenomena-implications-on-national-security-public-safety-and-government-transparency/) gives his account of approaching it and observing motion he considered extraordinary. The infrared recording came from a later flight by Chad Underwood. It is not a continuous film of Fravor's encounter.

Combining those sources requires care. Fravor's recollection, reports of shipboard radar tracks, and Underwood's image can strengthen one another only where their timing and relationship are established. A number repeated from a witness interview is not equivalent to an available radar measurement. Nor does the object leaving a camera's field of view, by itself, establish its acceleration. The sensor may move, tracking may change, and range may be uncertain.

Gimbal and GoFast, both from 2015, pose their own questions. In Gimbal, a dark infrared image appears to rotate. Establishing physical rotation requires knowing what the optics and stabilization system contribute to the displayed shape. A viewer cannot simply treat that shape as the outline of a craft seen by daylight. The excited voices help document the crew's reaction; they do not calibrate the image.

GoFast offers a particularly useful correction. The object appears to race over the sea, but its distance above the water changes the geometry. In a [case analysis published in February 2025](https://www.aaro.mil/Portals/136/PDFs/case_resolution_reports/AARO_GoFast_Case_Resolution_Card_Methodology_Final.pdf), AARO assessed its altitude at roughly 13,000 feet and found no anomalous speed. The office did not definitively identify the object. It separated an uncertainty about what the target was from a claim about how fast it moved.

Parallax explains why that separation matters. From a moving car, a nearby post sweeps rapidly across the view while a distant hill barely shifts. Much of the apparent movement comes from the observer. An airborne camera adds changes of heading, pointing angle, and wind. Solving that geometry can remove an apparent performance anomaly while leaving the object's identity unresolved. That is a result, not an evasion of the original question.

## The change in reporting

The [June 2021 ODNI preliminary assessment](https://www.dni.gov/files/ODNI/documents/assessments/Prelimary-Assessment-UAP-20210625.pdf) considered 144 reports from 2004 to 2021, mostly from military sources. It identified one with high confidence as a deflating balloon and could not resolve the others from the available information. It also described limited high-quality reporting and the need for a tailored collection process.

The unresolved total was not a count of 143 vehicles proven to exceed known technology. In a limited number of incidents, observers reported unusual behavior that required further analysis, including checks for sensor errors, spoofing, and misperception. Multiple sensors suggested many reports concerned physical objects. Their origins could still differ.

For a pilot, that was reason enough to improve the system. An unknown object in training airspace can be a collision risk, a drone, an intelligence platform, or something whose nature cannot yet be established. A reporting process should preserve the observation before a witness decides whether its implications will sound embarrassing. Better records serve aviation safety even if every eventual identification is ordinary.

The UAP Task Force was followed by further organizational changes, including the establishment of AARO in 2022. Their creation made responsibility for collection and analysis more explicit. The practical test is whether the reports become usable. Sensor data must survive; incidents need enough context to be correlated; a conclusion should explain the uncertainties behind it. The number of reports alone is a poor measure. A rise can reflect easier reporting, new coverage, or changed rules as well as more events.

The unremarkable details of collection are often decisive. A timestamp lets an analyst compare two sensors; an instrument setting can explain why a target changed appearance; a location allows weather and traffic to be checked. If those details are lost, an interview years later may preserve the surprise without recovering the measurement. A useful reporting system has to save what an investigator will need before anyone knows whether a case will become famous.

## Allegations reach Congress

At the July 26, 2023 House hearing, witnesses described different kinds of evidence. Ryan Graves discussed aviation encounters and reporting conditions. Fravor described an encounter he had personally observed. David Grusch alleged a concealed effort to retrieve and reverse-engineer nonhuman technology, drawing on information he said he had obtained through his work and interviews.

The setting made those allegations consequential. Legislators could seek names, locations, records, and testimony through appropriate channels. Grusch's willingness to testify under oath gave his statements a formal status, but it did not turn information conveyed to him into independently established fact. His public appearance did not include an authenticated recovered vehicle or biological sample.

The complaint and oversight questions also have their own scope. A finding that a whistleblower's complaint deserves investigation is not necessarily a finding that every underlying allegation is true. Claims about retaliation, obstruction, access to information, and the origin of alleged material need to be followed separately. One can be substantiated while another remains unresolved.

AARO reported in March 2024 that its review had not found evidence confirming possession or reverse engineering of extraterrestrial technology. Anyone challenging that conclusion should be able to point toward the evidence the office missed or mishandled. The office, in turn, owes as much explanation of its methods and access as it can provide. An official conclusion becomes more useful when its basis can be examined.

Congress also pursued records legislation. Sections 1841–1843 of the enacted fiscal 2024 defense authorization law required a UAP records collection at the National Archives and agency review of relevant holdings. The [Archives' guidance](https://www.archives.gov/records-mgmt/uap-guidance) specifies identification, digital copying, and transfer procedures. It is a less dramatic development than a hearing about recovered craft, but it can make future work less dependent on somebody's account of a document nobody else has seen.

## Disclosure without a promised ending

The word “disclosure” often carries a destination inside it. Each release becomes a step toward an expected revelation, and an ordinary explanation looks like a delay or retreat. The record since 2017 is less orderly. Publicity exposed a funded research effort. Released documents complicated its name, mandate, and results. Video authentication established provenance. Geometric analysis changed a claim about motion. Hearings opened routes for investigating allegations without publicly settling them.

Secrecy runs through this history, but so does disagreement over how much secrecy was justified. Reid's 2009 request is a useful place to keep that complexity in view. If we assume that every official involved already knew the same extraordinary truth, we lose the dispute the memo records. We also lose the ability to ask who made a poor decision, whose judgment prevailed, and what the money bought.

The [[deep-state]] and [[shadow-elite]] interpretations offer larger stories about hidden authority. This case can inform those arguments only through specific acts and chains of responsibility. Legislative sponsorship, a contractor's unusual interests, and an intelligence agency's internal objection are details to explain, not interchangeable signs of one intention. Likewise, a contract involving speculative aerospace research does not establish the technology proposed by the [[breakaway-civilization]] thesis, any more than the secrecy of [[area-51]] identifies every object reported nearby.

The most valuable further disclosure would make a claim easier to check: a contract file establishing an authorization, source measurements permitting a reconstruction, a contemporary report linking an allegation to a named office. Each would answer a question the present record leaves open. There need be no promised final revelation for that work to matter. The history becomes more intelligible whenever a document lets us distinguish what someone hoped, what someone funded, and what someone actually found.
