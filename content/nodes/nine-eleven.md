At 8:46 on the morning of September 11, 2001, American Airlines Flight 11 struck the North Tower of the World Trade Center. United Airlines Flight 175 struck the South Tower seventeen minutes later. American Airlines Flight 77 hit the Pentagon at 9:37. United Airlines Flight 93 crashed near Shanksville, Pennsylvania, at 10:03 after passengers and crew tried to regain control. The attacks killed 2,977 people, not counting the nineteen hijackers. Both towers fell that morning. A third building at the World Trade Center, which no plane had struck, collapsed late in the afternoon.

The evidence for who carried out the attacks is stronger than the evidence for some of the decisions that preceded them. That is partly a difference in records. Airline and financial records, calls from the aircraft, and air traffic recordings can be checked against one another. Knowing why someone let a warning sit in a file often requires reconstructing what was on that person’s desk, what it meant at the time, and who else might have acted. The most serious failures are visible even where the archive cannot tell us what every official thought.

## Reconstructing the morning

Chapter 1 of the [9/11 Commission Report](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/GPO-911REPORT/pdf/GPO-911REPORT.pdf) brings together records that were scattered while the attacks were in progress. Flight attendants Betty Ong and Amy Sweeney separately reached people on the ground from Flight 11 and reported what they could see or had learned from their colleagues. Air traffic controllers were tracking the same aircraft from outside it. Some of the people who called from Flight 93 learned about the earlier crashes from people on the ground and said that passengers intended to resist. The cockpit recording and radar record establish a sequence that is consistent with those calls. For Flight 77, the radar track and contemporary reports from the aircraft and the ground carry more weight than an indistinct frame from a security camera. The poor quality of a particular image is a limit on that image, not on the rest of the record.

Establishing that four planes were hijacked is different from identifying who organized it. The Commission’s reconstruction follows travel, flight training, and financial records connecting the nineteen men to one another and to al-Qaeda. Its account identifies Khalid Sheikh Mohammed as the planner who brought the proposal to Osama bin Laden and traces money and communications through intermediaries. The parts of that account that depend on statements from prisoners deserve particular care. In its note on the sources for Chapter 5, the Commission says it could submit questions for use in interrogations but could not interview the prisoners or their interrogators itself. Those reports should not carry the same weight as records made before the attack. Doubt about a particular conversation in an al-Qaeda camp leaves the independent records of the plot in place.

The Commission did not insist on the same conditions for all its evidence. Its own announcement describes a joint, closed interview with Bush and Vice President Dick Cheney. Readers can set the published daily briefing beside the report’s description of Bush’s recollection, but the announcement supplies no transcript from which to judge how the commissioners pressed him on it. Investigators had a better record of the air defense response: recordings let them compare later testimony with what people actually said that morning.

Military officials initially said the fighters launched from Langley had been sent after Flight 77 or Flight 93. The recordings show that the order followed a report that Flight 11 was still airborne and heading toward Washington. Flight 11 had already hit the North Tower. The air defense command did not learn that Flight 77 was missing until 9:34, about three minutes before it hit the Pentagon. That correction matters: it shows that the people charged with defending Washington were acting on the wrong information and that early public testimony gave a misleading picture of what they had known. It does not show that someone ordered the fighters to stay away.

## Warnings with no owner

Nawaf al-Hazmi and Khalid al-Mihdhar came to the attention of U.S. intelligence in connection with a January 2000 meeting in Kuala Lumpur. The CIA had a cable recording Mihdhar’s U.S. visa and later received one reporting that Hazmi had flown to Los Angeles. The information did not produce a timely watchlist entry or a domestic search. When analysts revisited the old cables in August 2001, immigration records showed that Mihdhar had entered the country again in July. Both names were added to the watchlist on August 24. The FBI lead went to New York four days later, marked routine. The agent assigned to it was working his first counterterrorism lead.

An agent investigating the 2000 bombing of the USS *Cole* wanted to help. He was told that his role in a criminal investigation kept him on the wrong side of the rules separating criminal and intelligence work. The Commission found that he could have looked for Mihdhar as part of the existing *Cole* case. This was not simply a bad rule waiting to be repealed. People with different responsibilities misunderstood the rules, and nobody senior enough to settle the dispute made the search a priority. The two men had used their real names in the United States. Finding them by September 11 was far from assured, but there was something concrete to do and very little time left to do it.

The warning President George W. Bush received on August 6 was different. The briefing, titled “Bin Ladin Determined to Strike in US,” mentioned suspicious activity consistent with preparations for hijackings. It did not name the four flights, give a date, or describe the plan that unfolded. That makes it too vague to serve as evidence that the White House knew what was about to happen. It was still serious enough to warrant follow-up; the Commission found that the briefing prompted no National Security Council meeting on a possible domestic strike. Meanwhile, a Phoenix agent’s memo about aviation schools went essentially nowhere, and the FBI’s Minneapolis office had detained Zacarias Moussaoui but could not get headquarters to seek the surveillance warrant it wanted to search his belongings. The Commission found no sign that senior officials had put those leads and the Hazmi–Mihdhar search together before the attacks.

## Who helped in California

The search that started too late in August 2001 also helps explain why a lease signed the previous year matters. Omar al-Bayoumi met Hazmi and Mihdhar at a restaurant in Southern California in February 2000, helped them find an apartment in San Diego, and co-signed their lease. He supplied the deposit by check when the landlord would not accept cash. The Commission reported that the men reimbursed him at once. The help itself is established; whether the meeting was arranged and what Bayoumi knew about the men are different questions.

Part IV of the earlier [congressional Joint Inquiry](https://intelligence.house.gov/uploadedfiles/declasspart4.pdf), released with redactions in 2016, recorded reports that Bayoumi might have been connected to Saudi intelligence and drew attention to his contacts with Saudi government offices. The inquiry expressly said that it had not independently verified some of the information it was passing on. The 2004 Commission report, for its part, said it had not found evidence that the Saudi government as an institution or senior Saudi officials individually funded al-Qaeda. That finding concerned funding of the organization. It did not amount to a finding about every act of assistance that people with Saudi government connections may have provided to two men in California. Nor does the evidence of help with an apartment establish knowledge of the plot, much less that the Saudi government ordered it. Those are separate steps that require separate evidence.

## What fell, and when

The plane impacts damaged columns in the two towers, and in the National Institute of Standards and Technology’s reconstruction they also stripped fire protection from parts of the steel framing. As heating continued, steel lost strength and stiffness without having to melt. Floors sagged and pulled exterior columns inward; photographs and video showed inward bowing before each tower began to fall from the damaged area. NIST’s detailed work addressed the onset of collapse, not the full descent of every floor. That distinction also matters for the figures often quoted as nine and eleven seconds: NIST was estimating when the first exterior panels reached the ground, not timing the collapse of the entire building. Debris falling outside the structure is not a clock for what is happening inside it.

Building 7 is a different case. It was hit by debris when the North Tower came down, and fires burned on several floors for hours. In NIST’s reconstruction, heated framing expanded, failures in the floors left a key interior column without enough lateral support, and the column buckled. Other failures spread inside before the exterior came down. One thing the video establishes independently of the simulation is that the east penthouse dropped before the main roofline. NIST’s final analysis also recognized that a portion of the visible roofline descended at gravitational acceleration for about 2.25 seconds. By the time the roofline reached free fall, the fallen penthouse had already signaled a failure inside the building. The brief interval of acceleration tells us that the exterior had lost its support by then, not how the failure began.

A University of Alaska Fairbanks study funded by Architects & Engineers for 9/11 Truth challenged NIST’s fire model and found it could reproduce the observed descent by imposing nearly simultaneous failure of the columns. That is a substantive challenge to a proposed sequence inside a building where no camera could see the supports. But matching the visible motion after specifying that supports fail does not establish what made them fail. A claim of deliberate demolition needs independent evidence of intervention; the study’s match to the video does not provide it.

## Who made the trades

Unusually heavy trading in airline options before the attacks looked like a clue that might identify somebody who knew what was coming. A put option can gain value when a stock falls, and there was a rush of trading in puts on the two airlines whose planes would be hijacked. In a footnote, the Commission reported that one institutional investor bought most of the September 6 United Airlines puts while also buying American Airlines shares on September 10, and that much of the September 10 trading in American Airlines puts followed a recommendation in an options newsletter. The footnote does not make the full trading file available for public audit. It does show why timing alone is not enough: to turn a profitable position into evidence of foreknowledge, an investigator needs to trace the order to a person and establish what information that person had.

## The authority that followed

Congress’s September 2001 authorization empowered the president to use force against those he determined had planned, authorized, committed, or aided the attacks, or harbored those responsible. It became the legal basis for the war in Afghanistan and contained no expiration date. The invasion of Iraq rested on a separate authorization in 2002. That resolution invoked September 11 as a reason to take threats more seriously, cited allegations about Iraq’s weapons, and referred to al-Qaeda members in Iraq. The Commission found no evidence of a collaborative operational relationship between Saddam Hussein’s government and al-Qaeda. Read alongside the resolution, that finding shows how the case for a new war could borrow the force of September 11 without establishing Iraqi responsibility for it. The history of those weapons claims belongs to [[iraq-wmds]].

The Patriot Act brought a different change home. Section 215 let the FBI seek secret court orders for “any tangible things” for qualifying investigations. Under an interpretation the public could not see, the government obtained bulk records of telephone calls, including which numbers connected and when, rather than the conversations themselves. In 2015, after the program became public, the Second Circuit held that Section 215 did not authorize collection on that scale. The ruling concerned what Congress had allowed, not whether September 11 had really happened or whether the risk of another attack was real.

The evidence that al-Qaeda carried out the attacks does not vindicate the Iraq war, make the late watchlist entry less serious, or supply statutory authority for a surveillance program. For that last question the court had to read the law Congress enacted, not weigh the terrible day that made its passage possible.
