In May 2004, the *New York Times* public editor Daniel Okrent examined how his newspaper had covered Iraq’s alleged weapons programs. One of his examples involved a headline announcing the discovery of radioactive material. The article beneath it said the material was very unlikely to be connected to weapons. The newsroom had helped make a story sound more alarming than its own reporting warranted.

Okrent placed responsibility beyond an individual reporter. Editors assigned work, approved it, chose headlines and decided where and when stories appeared. A newspaper’s account of the world emerged from those decisions together. His [column](https://cryptome.wikileaks.org/nyt-wmd.htm) follows the decisions through which a newspaper could give an unreliable account greater prominence than a skeptical one.

That institutional question is the subject of Edward S. Herman and Noam Chomsky’s *Manufacturing Consent*, published in 1988. Their **propaganda model** asks why a press with many competing organizations can repeatedly privilege the outlook of powerful institutions. Its answer concerns the resources, dependencies and professional routines through which news gets made.

Herman and Chomsky argue that those arrangements systematically favor dominant interests and restrict the range of respectable debate. A list of wealthy proprietors would establish only part of their case. The pressure has to reach the work.

## Five pressures on the same story

Herman and Chomsky identify five filters: ownership, size and profit; advertising; reliance on powerful sources; costly negative pressure; and anticommunism. The last reflects the Cold War setting of their original account. Their [opening chapter](https://chomsky.info/consent01/) describes these pressures as interacting parts of a system. It also allows for journalists who sincerely believe they are exercising independent judgment.

Consider an imaginary local newspaper covering a large development proposal. The developer promises jobs; residents are concerned about rent, traffic and the use of public land. The paper must report a complicated decision while paying its staff and filling tomorrow’s edition. None of the pressures that follow requires an editor to receive an instruction to lie.

**Ownership** helps determine the resources and purposes of the organization. A proprietor can appoint senior managers, set financial expectations and decide whether a newsroom gets another reporter. In the imagined paper, a prolonged investigation might be discouraged through a budget decision before anyone argues about a particular sentence. A direct veto is one possible intervention; the ordinary allocation of time is another.

The paper could also have commercial reasons to investigate. A well-supported exposé might attract readers and strengthen the paper’s reputation. Ownership identifies a source of power over the newsroom, but it does not determine the outcome of every conflict within it. A budget that protects weeks of investigative work gives a reporter different possibilities from one that rewards a stream of inexpensive stories.

**Advertising** introduces a customer whose interests can differ from the reader’s. Imagine that the development company and local property agents buy substantial space in the paper. The editor might face a threat to withdraw that business, or anticipate one without being threatened. More subtly, the publication may devote attention to the households advertisers hope to reach and give less attention to people with little purchasing power.

An editor could keep an advertiser’s threats away from the reporting staff and still run a publication designed mainly for affluent households. Protecting individual stories and widening the range of people considered worth covering are separate editorial tasks.

**Sourcing** concerns the unequal cost of obtaining information. The developer has a press officer, attractive images and a completed economic-impact report. The local authority can supply a named spokesperson and an official meeting. Tenants may need time off work to speak, and their account of displaced neighbors may take days to verify. A newsroom with one available reporter may begin with the easiest material to obtain and attribute.

Reporting what an official says can be entirely legitimate. The problem develops when the official’s question becomes the newspaper’s question by default. A dispute over a hypothetical forecast of two thousand jobs may leave little space for asking who will receive them, what happens to existing tenants or why this use of the land was selected. All the quoted statements could be accurate while the account remains narrow.

The job estimate itself could require a different kind of work. Does it count temporary construction posts and continuing employment as if they were the same? Does it deduct jobs displaced elsewhere? A reporter who asks for those assumptions is doing more than adding a dissenting quotation to an official claim. They are testing the claim. An apparent balance of supporters and opponents can leave the underlying calculation untouched.

**Flak** is the authors’ term for negative responses that can make coverage costly: complaints, legal threats, political attacks or pressure on advertisers. Its influence may extend beyond the story that provoked it. After an expensive confrontation, a small newsroom may become reluctant to pursue another report about the same company.

Complaints can also reveal a genuine error. A threat is not proof that the challenged article was true, and a correction need not be capitulation. The objection’s merits and the resources behind it both deserve attention. A tenant requesting a factual correction and a company threatening prolonged litigation do not have equivalent leverage.

**Anticommunism**, the original fifth filter, concerns an ideological framework that sorts legitimate political positions from suspect ones. In a Cold War dispute, an accusation of communist sympathy could change how a source or proposal was heard. The filter addresses assumptions about who can be trusted and which alternatives deserve consideration.

In the local example, officials might equate objections to this development with opposition to employment. A reporter could then be asking tenants to defend themselves against a position they never took. That frame would have to be demonstrated in the coverage. In a later retrospective, Herman discussed faith in the market as another ideological force. Updating the argument responsibly means naming the outlook and showing its operation, not replacing anticommunism with whatever belief happens to be disliked. [His retrospective](https://libcom.org/article/propaganda-model-retrospective-edward-s-herman) also emphasizes the model’s limits.

The pressures can reinforce each other. In this example, fewer staff would increase dependence on prepared material; advertiser pressure might make an already expensive investigation harder to defend. To discover which pressures actually mattered, we would need the paper’s financial relationships, reporting process and editorial decisions. The resulting article alone would leave several explanations open.

The example illustrates how the filters might work; Herman’s retrospective also cautions that local conflicts can offer more room for effective opposition. The original framework concerns dominant media and patterns extending beyond a single dispute. A local development story cannot establish its account of the national press.

## A claim about news, and a claim about people

The title *Manufacturing Consent* can suggest that the public reliably believes whatever it is given. Herman explicitly distinguishes the model’s account of media behavior from the effects of that behavior. An audience can reject a message, encounter other sources or distrust the institution delivering it. A pattern of biased coverage would not by itself demonstrate successful persuasion.

An investigation of news performance can compare whose views appear, which questions recur and how much attention an event receives. An investigation of persuasion needs evidence about what audiences encountered and what changed in their understanding or conduct. Counting headlines cannot complete the second investigation.

Even a survey showing that readers of one publication hold a particular view leaves a difficulty: they may have chosen it because they already agreed. Repetition, exposure and persuasion are different parts of a possible explanation. A credible account of influence has to connect them.

An institution can also proceed without persuading most people. It might need support from a legislature, the acquiescence of a regulator or enough uncertainty to delay opposition. Those are different possible outcomes, with different evidence needed for each. Public disbelief would matter greatly to a claim about persuasion, while leaving open a question about whose concerns reached the people making a decision.

The model’s phrase *worthy and unworthy victims* concerns the selection of attention. The terms criticize unequal treatment, not the victims’ actual moral worth. Herman uses politically selective attention to suffering as an example of the pattern he expects. The empirical task is to compare coverage without assuming at the outset that every disparity has the same cause.

Distance, available images, access for reporters, the timing of an event and the identities of the victims can all affect attention. These factors can themselves be shaped by power. That possibility makes them part of the investigation; it does not excuse leaving them out. A persuasive comparison explains why the cases are comparable and tests whether their political relationship predicts treatment beyond those other differences.

## Following a change within one newspaper

W. Lance Bennett’s 1990 study of the press and government gives a more specific hypothesis to examine. He proposed that leading news organizations tend to **index** the range of credible voices to the range of views in mainstream government debate. When influential officials disagree, opposition acquires a place in the news. If that disagreement contracts, public opposition may lose coverage even when it remains substantial.

The dispute concerned U.S. support for the contras, forces fighting Nicaragua’s Sandinista government. Congress and the Reagan administration disagreed over that support. The changing strength of congressional opposition gave Bennett a way to compare the range of political debate with the voices appearing in the news.

Bennett studied *New York Times* coverage of Nicaragua policy from January 1983 to mid-October 1986. His method located 2,148 relevant news articles and opinion pieces through the paper’s index, supplemented by database searches. Two coders used index abstracts to identify expressed opinions, their sources and whether they supported or opposed contra policy. He reported a contraction in opposition coverage after congressional opposition to contra aid weakened. Several regular columnists continued to oppose the policy. [The study](https://web.stanford.edu/class/comm1a/readings/bennett-press-state.pdf) therefore describes a changing distribution of voices, rather than their complete disappearance.

Bennett’s comparison follows the same newspaper through a change in official debate. Its movement toward narrower coverage can therefore be examined without assuming a change of owner or an advertiser’s intervention. The proposed route is through sourcing: as an official position loses influence, other opponents find it harder to remain part of the story. That would support indexing, while leaving the contribution of advertising or a proprietor’s preferences to separate investigation.

A study of one newspaper and one policy dispute has limits. Index abstracts are a selected representation of articles, and identifying an opinion does not capture everything a report communicates through narrative or emphasis. The comparison can support a particular explanation while leaving its reach across outlets and subjects to further study. Its value lies partly in making the proposed relationship specific enough to examine elsewhere.

## What the Iraq failure establishes

Okrent’s account of the *Times* describes incentives within the newsroom: competition for scoops, the attraction of front-page placement, dependence on anonymous sources and inadequate follow-up. He reported that skeptical work sometimes received less prominent treatment. The problem included scrutiny that existed inside the newspaper but did not receive equivalent editorial weight. His column was his own assessment as public editor, not a finding that every newsroom behaved identically.

These observations fit important parts of Herman and Chomsky’s framework, particularly its attention to sourcing and institutional selection. They do not independently establish that an advertiser or proprietor ordered the Iraq coverage. To conclude that every filter caused the same outcome would outrun the account.

If editors already had access to a substantial objection, the remedy cannot be limited to finding more skeptical reporters. Someone must give the objection time, examine its evidence and decide how prominently to present it. A correction that receives far less attention than the original claim may leave much of the original impression intact.

Editorial responsibility survives a structural explanation. A deadline can explain why a prepared official account was attractive. It does not compel an editor to turn uncertainty into a stronger headline. The institution sets conditions in which people work, and people still make decisions that can be challenged.

## The problem of an explanation that always wins

The strongest objection to the propaganda model concerns how its predictions are defined. If supportive coverage confirms the filters, while adversarial coverage is always described as a useful appearance of freedom, no possible outcome can count against the account. The explanation becomes a way of assigning meaning after the event.

Herman rejects the claim that the framework explains everything. He acknowledges disagreement among powerful interests, alternative media and opportunities for resistance. Those qualifications make the argument more plausible. They also place a responsibility on anyone using it to say in advance where it should apply.

For the imaginary development story, a test could begin before publication: identify the developer’s financial relationship with the paper and record who has authority over reporting time. Then compare the attention given to evidence threatening the project with the attention given to favorable claims. A finding that the paper consistently funded and prominently published well-supported challenges would count against the prediction of advertiser restraint. The test would still have to examine whether accuracy, audience interest or access to evidence explains any difference in treatment.

A single strong investigation cannot settle the independence of an entire media system, but its success still needs to be explained. Repeated findings that the expected boundaries fail to hold would require a revision of the model’s scope. Saying that every successful challenge ultimately serves the system would avoid that responsibility.

The category *elite interests* also needs care. A government, an advertiser, a proprietor and a large audience can want different things. The researcher has to identify the interests involved and show when they align, conflict or have little stake in the outcome. Otherwise *power* becomes the name given to whichever interest prevailed, and the model cannot explain why it prevailed over another.

## Beyond the newsroom

Digital distribution adds another institution between a report and its audience. An editor may publish a story that a ranking system rarely displays. A recommendation may give prominence to material that no news editor commissioned. The commercial decisions and ranking rules at this stage may differ from the decisions that produced the report.

In its June 2023 explanation, Meta described combining predictions about a person’s likely responses with other information, including survey feedback, to rank content. It gave likely sharing as one example and discussed recommendations from accounts a person did not follow. [That statement](https://about.fb.com/news/2023/06/how-ai-ranks-content-on-facebook-and-instagram/) explains the company’s account of its methods at that time; it is not an independent audit of their political effects.

A ranking system that predicts attention is not directly measuring truth or civic importance. A user may share a report because it is useful, misleading, infuriating or funny. The relationship between the prediction and the publication’s eventual reach depends on the particular system and its objectives. Treating all those routes as automatic production of agreement loses the behavior that needs explaining.

Synthetic accounts raise further questions about who supplied the material and whether engagement was genuine. The [[dead-internet]] claim cannot be established by the existence of ranking systems alone. Production, recommendation and reception require their own evidence, even when the same commercial platform connects them.

Herman and Chomsky’s framework remains useful because it directs attention upstream of an individual article: who paid for the reporting, who could supply a ready account, and which objections became expensive to pursue. Those questions are worth asking of a publication the reader trusts as well as one they dislike.

For a reader facing an important claim, the first useful move is often to follow it back to the evidence. Five outlets repeating one official’s statement do not provide five independent confirmations. A publication that shows how it checked that statement has given the reader something more valuable than another expression of confidence. That work takes time and money. A press that promises independence has to protect the conditions in which it can be done.

Changing the source of funding may help, but no arrangement certifies the result. Subscriptions can create pressure to flatter paying readers; a donor can impose priorities; public funding can become politically vulnerable. The aim is to give reporting enough independence that it can disappoint its patrons and still survive. The model’s questions become most useful when they help identify which protection is missing.
