In September 2009, the US Justice Department announced a $2.3 billion resolution involving Pfizer and its subsidiary Pharmacia & Upjohn. Part concerned the promotion of Bextra, an anti-inflammatory drug, for uses and doses the FDA had declined to approve because of safety concerns.

The [announcement](https://www.justice.gov/archives/opa/pr/justice-department-announces-largest-health-care-fraud-settlement-its-history) described a criminal and a civil resolution. The subsidiary agreed to plead guilty to felony misbranding with intent to defraud or mislead. A separate $1 billion payment would resolve civil allegations involving several products and kickbacks.

A person who remembers such a case has a reasonable question when the same corporate name appears on another medicine: why should the next assurance be believed? A patient deserves more than an instruction to trust the company again. The earlier crime also leaves the next claim about harm to be investigated on its own evidence.

A manufacturer wants to sell a product; the patient wants to know whether it will help and what it may cost them in harm. The difficulty is how to make that information dependable when the company benefiting from a favorable result may also fund the work that produces it. Vaccine controversies bring this problem into sharp relief because the decisions concern healthy people, often children, receiving an intervention to prevent a future illness.

## How an evidence base becomes misleading

A set of accurate papers can still mislead if it omits the studies that went badly.

In a [2008 study](https://www.nejm.org/doi/10.1056/NEJMsa065779), Erick Turner and colleagues compared FDA reviews of trials for twelve antidepressants with the corresponding journal literature. They examined 74 studies, including work absent from the journals. The regulator's files supplied a denominator the published literature could not.

Of 38 studies the FDA considered positive, 37 were published. Of 36 judged negative or questionable, 22 were not published, eleven appeared in a way the researchers considered positive, and three were published as not positive. The journal literature therefore appeared far more favorable than the full set of reviews. The authors could not determine how responsibility divided between authors or sponsors failing to submit work and journals declining it.

Imagine a doctor searching carefully, reading every relevant article and finding repeated favorable results. Diligence does not recover a study absent from the literature. The doctor can make an informed decision from the available papers and still have an incomplete picture. Peer review of individual manuscripts cannot tell that reader how many other manuscripts never arrived.

Turner's analysis still found the drugs superior to placebo when results were combined. Publication made the benefit look larger. An unsuccessful trial can fail to distinguish an effect without proving none exists; the combined evidence and the magnitude of benefit matter.

The investigators could make the comparison because the regulator held records beyond the journals. That access helped expose a distorted public account without settling every question about the regulator's own decisions. It shows why preserving records in more than one place, and allowing people to compare them, matters.

Someone questioning a vaccine should be able to ask for the same comparison. Identify the studies, what each was designed to measure, the results that remain unavailable, and whether the public account matches the underlying record. A long list of unrelated corporate offenses cannot supply that missing comparison.

## A small series and a population question

In the MMR-autism controversy, the evidence had to answer whether vaccination changed the rate of autism, beyond showing that a diagnosis sometimes followed an injection.

Andrew Wakefield and colleagues' 1998 paper described twelve children. It was a case series: an account of selected patients, without a comparison group able to establish whether similar children who had not received MMR developed the same problems at a different rate. Even an accurate series could suggest a question for investigation without answering the causal question.

The account of those twelve children also came under scrutiny. In his [2011 investigation](https://www.bmj.com/content/342/bmj.c5347), Brian Deer reported discrepancies between the paper and the children's medical records, including accounts of when developmental problems began. He also examined how the children had been selected. The paper was retracted in 2010. The proposed link depended on the timing of those changes, so discrepancies in the recorded sequence affected the evidence itself.

The larger question could be investigated through population records. A [2019 Danish study](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30831578/) followed 657,461 children, linking vaccination and autism diagnoses through national registries. The researchers compared vaccinated and unvaccinated children while accounting for measured differences including age, sex and family history. Their adjusted estimate showed no increased autism risk after MMR. They also examined proposed susceptible groups and periods after vaccination, without finding a consistent increase.

This was an observational study, using existing records rather than assigning vaccination at random. Its authors noted that they had not reviewed individual medical charts. Those limits affect how the result should be read. A selected case series still lacks the comparison needed to measure the population relationship. The study's stated funding came from the Novo Nordisk Foundation and the Danish Ministry of Health; a funding disclosure invites scrutiny of methods and access rather than substituting for that scrutiny.

A [2021 Cochrane review](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34806766/) likewise found no association between MMR and autism in the two observational studies contributing to that outcome, involving about 1.19 million children. That is the relevant sample, rather than the more than 23 million participants across all the review's different questions. The review also assessed prevention of disease and other possible harms, with different levels of certainty for different results.

A parent's account of a change after vaccination deserves to be heard accurately. The account can establish what the family noticed and when. Determining whether vaccination caused the change requires a comparison that separates it from changes occurring at the same ages without vaccination. This is why the design of the study matters more than the force with which either side tells the story. The [[vaccine-autism]] essay follows the controversy in more detail.

## A report is a reason to investigate

Safety monitoring begins with events that may turn out to be caused by a vaccine, coincidental, or not yet explainable. Demanding proof of causation before accepting a report would defeat that purpose.

The US Vaccine Adverse Event Reporting System, VAERS, accepts reports after vaccination without requiring the reporter to establish the cause. Its [official guidance](https://vaers.hhs.gov/faq.html) describes an early-warning system. Reports can identify patterns worth examining. They can also contain errors or missing information, and reporting can rise when an issue receives public attention. The program attempts to obtain medical records for serious reports; it is incorrect to say that the entries are never followed up.

Counting reports alone cannot establish how many injuries vaccination caused or the rate at which a particular injury occurs. A rate needs an appropriate denominator, a defined period and a consistently identified outcome. An investigation also needs to consider how often that outcome would have occurred without the intervention. Giving a count the name vaccine deaths does not perform those steps.

That background comparison needs to fit the people being observed. Comparing a group of older adults with a much younger population could mistake an age difference for an effect of vaccination. The definition of a case matters too: a reported symptom, a diagnosis in billing records and a diagnosis confirmed by reviewing a chart are different observations. Each can contribute evidence, but combining them without explanation can make a precise-looking total hard to interpret.

An institution can also use the limits of reporting to answer less than it should. Saying that a report does not establish causation leaves open whether it points to a real problem. A useful answer explains what follow-up was done and what evidence changed the assessment. Otherwise a correct description of the database becomes a way to avoid the concern it was built to receive.

The [April 2021 Janssen vaccine pause](https://www.fda.gov/news-events/press-announcements/fda-and-cdc-lift-recommended-pause-johnson-johnson-janssen-covid-19-vaccine-use-following-thorough) provides a specific example. In April, FDA and CDC recommended a pause after six reports of an unusual combination of severe clotting and low platelet counts, following more than 6.8 million administered doses. By April 23, the agencies described fifteen reported cases, including the original six. They reviewed the available evidence, added risk information and recommended resuming use at that time.

Investigators examined a distinctive pattern and changed the information supplied with the vaccine. It concerned a particular vaccine and syndrome. It cannot be transferred unchanged to every other product or read as a current recommendation about a vaccine's use.

A rare injury still happens to someone. For an assessment across a population, frequency still matters, along with severity, the benefit expected and the alternatives available. A useful account must be able to hold both facts without treating a patient as a rounding error or a suspected event as a confirmed cause.

## What the heart-inflammation warning says

Myocarditis and pericarditis, inflammation affecting the heart muscle or its surrounding lining, are recognized risks following mRNA COVID-19 vaccination. Public information needs to describe them accurately, including what remains uncertain about recovery.

In [June 2025](https://www.fda.gov/vaccines-blood-biologics/safety-availability-biologics/fda-approves-required-updated-warning-labeling-mrna-covid-19-vaccines-regarding-myocarditis-and), the FDA required updated warnings. Its account gave an estimated unadjusted incidence of myocarditis and/or pericarditis of about 27 cases per million doses among males aged twelve through twenty-four, during days one through seven after a dose of the 2023–24 formula. The estimate came from insurance claims. The age group, formulation, observation window and the word unadjusted are part of the result; removing them produces a different claim.

The update also addressed a separate follow-up study of people with myocarditis after the original formulation. Abnormal cardiac MRI findings remained common at a median follow-up of about five months. The FDA said their clinical and prognostic significance was unknown. Improvement in symptoms and uncertainty about later imaging findings are not interchangeable descriptions of recovery.

The follow-up findings make a blanket assurance of mild, quickly resolved illness inadequate. The incidence estimate also needs care: unadjusted cases recorded after vaccination are not the same measure as excess cases attributable to it.

A comparison with infection must examine the same outcome in comparable people and periods. Age, prior immunity and the formulation being offered can change what evidence is relevant. An overall average cannot settle every case, and the vaccine-risk estimate alone leaves out the benefit side of the decision.

The possibility of harm also does not erase evidence of prevention. In the Cochrane review, seven cohort studies involving about 12,000 children estimated one-dose effectiveness against measles at 95%, with moderate certainty. That percentage describes a reduction relative to the comparison group, not a claim that 95% of all children would otherwise have contracted measles. The number of cases prevented also depends on how likely exposure and illness would have been without vaccination. The same relative reduction can prevent different numbers of cases in different circumstances. This is evidence about one disease and intervention; it cannot establish the benefit of an unrelated product.

## Records another researcher can check

Industry influence deserves examination at the level where it can operate: funding, study design, access to data, publication, promotion and decisions about enforcement. Each gives a more useful point of inquiry than declaring an entire agency bought.

The [Prescription Drug User Fee Act](https://www.fda.gov/industry/fda-user-fee-programs/prescription-drug-user-fee-amendments) authorizes the FDA to collect fees for specified prescription-drug applications and programs. Created in 1992, it supplies resources for review and creates a financial relationship with regulated firms. The relationship raises questions about dependence and priorities. It does not establish that an application fee purchases approval, or describe every part of the agency's funding.

Conflict-of-interest disclosures should make relationships visible, but visibility alone changes little if nobody can examine the work. A study needs a question specified clearly enough that later reporting can be checked against it. Researchers need access sufficient to challenge the analysis, with protections for participants' private information. A journal's reputation cannot replace those conditions.

Trial reporting is already subject to legal requirements. [ClinicalTrials.gov's account of FDAAA 801 and its implementing rule](https://clinicaltrials.gov/policy/fdaaa-801-final-rule) explains registration and results obligations for applicable trials, generally with results due one year after primary completion, subject to the rule's conditions and permitted delays. The existence of a deadline is different from demonstrating compliance. An assessment should check which trials were covered, what was due and what actually appeared.

The Bextra resolution also included a corporate integrity agreement and arose from whistleblower litigation. Those facts show routes by which misconduct reached an enforcement process. To judge whether the response worked, one would need to examine subsequent conduct and compliance. The size of a payment alone cannot establish either deterrence or the claim that every offense remained profitable after penalties.

Such scrutiny should apply to anyone selling an alternative account as well. A critic's independence from one manufacturer does not make a clinical claim correct. Patients deserve to know the evidence and interests behind an alternative treatment, paid subscription or expert campaign, just as they do behind a branded medicine. Claiming to protect the public brings the same obligation to explain the evidence.

Trust cannot sensibly mean forgetting the earlier misconduct. It can mean having enough access and accountability to discover the next problem, correct it and compensate people harmed. The patient should not have to become a trial auditor to deserve accurate information. The institutions asking for confidence owe an account that other people can check, and a response when that checking finds something wrong.
