On November 27, 1941, Washington sent Admiral Husband Kimmel a message explicitly designated a war warning. It said that an aggressive Japanese move was expected within days and directed a defensive deployment. The Army separately warned General Walter Short that hostile action was possible at any moment and told him to undertake reconnaissance and other necessary measures.

Short’s reply reported an alert against sabotage. No one in Washington obtained a clarification sufficient to uncover the gap between the warning and the preparations. When Japanese aircraft attacked Oahu on December 7, planes concentrated for protection against saboteurs were vulnerable to destruction from the air.

The Americans were expecting war. There were warnings they acted on inadequately, intelligence they failed to pass along, and approaching forces they failed to identify. The further allegation is that Roosevelt and senior officials knew a strike against Hawaii was coming and deliberately allowed the damage. The dispute turns on what information those officials actually possessed before the bombs fell and what they did with it.

## What kind of knowledge?

Japan attacked Pearl Harbor with aircraft from six carriers. More than 2,400 Americans died. The next day Congress declared war on Japan; Germany declared war on the United States on December 11. The attack transformed the political situation Roosevelt had confronted while assisting countries already fighting the Axis.

The attack united support for war with Japan. Critics of Roosevelt have argued that this was the outcome he needed and that he deliberately permitted it. The argument joins a known political result to a claim about his intentions before the attack.

Knowing that relations were approaching war was common in the relevant military and diplomatic circles by late November. Knowing that Pearl Harbor was vulnerable to an air attack was also within the commanders’ understanding. Knowing that a Japanese carrier force would launch against Hawaii on a particular morning would have been much more specific information.

These forms of knowledge imposed different obligations. A warning of imminent war could justify stronger readiness even without a target and an hour. The absence of a decoded attack order cannot excuse neglect of the warnings that were available. Equally, an ignored warning of possible attack does not establish that the person ignoring it secretly knew the operational plan.

## McCollum’s proposal

On October 7, 1940, naval intelligence officer Arthur McCollum wrote a memorandum assessing the strategic situation and recommending action against Japan. Its argument began with the relationship between the two oceans. Britain’s survival and naval power helped protect the United States in the Atlantic; Japanese expansion could make Britain’s position worse and draw American attention away from Europe.

McCollum favored an assertive response. His [eight proposed measures](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/McCollum_memorandum) included arrangements for British and Dutch bases, assistance to China, naval deployments, keeping the main Pacific fleet near Hawaii, and economic pressure extending to a coordinated trade embargo. He believed American naval power could constrain Japan and improve the terms on which a conflict would be fought.

He also recognized an obstacle at home: public opinion did not permit an easy declaration of war. His concluding paragraph treated a Japanese overt act of war, brought about through the proposed measures, as an advantageous outcome. McCollum was prepared to accept a confrontation and saw an advantage in Japan starting it.

The same paragraph called for readiness to meet the threat. The memorandum did not propose leaving Pearl Harbor undefended while waiting for a known carrier attack. Its measures included strengthening America’s position, and it allowed the possibility that vigorous action would modify Japanese policy.

Captain Dudley Knox’s appended comment gives the proposal another dimension. He agreed with McCollum’s courses of action but warned against precipitating trouble in Asia that would hamper assistance to Britain. An American confrontation in one ocean could consume ships and resources needed in the other. Agreement with firm measures against Japan did not eliminate that strategic constraint.

The fleet’s placement had already provoked an argument of its own. Admiral James Richardson objected to keeping it in Hawaii, citing inadequate facilities, crowded anchorages, and difficulties with training and morale. In his testimony, he described understanding the deployment as a deterrent. His objections exposed the costs and readiness problems of that policy; they did not depend on knowing a Japanese attack date.

The fleet was already operating from Hawaii in May 1940, months before McCollum wrote. His recommendation was to keep it there. A policy can agree with a memorandum because its author is endorsing an existing decision, rather than because the president later implements the author’s instructions.

Researcher Robert Stinnett made the memorandum central to *Day of Deceit*. In a [2002 interview](https://www.independent.org/article/2002/03/11/do-freedom-of-information-act-files-prove-fdr-had-foreknowledge-of-pearl-harbor/), he argued that Roosevelt adopted all eight proposals. Asked whether there was proof Roosevelt saw the paper, Stinnett acknowledged the absence of direct proof that he actually saw it, while claiming that subsequent actions and other files established the connection.

The paper survives as McCollum’s recommendation. It carries no presidential instruction to suppress a warning or leave the fleet exposed to a known attack.

## The first shot

Secretary of War Henry Stimson’s diary supplies a closer view of senior policy discussions. His November 25 entry described a White House meeting about the danger of Japanese attack without warning. He recorded a discussion of how to put Japan in the position of firing first while limiting American danger.

American leaders cared about who would appear to initiate a war and about their ability to respond with public support. The diary preserves that political calculation.

But the entry did not identify Hawaii as the location of an imminent strike. Its discussion concerned the broader crisis with Japan, including movements toward Southeast Asia. Stimson’s account is a record of what he understood and subsequently dictated, not a transcript in which Roosevelt lays out a carrier attack to be permitted.

The [congressional investigation](https://www.ibiblio.org/pha/pha/congress/part_4.html) reproduced the diary passage and heard General George Marshall’s explanation: he understood the discussion as one about diplomatic procedure, not an order for military inaction. Marshall had reasons to defend the administration and himself. His explanation still has to be considered alongside what the resulting instructions actually said.

The Army warning to Short expressed the desire that Japan commit the first overt act if hostilities could not be avoided. It then explicitly said this policy should not restrict actions needed for defense. Short was directed to conduct reconnaissance and other measures before Japanese hostile action. The message also cautioned against alarming civilians or revealing intentions, language that contributed to disagreement about its meaning.

The message directed Short both to avoid initiating hostilities and to prepare his defense. Its instruction about the first act did not cancel the instruction to conduct reconnaissance.

The broader policy involved real risks. Economic pressure and support for Japan’s opponents could increase the risk of war even when their purpose was deterrence or restraint. Japanese leaders also had choices about their continuing war in China and expansion into Southeast Asia. The attack grew out of this conflict over expansion, resources, and the use of force; its precise plan was a further military decision.

## What MAGIC could read

American codebreakers had achieved an extraordinary success against Japanese diplomatic communications. The system associated with the name PURPLE allowed officials to read important exchanges between Tokyo and its diplomats. Other intercepted consular traffic supplied reports from places including Honolulu.

Diplomatic access was not access to every Japanese military order. Naval operational communications used different systems. Progress against a code might mean recovering some code groups, identifying units through patterns of traffic, or reading actual message contents. Those achievements could supply very different information to a commander.

There were further steps between reception and use. Someone had to intercept the transmission, recover its meaning, translate it, assess it, and distribute the result. Each step had its own date. A message transmitted on December 1 might appear in an archive with a clear English account of Japanese preparations while having remained unread until years later.

Frederick Parker’s [*Pearl Harbor Revisited*](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/GOVPUB-D-PURL-gpo52783/pdf/GOVPUB-D-PURL-gpo52783.pdf), published by the NSA’s Center for Cryptologic History, reproduces a revealing set of naval messages. Its appendix explicitly states that the selected messages were intercepted in 1941 but decoded and translated in September 1945–May 1946. Some concern carrier training, specially prepared torpedoes, tankers, and arrangements for a distant operation.

Seen together in translation, these messages make the preparations much easier to reconstruct. The English texts reveal information that was absent from the wartime summaries. Their dates of translation place them in the postwar reconstruction.

Parker also identifies a troubling feature of the later investigation: the translations made during the congressional inquiry apparently were not supplied to its naval liaison office. That omission limited the subsequent accounting, even though the messages had been unreadable before the attack.

Stinnett disputed the conventional account of unread naval codes. He argued that the Americans could read operational messages and that the Japanese force broke radio silence while approaching Hawaii. His case would be much stronger if the surviving record showed a timely translation or an authenticated location report identifying the approaching carriers and its delivery to the officials accused of concealing it. Parker’s reconstruction instead distinguishes the later translations from the traffic analysis available during the crisis.

Direction finding offered another way of learning where Japanese forces were. Stations comparing the bearings of a signal can estimate a transmitter’s position, but attributing it to a particular ship requires identifying that transmitter. Reception of messages addressed *to* carriers does not establish that the carriers themselves were transmitting their positions. The identity of the transmitter is crucial to placing a particular ship at the intersection of those bearings.

## The request for Pearl Harbor’s berths

One intercepted message was both specific to Pearl Harbor and translated well before the attack. On September 24, Tokyo instructed its Honolulu consulate to divide the harbor into five areas and report the positions of warships. It sought details including whether multiple ships were tied alongside the same wharf. The translation was completed on October 9.

The document became known as the bomb-plot message. That name reflects its later interpretation; the actual text requested a detailed berthing report. Such information would be useful in attacking ships in port. It was more pointed than simply reporting that American battleships had entered or left a harbor.

Kimmel argued that he should have received it. His criticism was forceful because his command had to assess the danger to precisely the ships and facilities being surveyed. Later messages increased the urgency. A November 15 instruction, translated on December 3, called for reports twice a week and extra secrecy in view of critical relations. A further request, translated the following day, sought investigation of fleet bases around the Hawaiian military reservation.

Washington’s analysts considered the traffic within a much larger Japanese espionage effort. Japanese agents gathered information from other ports and strategic locations too. Some officials treated the berthing scheme as a way to organize and compress reports, rather than as evidence of a particular attack plan.

The [1946 majority report](https://www.ibiblio.org/pha/pha/congress/part_4.html) did not accept that the messages directly disclosed the coming attack. It nevertheless concluded that they deserved serious attention and should have been supplied to the Hawaiian commanders. Officials had material relevant to the defense of the base and failed to let the people responsible for that defense judge its significance.

Other Honolulu reports look even more ominous in retrospect. Messages about the absence of torpedo nets and air reconnaissance could have strengthened an alert. The congressional report and historian R. J. C. Butow identify translations completed on December 8, after the attack. By the time those English texts reached readers, the damage had already been done.

The berthing messages that were read in time remain a serious missed opportunity. Their usefulness did not depend on their supplying a complete attack plan. They could have prompted a fresh assessment of ships’ exposure in the harbor, reconnaissance, and coordination with the Army. The messages translated too late show another weakness: interception was generating more potentially valuable material than the system could promptly turn into useful intelligence.

## A diplomatic deadline on Sunday morning

The Japanese reply ending the current negotiations was transmitted in fourteen parts. American officials had the first thirteen on December 6 and the final part on the morning of December 7. The text announced the failure of negotiations. It did not identify Pearl Harbor as a target.

A separate instruction told the Japanese embassy to deliver the reply at 1 p.m. Washington time. The final part and the delivery instruction were separate messages. The appointed hour gave the diplomatic break a more immediate military significance. Something might follow the delivery, even though the message did not say where.

Marshall ordered an additional warning to Pacific commands after seeing the material. The attempt to get it to Hawaii failed to deliver it to the commander in time. The route chosen has been described as proof that Marshall deliberately avoided a reliable military channel. The actual [communications testimony](https://www.ibiblio.org/pha/pha/congress/part_4.html) is more specific.

Colonel Edward French, in charge of the relevant signal arrangements, said the Army circuit to Honolulu was experiencing interference. He selected Western Union and RCA as the means he believed fastest under the circumstances. He said he did not tell Marshall which route he had chosen. Marshall testified that he had not known the Army circuit was unable to establish contact with Hawaii.

The committee majority accepted the explanation as a communications failure. Its conclusion was disputed within the larger controversy about responsibility. The testimony describes assumptions about speed, security, and the functioning of a relay that proved disastrously inadequate.

The morning’s intercepted material deserved faster and more effective handling. The committee faulted Washington for insufficient alertness and for failing to appreciate the urgency of the timed delivery instruction. The urgency came from the deadline, even though the target was unspecified.

## The warnings already in Hawaii

By the last morning, the problem had also moved beyond Washington’s distribution of secret messages. The destroyer *Ward* attacked a submarine near the harbor entrance at 6:45 a.m. Reports went up the local chain of command. According to the congressional record, Kimmel received the information between 7:30 and 7:40 and was waiting for verification when the air raid began.

There had been doubtful submarine reports before. Officers initially considered the possibility of another mistake and believed the destroyers could handle the contact if genuine. But this report described an actual engagement in a defensive sea area. Kimmel’s own prior guidance recognized that submarine activity could accompany a larger force including a carrier. The majority report criticized the failure to attach sufficient significance to the warning.

At Opana, Army radar operators Joseph Lockard and George Elliott detected the approaching aircraft at 7:02. Their report reached the information center at Fort Shafter. Lieutenant Kermit Tyler believed the contact represented American B-17 bombers expected from the mainland and told the operators not to worry. The [National Park Service’s account](https://www.nps.gov/perl/learn/historyculture/opana-mobile-radar-site.htm) describes him as newly assigned and still training in the role.

The radar functioned. The warning failed at the point where the contact had to be interpreted and connected to a defensive response. The submarine report and the radar detection passed through different local systems, and their significance was not combined in time.

Earlier decisions had already left the defense vulnerable. Short’s sabotage alert concentrated aircraft rather than preparing them to meet a raid. Washington saw his reply without resolving what he had done. The Navy’s warning emphasized probable operations toward Southeast Asia. Local commanders continued to focus on those expected moves while underestimating the possibility that a striking force could reach Hawaii.

The destroyer’s crew and the radar operators supplied warnings through observation. No Japanese code had to be broken for their reports to reach the officers responsible for acting on them.

## The carriers and the accounting afterward

The American carriers were absent from Pearl Harbor, which helped preserve the fleet’s ability to fight. Their absence has also been offered as evidence that valuable ships were deliberately saved while expendable battleships were left behind.

The deployments have ordinary operational records. *Enterprise* was returning after delivering aircraft to Wake, and *Lexington* was carrying aircraft toward Midway. *Saratoga* had completed work on the West Coast and was arriving at San Diego on December 7. These were different missions, not a single evacuation order.

The congressional report also records a difference in what their commanders knew. Admiral William Halsey, taking *Enterprise* to Wake, knew of the war warning and discussed the situation with Kimmel. Admiral John Newton, taking *Lexington* toward Midway, was not shown the warning. That failure to share information occurred within the fleet command in Hawaii.

Japan’s own use of six carriers to attack Hawaii also makes it difficult to maintain that only American planners understood the value of naval aviation.

The dispute over the local commanders’ reputations is more substantial. The Roberts Commission’s early findings attached dereliction of duty to Kimmel and Short. Subsequent inquiries differed, examined Washington more closely, and produced criticism reaching the high command. The [1946 congressional majority](https://www.ibiblio.org/pha/pha/congress/part_5.html) called the local failures errors of judgment rather than derelictions, while also faulting intelligence and war-planning divisions in Washington.

The minority senators, Homer Ferguson and Owen Brewster, assigned responsibility more broadly and more severely to Roosevelt, Stimson, Knox, Marshall, Stark, and others. Their [conclusion](https://www.ibiblio.org/pha/pha/congress/minority.html) also retained responsibility for the commanders in Hawaii. It treated the duties in Washington and at the base as interdependent. Their conclusion assigned failures of responsibility to named officials; it did not establish that those officials had agreed to permit the attack.

In 2000, [Congress requested posthumous advancement in rank](https://www.congress.gov/106/plaws/publ398/PLAW-106publ398.pdf) for Kimmel and Short. Section 546 cited intelligence not supplied to them and expressed the judgment that they had performed professionally without dereliction. This was a significant intervention on their behalf. Its findings and request concerned responsibility and recognition; they did not establish presidential knowledge of the carrier force’s intended attack.

The later record widened responsibility beyond the two commanders. It also preserved evidence of their own decisions, including the limited alert, reconnaissance arrangements, and response to the submarine report.

Warnings of imminent war, relevant espionage reports, local contacts, and defensive instructions failed to become an effective defense. Responsibility ran through both Washington and Hawaii. The record does not establish that Roosevelt had advance operational knowledge of the Pearl Harbor strike and chose to let it succeed.

Short’s reply remains an especially plain record of what went wrong. Washington had asked for defensive measures against a possible enemy attack. He reported preparation against sabotage. The difference was visible before December 7, and it was left unresolved.
