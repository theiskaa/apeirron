The investigators had Marilyn Monroe's telephone bills. In its 1982 review of her death, the Los Angeles district attorney's office described toll records covering June 1 through August 18, 1962. They included eight calls to a Washington number. Investigators traced it to the main switchboard of the Department of Justice, where Robert Kennedy was attorney general.

The bills showed calls to the department without identifying who answered after a switchboard transfer. They did not list incoming calls. The last recorded call to that number was on July 30, several days before Monroe died.

In retellings, these records have often become a complete account of her final telephone conversations, seized on the morning of her death and then made to disappear. The bills support contact with the department while leaving the recipient unidentified. The difference changes the story: a call to a government office becomes evidence of a secret relationship only after someone supplies what the bill leaves out.

Monroe was found dead at her Los Angeles home in the early hours of August 5, 1962. She was thirty-six. The coroner concluded that she had died from an overdose of sedative drugs and classified the death as probable suicide. Later allegations have implicated her doctors, the Kennedys, organized crime, and intelligence agencies. The essential difficulty is deciding which questions the surviving evidence can answer, without allowing either her fame or the attraction of a political murder to answer them in advance.

## What was recorded that morning

The account summarized in the 1982 review begins with Eunice Murray, Monroe's housekeeper, becoming concerned during the night. A light was on in Monroe's bedroom and the door was locked. Murray called psychiatrist Ralph Greenson, who came to the house and entered the bedroom through a window. Monroe's physician, Hyman Engelberg, was then summoned. The police received a call at 4:25 a.m.

Sergeant Jack Clemmons was the first officer to arrive. Murray and the two doctors were there. Another officer, R. E. Byron, subsequently handled the police investigation. Thomas Noguchi performed the autopsy later that morning at the Los Angeles County coroner's office.

Already there was a problem with the sequence. Clemmons recalled Murray saying that she had discovered the body around midnight and that the doctors arrived soon afterward. The police investigation placed her call to Greenson at about 3:30 a.m. In 1982, Murray described the discovery less precisely, as occurring in the middle of the night. The doctors' reported times corresponded with the later sequence.

If Clemmons remembered her initial statement correctly, the discrepancy could amount to hours. The review had no recording of that first conversation to resolve the conflict. It treated a mistaken recollection, a confused statement, or Murray misspeaking as possible explanations.

The later sequence itself left roughly thirty-five minutes between Engelberg's reported pronouncement of death and the call to police. That interval deserves an explanation. It is different from an established, several-hour period during which all three people in the house secretly knew she was dead. Turning the disputed earlier time into a confirmed fact makes the cover-up appear to have been demonstrated before the evidence is examined.

Clemmons also remembered Murray using the washing machine. He thought the behavior odd, but had not established what she was washing. Later accounts supplied the implication of sheets or towels being washed to remove evidence. Clemmons's observation left that crucial detail unresolved.

The condition of the body also suggested that death preceded discovery by some time. That does not establish when anyone in the house knew she had died. A person can die behind a closed door and remain undiscovered. The contested recollection about midnight would therefore matter as evidence of what Murray knew, while the condition of the body concerned when death occurred. Combining them produces an apparent confirmation that neither supplies independently.

The first investigation left material that later inquiries had to reconstruct. The 1982 report acknowledged uncertainty even about the route by which the body reached the coroner's office. Even the handling of the body was harder to reconstruct twenty years later than it should have been.

## Cause of death and the question of intent

The [autopsy report](https://www.autopsyfiles.org/reports/Celebs/monroe,%20marilyn_report.pdf) identifies acute barbiturate poisoning due to ingestion of an overdose. Toxicology found pentobarbital, sold as Nembutal, and chloral hydrate, another sedative. Chloral hydrate is not itself a barbiturate. The reviewing physicians considered its presence an additional contributor to the toxicity of the pentobarbital.

Determining the drugs responsible was a different task from determining why Monroe took them. Chemical analysis could not directly distinguish an intentional overdose from an accident. For the question of intent, coroner Theodore Curphey sought help from a psychiatric investigative team headed by Robert Litman, Norman Farberow, and Norman Tabachnick.

Their approach was a psychological autopsy: an attempt to reconstruct a dead person's circumstances and state of mind from information supplied by others. They considered her distress, sleep problems, long use of sedatives, previous episodes reported as suicide attempts, and accounts of her final evening. They judged probable suicide the appropriate finding. Curphey announced that conclusion on August 17, after considering the toxicological and psychiatric reports.

The finding remained qualified. There was no surviving statement from Monroe describing her intention at the moment she took the drugs. The conclusion drew together physical findings and accounts of her behavior. It was an inference about a private act, not something the pathologist could see by examining an organ.

The 1982 review retained that judgment as reasonably supported. It also allowed for accident, or for voluntary ingestion during emotional confusion without a clearly formed purpose. Its conclusion was therefore more qualified than the assertion that authorities had proved every part of a deliberate suicide beyond doubt.

People who remembered her plans for work or personal life offered a competing picture. They had seen reasons for her to continue living and sometimes resisted a finding of suicide. Investigators nevertheless had to infer her intention at a moment none of those plans directly recorded.

## The empty stomach

The most persistent forensic objection concerns what Noguchi did not find. The stomach contained little material, and he noted no visible pill residue. Examination of gastric and duodenal material under polarized light did not show the crystals investigators had looked for. If she had swallowed enough medication to die, critics asked, where had it gone?

The question sounds decisive when the body is imagined as a container in which everything swallowed should remain until an autopsy. Drugs move through the digestive tract and are absorbed while a person remains alive. The condition found afterward depends partly on that elapsed time. A lethal concentration in the body need not be accompanied by recognizable capsules still sitting in the stomach.

The district attorney's office put this issue to Boyd Stephens, San Francisco's chief medical examiner-coroner. In his [October 13, 1982 interview](https://themarilynmonroecollection.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/03/1982-marilyn-monroe-investigation-report-Part1.pdf), the prosecutors asked him directly about capsule remains. He rejected the assertion that an overdose should always leave capsule remains. His experience included fatal overdoses both with and without visible material left in the stomach.

Stephens considered the blood and liver findings consistent with a process of absorption over time. The [December report](https://themarilynmonroecollection.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/03/1982-marilyn-monroe-investigation-report-Part5.pdf) used his analysis, alongside other consultations, to support oral ingestion. The elapsed time offered an explanation for the finding that had looked anomalous.

A related claim concerns yellow coloring from Nembutal capsules. The proposition is often stated as though a dye trail should necessarily have survived. The 1982 report records a more revealing exchange. A representative of the manufacturer initially supposed that coloring might leave a residue, but knew of no study supporting that expectation. Pathologists consulted about their actual cases said such yellow staining was not a reliable feature of Nembutal deaths. An expectation based on the color of a capsule had been mistaken for an established diagnostic test.

The report also addressed the belief that a sedative overdose should leave a violently contorted body or evidence of vomiting. Reviewing physicians rejected that as a general requirement. Clemmons's discomfort with the appearance of the scene was an observation to investigate; it did not give him a medical basis for declaring the body staged.

The consultations shifted attention back to the use of the medication: how Monroe obtained it, how her treatment was being managed, and what could be learned about her intention. The empty stomach had not made oral ingestion impossible.

## What was not tested

Noguchi's autopsy report lists specimens saved for further toxicological study. In his November 1982 interview, he explained that only blood and liver had been submitted for that work; stomach contents and other material had not received every further analysis that could have been undertaken. The autopsy's microscopic inspection and the laboratory's chemical tests were different procedures. Saying that everything collected was tested would overstate the examination.

The same interview records how he would approach the case differently twenty years later: more extensive chemical analysis, examination of drug distribution among organs, and an investigation at the scene. These are specific opportunities to improve the evidence. They are more useful than an assertion that any incomplete examination must have concealed a murder.

The surviving report and blood and liver findings still permitted review. In 1982, Noguchi maintained that they were consistent with an overdose. His assessment of the earlier work was therefore neither that it had answered every possible question nor that its findings had become worthless.

John Miner, a deputy district attorney who said he attended the autopsy, offered an important dissent from the suicide interpretation. The 1982 report records his assessment that Noguchi's examination was professional and that the physical observations agreed with his own. It also records his explanation of his doubts: a later conversation with Greenson, including discussion of Monroe's recorded statements and future plans, had persuaded him that intentional suicide did not fit her psychological condition.

According to that report, Miner specifically said he had not alleged murder. His disagreement was about intent, and he allowed accidental overdose as a possibility. His [2005 account](https://www.latimes.com/news/la-et-marilyn5aug05_miner-story.html) instead alleged homicide by enema.

An account of his position needs those dates. His presence at the examination in 1962 does not mean that every later conclusion he advanced was an observation made during it. Nor can recollections of Monroe's recorded speech replace the recordings themselves for people trying to assess their context.

One anatomical description has been enlisted in the later theory: the report's reference to a congested, purplish colon. Yet its next description says the mucosa, the inner lining, showed no discoloration. A passage about the appearance of an organ cannot be silently rewritten as a finding of chemical injury to its lining. The proposed route and the person alleged to have administered the drugs remain additional claims.

Noguchi's later public comments also require context. After a 1985 news interview suggesting that Monroe might have been murdered, prosecutors interviewed him again. The [October 31 transcript](https://themarilynmonroecollection.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/03/1982-marilyn-monroe-investigation-report-Part3.pdf) records him saying that he had no new independent information and still agreed with the earlier conclusion. The questions raised by a newly published book had drawn him back into the discussion. A headline about doubt and a pathologist's account of what evidence had changed were different things.

There were also unresolved prescription questions. The 1982 investigators found inconsistencies among accounts of who prescribed what and when, and could not trace the exact origin of all the medication. The uncertainty left responsibility for her medication harder to assess. A record of which doctor supplied each prescription, and what that doctor knew of other treatment, would have been more useful than a reconstruction starting from the assumption that someone meant to kill her.

## The diary and the visitors

The red diary is often described as a missing piece of physical evidence containing secrets Monroe had learned from the Kennedys. In fact, establishing that such a document existed is part of the dispute. Its alleged contents cannot be read or compared with the claims made for them.

Robert Slatzer described the diary in his 1974 book. Lionel Grandison, a former coroner's aide, later said he had seen it among material associated with Monroe's body. At first sight these seem like two sources corroborating one object.

The 1982 inquiry found a problem with their independence: Grandison had read Slatzer's book before making his public claims. The coroner's property record did not list the diary. Noguchi and several people close to Monroe said they had not seen it. The investigators concluded that the allegation was unsupported. Grandison's statement was a lead they checked, rather than the discovery of an authenticated object that subsequently vanished from their custody.

One of the report's other arguments was much weaker. It suggested that Monroe lacked the emotional organization to keep a diary. Distress is a poor basis for deciding whether someone could write private notes. That characterization adds little to the investigation. The documentary questions are firmer: who saw the object, when did they first report it, and can their accounts be checked independently?

The same problem of transmission affects claims that Robert Kennedy visited the house on August 4. The report traced one account through Slatzer to a woman recounting what her mother had said about a sighting. The original statement did not securely date the event. The specific date appeared in a later exchange prompted by the interviewer. In another account, a remembered visit concerned a publicized social call weeks before Monroe died.

A claim can therefore become more precise as it travels while becoming no better supported. “Someone saw Kennedy near the house” acquires the date of the death; a visit becomes an argument; the argument becomes a demand for a diary. Each addition changes the causal story.

The [Kennedy Library's records of May 19, 1962](https://jfk.blogs.archives.gov/2020/05/21/happy-birthday-mr-president/) establish Monroe's performance at the president's birthday fundraiser and her presence at the later reception. They establish social access. Details of an affair, access to classified information, and a murder ordered to prevent disclosure require other evidence. The performance cannot supply all of that merely because it has become the best-known image of their connection.

The district attorney's inquiry found no reliable basis for placing Robert Kennedy at the house on the day of the death. The often-repeated description of his presence as a fact officially confirmed after years of denial misstates that result.

## Reading the federal file

The [FBI's released Monroe file](https://vault.fbi.gov/Marilyn%20Monroe/Marilyn%20Monroe%20Part%2001/at_download/file) includes reports about her political associations and her 1962 visit to Mexico. A March communication discusses information supplied by sources about people she met, including a social circle associated with communism. Other pages preserve publicity and later writing about her death.

There is reason to object to the gathering of such information about an actor's friendships. There is also a basic archival trap: an allegation placed inside an FBI file can later be presented as an allegation established by the FBI. A clipping about a murder theory shows that the bureau retained the clipping. An informant's account shows what was reported, with the informant's knowledge and reliability still to be assessed.

In the Mexico reports, the bureau was collecting accounts of friendships and political views. A passage about her reported conversation with Robert Kennedy concerned politics. The material shows the reach of information gathering into private life; the proposed access to operational secrets remains an allegation elsewhere in the literature.

The larger allegation of an intelligence operation requires a further documentary history. Similarities between a psychiatrist's influence and a supposed handler's methods cannot establish employment, funding, or instructions. Greenson's relationship with his patient can be assessed as a treatment relationship, including its dependence and boundaries, without assigning it to a CIA program on the strength of professional proximity.

The same applies to blacked-out passages. A redaction leaves information unavailable to the reader. It does not reveal whether the hidden material concerns a source's identity, an administrative matter, or evidence relevant to the death. Filling every concealed space with the most consequential possible secret produces a story more complete than the available record.

## What the 1982 review achieved

The review was a threshold inquiry intended to decide whether a full criminal investigation was justified. That narrower purpose matters when assessing its limits. It did not stage a public trial or recover everything lost since 1962.

It nevertheless did more than collect old newspaper stories and announce closure. Carroll and investigator Alan Tomich reported interviewing forty people, consulting four pathologists to varying degrees, obtaining records from several agencies, and visiting the house to check witnesses' possible views of it. The underlying file includes requests, interview reports, and the extended Stephens transcript. Its work can be examined rather than taken entirely on trust.

The telephone inquiry is a good example. The report placed the police acquisition of toll records roughly two weeks after Monroe's death, not before officers arrived at the house. It distinguished the Justice Department's switchboard from a private number belonging to Robert Kennedy. It also said the records did not capture incoming calls. These limits reduce what the bills can disclose, but they contradict the claim that investigators had no records to examine at all.

The review also exposed weaknesses in claims about electronic surveillance. Investigators followed reports of recordings and purported discoveries of unusual wiring. Some stories led to people who had heard about a tape without possessing it; another involved wiring observed years after Monroe's death and after the house had been rewired. These were problems of provenance, not merely an agency refusing to accept unwelcome evidence.

The report has limitations of its own. It sometimes dismisses a person's behavior too readily or relies on assumptions about the size a murder conspiracy would have needed. Its discussion of clothing and supposed female modesty is a poor substitute for relevant evidence. The medical consultations and checks of the diary story supplied much firmer reasons for its conclusion than those assumptions did.

Its central conclusion was that the available material did not justify opening a homicide investigation. Probable suicide remained reasonably supported, with accident receiving some support. That is the strongest conclusion this review supplies. It does not reconstruct every minute of the night, and a later witness or document must still be assessed on what it adds.

## A person outside the case file

Monroe's own voice is easily crowded out by people explaining what she must have known or wanted. In a 2001 television discussion, Richard Meryman recalled interviewing her shortly before her death for *Life*. He remembered her vitality and humor alongside her anger at being treated as an ornament and having private conversations become publicity. His account conveyed a person trying to describe the conditions of her fame, rather than a witness obligingly supplying clues to a future mystery.

Her work and plans belong to her history even when they cannot establish her last intention. So do illness, dependence on medication, and distress. None requires a verdict about whether she was weak, calculating, innocent, or dangerous enough to explain her death.

The evidence supports a fatal overdose and a qualified judgment of probable suicide. There are gaps in the first investigation and unresolved details of care. The more elaborate murder accounts depend on objects that have not been authenticated, disputed recollections, and forensic propositions that the reviewing physicians rejected.

Monroe does not need to have possessed a state secret for her death to deserve serious attention. The surviving record asks for care in a more ordinary sense: distinguish what was observed from what was later added, preserve the limits of other people's recollections, and allow her life to remain larger than the explanation chosen for its end.
