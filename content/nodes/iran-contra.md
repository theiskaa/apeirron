In July 1985, the men buying weapons for Nicaragua’s Contras arranged to create a company called Defex. It was registered in Liberia and had a bank account in Switzerland. The name closely resembled that of the Portuguese supplier from which they bought the weapons. That resemblance was useful: a payment to their own company could look like a payment to the arms supplier.

The arrangement hid their markup from the people they were supplying. In a memorandum reproduced in [independent counsel Lawrence Walsh’s account](https://irp.fas.org/offdocs/walsh/chap_08.htm), financial manager Willard Zucker explained how the accounts could show payments approximately equal to receipts if anyone asked where the money had gone. The apparent expense included a profit the intermediaries were keeping.

American officials were concealing something larger: military support for the Contras after Congress restricted it, and weapons sales to Iran that contradicted public policy. Some proceeds crossed between the operations; other money went to the businessmen conducting them. When the operations became public, documents were destroyed and accounts rewritten to hide who had known and approved what.

The familiar account of Iran-Contra follows the arms proceeds to the guerrillas. Following the authority is just as revealing. A small group could obtain public resources, use private agents to carry out decisions, and deny responsibility when Congress asked what was being done.

## Keeping the Contras supplied

The Sandinistas came to power in Nicaragua in 1979 after overthrowing the Somoza dictatorship. The Reagan administration regarded their ties to Cuba and the Soviet Union, and their support for revolution elsewhere in Central America, as a threat. Beginning in 1981, the CIA assisted armed opponents of the Sandinista government, known collectively as the Contras. Their war depended heavily on outside weapons, money and bases.

Congress imposed restrictions in stages. The first Boland amendment, enacted in 1982, prohibited specified assistance for the purpose of overthrowing Nicaragua’s government. A later measure capped funding. Disclosure of the CIA’s role in mining Nicaraguan harbors further damaged support for the administration’s policy. In October 1984, Congress enacted a broader prohibition on funds available to the CIA, the Defense Department and other American entities involved in intelligence activities being used to support military or paramilitary operations in Nicaragua.

The restrictions changed again, allowing limited forms of assistance, and Congress eventually restored substantial military aid in 1986. During the intervening restrictions, the administration developed a supply operation that it concealed from the legislators deciding what assistance to allow.

Reagan wanted the Contras kept alive. His national security adviser, Robert McFarlane, assigned much of that work to Oliver North, a Marine officer on the National Security Council staff. The NSC’s role was to advise and coordinate policy. North increasingly handled operations: contacting commanders, arranging money, obtaining intelligence and connecting suppliers to the war.

Senior officials discussed alternative financing before the broad cutoff took effect. Minutes of a June 25, 1984 meeting record Secretary of State George Shultz raising a warning attributed to White House chief of staff James Baker: soliciting money from other countries could be an impeachable offense. CIA director William Casey disputed that account of Baker’s position. Defense Secretary Caspar Weinberger argued that helping the Contras obtain another country’s money differed from spending American government funds. Vice President George Bush supported encouraging third countries but recognized that promises of something in return could create a problem.

McFarlane proposed waiting for legal advice before anyone sought outside support. According to the investigations, he did not disclose the Saudi contributions already being arranged. The meeting ended with the officials debating whether to proceed with fundraising that had begun.

## Private money with official direction

North enlisted retired Air Force officer Richard Secord, who had experience in covert supply work, and Secord’s business partner Albert Hakim. They provided companies, bank accounts, aircraft and personnel. Their network became known as the Enterprise, a name Secord introduced during the investigations. According to Walsh’s report, the participants had not used it while the operations were running.

The network could move a shipment, maintain aircraft and pay crews without a payment appearing as a normal American appropriation for the Contras. Its infrastructure included access to Ilopango air base in El Salvador and a secret airstrip in Costa Rica. Separate companies handled aircraft, maintenance, billing and on-site management. From his NSC office, North directed work and drew on the resources and standing of government.

Saudi Arabia was a major source of funds. Wealthy Americans also contributed, encouraged by briefings and opportunities to meet Reagan. North helped fundraisers Carl Channell and Richard Miller use the White House’s prestige to attract donors. Money raised through a tax-exempt organization helped purchase lethal supplies, an activity outside its charitable purposes. Both fundraisers later pleaded guilty to conspiring to defraud the government through that use of the organization.

The suppliers’ private status gave a misleading picture of how the operation worked. The congressional investigation found that CIA and other government personnel provided logistical or tactical help, that diplomats assisted the operation, and that secure communications equipment came from the NSA. Congress was being assured that the NSC staff was obeying the restrictions while a government officer coordinated an armed supply effort through nominally private agents.

Control over the money also shifted. Initially, Contra leader Adolfo Calero used foreign contributions to buy arms. In mid-1985, North and Secord arranged for new contributions to go directly to the suppliers’ accounts. The men procuring the weapons would now control much of the money intended to pay for them. That gave Secord and Hakim considerable power over their customer, including the ability to conceal how much they charged above cost.

North later testified that Casey had envisaged a self-financing covert capability available without new appropriations. The congressional report took that possibility seriously, while warning that much of its evidence about Casey came from North after Casey’s death. The actual supply network was also being prepared for a more immediate commercial use. As Congress moved toward restoring aid in 1986, its managers valued the assets and proposed selling them to the CIA or leasing them for a monthly payment. The CIA was reluctant to take over assets of an operation from which it had been trying to distance itself.

## The hostage bargain

The Iran initiative began from different concerns. Americans had been kidnapped in Lebanon by groups connected to Iran, and Reagan wanted them home. Other officials also hoped for contacts with Iranian figures who might improve relations with Washington or gain power after Ayatollah Khomeini. Iran was fighting a costly war with Iraq and wanted weapons and spare parts for American equipment acquired before its revolution.

Israeli intermediaries offered a route. In 1985, Israel supplied American-made TOW anti-tank missiles to Iran, with American agreement to replenish Israeli stocks. Benjamin Weir, one of the hostages, was released in September. The apparent success encouraged further transactions, although the promise of releasing the remaining hostages was repeatedly deferred.

An arms sale could serve the political opening and the hostage bargain at once. Selling weapons might demonstrate the influence of supposed moderates, but the people arranging the sales had financial incentives to exaggerate their access. The United States was trying to discover who could deliver a hostage through the same intermediaries who benefited when another shipment was approved. One important broker, Manuchehr Ghorbanifar, had already been regarded by the CIA as a fabricator.

The congressional report described a further problem: the NSC staff was gathering information, recommending policy and carrying out the transactions. Officials committed to the initiative could present intelligence in its favor while excluding objections. Shultz warned against combining those functions. A broker’s promise could move from a claim needing verification to a reason for another shipment without an independent assessment intervening.

The cabinet was divided. Weinberger’s notes of a December 7, 1985 meeting record his warning that an embargo made the proposed arms sales illegal and that routing them through Israel would not cure the problem. Shultz agreed. Weinberger recorded Reagan replying that he could face accusations of illegality but could not accept having passed up a chance to free the hostages. In Weinberger’s account, freeing the hostages had become Reagan’s answer to objections about legality.

Presidential authorization took several forms. After a troubled November 1985 HAWK missile shipment involving CIA assistance, a written presidential authorization, known as a finding, was prepared to cover that activity retrospectively. Poindexter later said Reagan signed it in December; Poindexter destroyed it when the scandal broke. The surviving finding, signed on January 17, 1986, authorized an expanded operation involving direct American arms transfers.

[The January finding and its cover memorandum](https://nsarchive2.gwu.edu/NSAEBB/NSAEBB210/15-Reagan%20Finding%201-17-86%20(IC%2002181).pdf) laid out the proposed strategic opening and hostage releases. They also directed that Congress not be told until Reagan ordered otherwise. The signed instruction made withholding information from the oversight committees part of the operation. Congress learned of the operation months later through its exposure. The requirement to report in a timely fashion became a central legal dispute.

The sales continued without delivering the comprehensive release the administration sought. McFarlane went to Tehran in May 1986 expecting a bargain that the Iranian side did not accept on the same terms. Parts were delivered; the mission failed to bring the hostages home. By the time the transactions were exposed, three Americans had been released during the initiative and three others had been kidnapped. The desire to retrieve the remaining prisoners continued to provide a reason for another deal.

## What happened to the proceeds

North connected the Iran transactions to Contra support by arranging to retain a large difference between the government’s price and the price charged through the intermediaries. An April 1986 memorandum described a proposed transaction and allocated $12 million in expected residual funds to supplies for the Nicaraguan resistance. The proposal is unusually direct about a connection that officials later denied knowing existed.

The $12 million was a proposed allocation. In the accounts, proceeds from arms sales could become a payment for supplies, a reserve for a later operation or a benefit to the intermediaries. Investigators had to establish how much had actually been spent on the Contras.

In one February 1986 transaction, the Enterprise received $10 million for 1,000 TOW missiles and paid $3.7 million into the CIA account used to purchase them from Defense. Transport and other direct expenses used some of the difference. A substantial balance remained under the private network’s control. North had told the CIA and Defense that the selling price would be $6 million, concealing the full $10 million receipt from them.

The Contra arms business also generated profits. The Liberian Defex company helped hide them by giving transfers the appearance of payments to the Portuguese supplier. Other companies and accounts separated the visible transaction from the people benefiting. This protected the businessmen from scrutiny by both government officials and the Contras. Opposition to communism did not make their interests identical to those of the forces they were supplying.

Walsh’s investigators reconstructed more than $47 million in receipts from the Iran and Contra operations. For the diversion, they calculated at least $3.6 million that could have been proved at trial, with additional likely Contra expenses beyond that conservative figure. Money had been mixed in common accounts, making a precise dollar-by-dollar reconstruction difficult. The report separately identified roughly $16 million in government arms-sale funds retained by the Enterprise beyond its payments to the United States and direct expenses. It included money used or held for other purposes.

There were commissions, personal benefits and investments as well as operational spending. Around $7.8 million remained in the Swiss accounts when they were frozen. The network had financed clandestine activities while also becoming a profitable business for people acting on the government’s behalf. Its freedom from ordinary controls gave them room to decide which costs, reserves and benefits deserved payment.

## Exposure and the struggle over the record

The public learned about the two operations separately. On October 5, 1986, a supply aircraft was shot down over Nicaragua. Its surviving crewman, Eugene Hasenfus, was captured and said he had been working for the CIA. Officials denied an American government connection to the flight. On November 3, the Lebanese publication Al-Shiraa reported the arms dealings with Iran. On November 25, Attorney General Edwin Meese publicly announced that proceeds had been diverted to the Contras.

The interval between exposure and an organized investigation gave participants time to change the record. North and his secretary Fawn Hall destroyed documents. Chronologies were rewritten to obscure prior American knowledge of the 1985 shipments. Poindexter destroyed the earlier finding. A surviving diversion memorandum escaped destruction and was found by Justice Department investigators.

These actions made later disagreements harder to resolve. A witness who said he could not remember could be checked against fewer contemporary records; another could assign knowledge to someone who could no longer answer. Casey died in May 1987, before he could testify to the congressional committees. Claims about his proposals and instructions often depended on North’s account. The committees treated his involvement as serious while acknowledging how much of that evidence came from a participant explaining his own conduct.

Reagan’s knowledge of the arms initiative was well established. His knowledge of the diversion was a different question. Poindexter said he had approved it and had not told Reagan, preserving the president’s ability to deny knowing. North said he assumed presidential approval but also acknowledged being told that Reagan had not been informed. The congressional majority held Reagan responsible for the policies and the environment in which the operation developed, without claiming it had conclusively established his prior knowledge of the diversion.

Bush attended important discussions of the Iran initiative, and his diary after its exposure described him as one of the few people familiar with the details. That is evidence about his access to the arms initiative, not an admission that he knew about the diverted proceeds. Walsh later disputed Bush’s broader claims to have been outside the operation’s orbit. Relevant diary material was not produced to his investigators until December 1992.

## Three investigations, different powers

Reagan appointed the Tower board to examine what had gone wrong in the national security process. Congress conducted joint hearings and issued a report in 1987. Walsh’s independent counsel investigation pursued criminal cases over several further years. The Tower board examined administration of the policy; Congress assessed conduct and political responsibility; prosecutors had to prove specified offenses with evidence a court could admit.

The congressional report was sharply divided. Its majority condemned the evasion of oversight and treated the NSC operation as violating the restrictions. The minority, including Representative Dick Cheney, emphasized presidential foreign-policy authority, changing statutory language and legitimate fears of leaks. It rejected the majority’s account of an administration-wide conspiracy and argued that the record supported Reagan’s lack of knowledge of the diversion.

The Boland restrictions were appropriations provisions. They carried no separate criminal penalty. Their application to the NSC was disputed. Walsh argued that the operation also defeated congressional oversight through deceit and implicated resources of agencies expressly covered by the restrictions. The prosecution’s conspiracy theory concerned obstruction of lawful government functions, rather than simply charging a man with breaking Boland.

Even that broader charge did not reach a jury in North’s case. The administration refused to release classified information that the court considered necessary to the defense, and the charge was dropped. Keeping information secret could therefore prevent a trial of the arrangements secrecy had helped create. A defendant’s right to present a defense remained real; so did the government’s responsibility for choosing to withhold the material needed to test the case.

Congress had described a coordinated operation, and the court had allowed the conspiracy charge as legally sufficient. The jury never got to test it. The later trials established narrower offenses, leaving the central charge untried.

## The cost of compelled testimony

Congress made a separate choice that damaged the prosecutions. To obtain public testimony from North and Poindexter, it granted immunity against the use of their compelled testimony and evidence derived from it. This did not give them general immunity for their conduct. Prosecutors could still bring charges using genuinely independent evidence.

But their testimony was broadcast nationally. Witnesses could watch it, remember an event differently after hearing it, or incorporate something they had learned into their own account. Keeping the prosecutors away from the broadcasts did not necessarily keep the evidence independent.

North was convicted on three counts in 1989, including obstruction and destruction of documents. The appeals court required a much more searching examination of whether his protected testimony had influenced witnesses. The issue was not only how investigators had found a witness, but where the content of that witness’s testimony came from. After further hearings on immunity, Walsh moved to dismiss the case in 1991. Poindexter’s convictions were also overturned on immunity grounds.

Those reversals did not establish that the underlying operations were lawful or that the documents had never been destroyed. They enforced a protection attached to testimony the government had compelled. Congress obtained public answers at a cost to criminal accountability. The state had promised protection to secure the answers, then could not establish the independence required to sustain the prosecutions.

Other cases produced guilty pleas or convictions that stood. Secord pleaded guilty to lying to Congress. Hakim pleaded guilty to supplementing North’s salary; Lake Resources, an Enterprise company, pleaded guilty to theft of government property. Thomas Clines was convicted of tax offenses and served a prison sentence. The investigation did impose consequences, although they fell unevenly and did not produce a trial resolving every senior official’s responsibility.

## Pardons and the unfinished questions

On December 24, 1992, Bush pardoned Weinberger, McFarlane, Elliott Abrams and three CIA officials: Duane Clarridge, Alan Fiers and Clair George. Weinberger and Clarridge had not yet gone to trial. Others had pleaded guilty or been convicted. Bush’s proclamation defended their patriotism, emphasized their lack of personal financial gain, and portrayed the prosecutions as turning policy disputes into crimes. He argued that political differences belonged before voters.

Walsh regarded the pardons as completing a cover-up. They prevented the Weinberger trial, where contemporary notes and earlier accounts could have been tested through testimony, and ended the prospect of obtaining further cooperation through the pardoned cases. Bush was himself a potential witness. The dispute was therefore about more than mercy to former officials: a president used his constitutional power to stop trials capable of examining events in which he had participated.

Walsh continued examining matters including the late production of Bush’s diary and submitted his final report in 1993. He described his investigation of Bush as incomplete, while acknowledging that the limitation periods for prosecuting most relevant acts had expired. A proposed further interview failed over limits Bush sought to impose; Walsh did not issue a grand jury subpoena, citing the lack of a sufficient prospect of prosecution. That left questions he had planned to ask Bush unanswered.

The broader Contra war also raised drug-trafficking questions that the arms-diversion account cannot absorb into one ledger. [The Senate inquiry led by John Kerry](https://nsarchive2.gwu.edu/NSAEBB/NSAEBB113/north06.pdf) found trafficking by Contra supporters, use of supply networks by traffickers, assistance from traffickers to Contra elements, and payments to trafficker-linked suppliers from American humanitarian-aid funds. It sharply criticized the official response. Its findings concern particular suppliers, traffickers, payments and official decisions. They cannot supply the missing transactions in an account that treats all Contra support as one cocaine-funded operation. The [[cia-drugs]] essay follows those investigations and their separate claims.

Iran-Contra demonstrates how official direction can persist after responsibility has been dispersed. North could invoke presidential policy; private suppliers could invoke government authorization; senior officials could deny knowing the details. The same distance that made those denials possible let businessmen conceal their own interests inside the operation.

The duplicate Defex name captures that arrangement on a small scale. An account could appear to show that the money had gone to a supplier while hiding who had benefited. Congressional oversight faced a larger version of the same problem: assurances that the rules were being followed, backed by accounts and explanations designed to prevent anyone outside the operation from checking.
