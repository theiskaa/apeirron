Robert Stevens worked as a photo editor for a Florida tabloid publisher. In October 2001 he became the first person in the United States diagnosed with inhalational anthrax since 1976. He died on October 5. At first, public health investigators had one patient and no established route of exposure. The appearance of additional cases, along with evidence from his workplace, turned a rare diagnosis into an investigation of deliberate attacks.

Five people died and seventeen others fell ill. The letters went to news organizations and to two senators, but the deaths showed how little the names on the envelopes told investigators about who was at risk. Two of the people who died, Thomas Morris Jr. and Joseph Curseen Jr., worked at the Brentwood postal facility in Washington, D.C. They were not addressees. The public health question was where people had encountered the material and who else might have been exposed. The criminal question, which gave the FBI investigation its name, was who had sent it. The first question had to be answered quickly; answering it did not settle the second.

## From a workplace to the mail

The [CDC’s account of the public health investigation](https://wwwnc.cdc.gov/eid/article/8/10/02-0353_article) records twenty-two cases, eleven involving inhalation and eleven involving infection of the skin. Twenty patients either handled mail or worked at sites where implicated mail was received or processed. The investigators compared diagnoses with workplaces, the movement of mail, and evidence of contamination. Taken together, those records explained why cases clustered among postal and media workers. The scattered cases were harder. In Connecticut, mail records gave investigators a reason to suspect that an elderly woman who died had been exposed through contamination of ordinary mail. The route was plausible, but they could not identify a particular piece she had handled as the source.

At Brentwood the implications were clearer. The postal facility had to be treated as an exposure site even though the letters were addressed elsewhere. The decisions that followed, including treatment for workers and testing at sites that had handled implicated mail, did not require a theory about the sender. By contrast, naming the sender required evidence specific to a person. Much of the public controversy over Amerithrax arose from how the FBI got from evidence about the material to a conclusion about the person who used it.

## From a laboratory sample to a suspect

The material in the letters was identified as the Ames strain, which was shared across laboratories. That narrowed the investigation without identifying a single source. The FBI built a collection of samples and compared them with the material from the letters. The comparisons pointed to a reference batch maintained at the Army’s research institute at Fort Detrick by microbiologist Bruce Ivins. Samples related to that batch were available to other researchers. Custody made Ivins important to the inquiry; it was not exclusive access.

That distinction had already mattered in the case of Steven Hatfill. A former Army biodefense researcher, Hatfill was publicly identified as a person of interest in 2002. According to the Justice Department’s later summary, he had worked where the Ames strain was available, but investigators found that he had not had access to the particular batch they came to regard as the source. He was cleared and reached a settlement with the government in 2008. His case illustrates the cost of a public identification that gets ahead of the evidence: working in the right field and having access to the general strain had looked incriminating until investigators asked a narrower question about access.

The case against Ivins was more specific. For both sets of letters, investigators pointed to records showing that he had spent unusual hours at the lab before the mailings. They also argued that the sample he initially supplied to the investigation did not represent the batch he maintained, and that other people with access had been investigated and excluded. Each of these facts had to carry a particular inference. The logs, for example, recorded when Ivins was present, not what he did while he was there. The sample discrepancy gave the FBI a reason to examine his conduct, but a discrepancy is not proof of an attempt to deceive. The published summary did not produce a witness who saw Ivins mail the letters or a record placing him at the mailbox. Its conclusion depended on how the strands fit together and how thoroughly competing explanations had been excluded.

Ivins died in July 2008, as prosecutors were preparing to seek an indictment. He was never charged. In February 2010 the Justice Department closed the investigation and said he had acted alone. The investigative summary is the prosecution’s account of the case; it says some material was omitted because it came from grand jury proceedings, sealed orders, or an off-the-record interview. No trial tested what the logs established, what the disputed submission meant, or how investigators ruled out other people.

## What the scientific review found

In 2011 a National Research Council committee examined the science at the FBI’s request. It did not review the whole criminal case or consider whether Ivins was guilty. The [committee’s summary](https://www.nationalacademies.org/read/13098/chapter/2) says the results were consistent with the Army reference batch being a source of the material in the letters, but did not definitively demonstrate that relationship. The panel also distinguished a possible ancestor from the immediate source: it found that the material in the letters had not come directly from the reference flask. A relationship to the batch would therefore still leave an intermediate history to explain. And because the FBI’s collection did not represent every sample that might matter, ruling out the samples in hand was not the same as ruling out every other source.

Even a definitive link to the batch would not have answered the question the Justice Department announced it had resolved. A finding about a shared sample cannot distinguish among people who had access to it. Conversely, the committee’s finding does not show that Ivins was uninvolved. It reduces the certainty of one link in the prosecution’s account while leaving the other evidence to be judged on its own. Those limits are more useful than a verdict the committee was never asked to deliver.

## The letters and the law

One of the Senate letters was opened in Majority Leader Tom Daschle’s office on October 15. The other was addressed to Patrick Leahy, chair of the Judiciary Committee. President George W. Bush signed the Patriot Act on October 26. The sequence has prompted the claim that the senators were targeted to push the legislation through. The legislative record supplies a useful check: the Senate had already passed an earlier version of the antiterrorism bill on October 11, four days before the Daschle letter was opened. Leahy continued to put objections about specific provisions on the record as the Senate debated the final bill. He and Daschle both voted for it on October 25. The attack added to the fear in which Congress finished its work, but the record shows bargaining and broad support already in place. It supplies no evidence that passage depended on the letters or that bringing about the vote was the sender’s purpose.

The same problem applies to early speculation about foreign responsibility. The attacks came almost immediately after [[nine-eleven]], and the letters invoked September 11. That helped make them look like a second phase of the same attack. Suspicions about Iraq also circulated, but the investigation did not establish an Iraqi source. The authority the government acquired in that period can be traced through votes, statutes, and court records. There is no comparable record showing that the sender chose the senators to bring about those votes.

At Brentwood, the evidence of exposure was enough to act on without knowing who sent the letters. The evidence about Ivins had to do more: distinguish him from other people who could have had access to the same material. The government concluded it had done that. The scientific review addressed the source of the material, not the identity of the sender, and the trial that would have tested the rest of the case never took place.
