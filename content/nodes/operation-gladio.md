In early 1972, the discovery of a weapons cache near Aurisina, outside Trieste, entered the official record. It belonged to an organization most Italians had never heard of. The military intelligence service intervened, offering misleading accounts of where the weapons had come from and pressing for the investigation to stop. The cache was part of Gladio, Italy's preparations for clandestine resistance after an enemy occupation.

On May 31 that year, a booby-trapped Fiat 500 killed three Carabinieri at Peteano. The eventual investigation established the responsibility of neo-fascist militants and exposed official obstruction of the original inquiry. It also raised questions about missing material from the Aurisina cache. Those questions helped bring a concealed defense organization into the history of political violence inside Italy.

Gladio had weapons, foreign support, and arrangements for resistance to invasion. Over time, its personnel and resources also became available for gathering information within Italian society. Officials concealed the organization from institutions entitled to supervise intelligence activities. Inquiries into terrorism exposed protection of far-right militants and manipulation of evidence. Responsibility for those acts, and for the attacks themselves, became the subject of overlapping investigations.

The most revealing documents concern the boundary between defending a country and intervening in its politics. That boundary was argued over inside the organization, revised in its plans, and obscured in what political leaders were told.

## Preparing to remain behind

A stay-behind network was meant to survive a defeat. If Soviet forces occupied Italy, its members would provide information, help people escape, sabotage transport and communications, and assist resistance. The members needed supplies they could reach after ordinary military installations had fallen. They also needed radio equipment, training, and identities that would withstand an occupation's searches.

This was a recognizable lesson from the Second World War. Resistance organizations had needed outside communications and dependable routes for moving people and material. Preparing those arrangements before another war could save lives. Early Cold War planners also faced the Korean War and the possibility of a conflict extending into Europe. The military purpose was substantial: maintain contact and the capacity to resist even after conventional defense had failed.

The [Italian parliamentary commission's 1992 report](https://www.docspopuli.it/fonti/Servizi/Gladio/gladio_commStragi_22-04-1992.pdf) follows the organization back through agreements and plans preceding its formal arrangement with the United States. An October 1951 memorandum from the head of Italian military intelligence proposed preparations for intelligence, sabotage, and escape under occupation. The Americans helped finance a training base at Capo Marrargiu in Sardinia. A November 1956 agreement between the CIA and Italy's military intelligence service put the bilateral relationship on a more formal footing.

Secrecy affected the paperwork as well as the recruits. The 1956 arrangement had only four bilingual official copies, according to the commission. Few people could consult the agreement governing the organization they were expected to maintain.

Recruitment proceeded slowly. A 1961 account listed only thirty-five external recruits, excluding personnel from certain existing formations. Plans were much larger: intelligence groups, sabotage teams, propaganda units, escape networks, and rapid guerrilla units. During the 1960s, the number of external recruits reached roughly three hundred. Gladio drew partly on earlier Italian organizations, including an anti-communist formation called Stella Alpina. Some of these recruits came from anti-communist partisan backgrounds. The early formations and the later individual recruits did not form a single wartime cohort.

The [list of 622 names released in 1990](https://legislature.camera.it/_bicamerali/sis/documen/xii34_1.htm) covered the organization over its history, including people who had died or left. It was not a count of 622 people ready for action that year. Later investigators also questioned the completeness and consistency of the records. The released roster could not by itself establish the network’s strength at disclosure or identify everyone who had assisted it.

Between 1963 and 1969, American-supplied material went into 139 caches known as Nasco deposits. A concealed store of weapons gave a small group a capacity it could otherwise acquire only through an exposed institution. It also created a practical danger: usable material remained in the country through years of political conflict, while the reason for its presence was secret.

## What NATO coordinated

Gladio was the Italian name. Other Western European countries maintained their own stay-behind arrangements, with different histories, services, and political controls. Using the Italian name for all of them can make a collection of national organizations sound like one army under a single headquarters.

Italy's dealings with NATO show a less straightforward relationship. In 1952, the country declined an invitation to join the Clandestine Planning Committee because it objected to unequal privileges within the arrangement. It continued to favor its bilateral relationship with the CIA. Italy joined that committee in 1959 and the Allied Clandestine Committee in 1964.

These bodies coordinated preparations across countries: communications, training, planning, and cooperation in wartime. NATO's senior military command had an important role in the surrounding defense arrangements. National intelligence services nevertheless retained authority over their clandestine organizations. The Italian report distinguishes the military forces assigned to allied command from the clandestine structures remaining under national control.

The distinction affected who could give an order and who should answer for it. A service could cooperate with allied planners while directing its own personnel at home. Conversely, foreign financing, equipment, and shared planning could give outsiders substantial influence without creating a direct command relationship for every operation. For Italy, both the bilateral agreement and the allied committees shaped the organization.

The Cold War produced other forms of American covert intervention, including the overthrow of Iran's government in [[operation-ajax]]. In Iran, the immediate objective was to remove a government. The Italian arrangements began with preparations for a war that had not happened, giving the organization a potentially indefinite life.

## The enemy moves inside

The division between occupation and domestic conflict was never entirely clean. Plans considered how internal disorder might accompany a foreign attack. Italy also had a large Communist Party participating in ordinary electoral politics. Treating communist influence as a preliminary stage of invasion could expand a military precaution into a project concerned with the direction of civilian life.

Documents examined by the Italian commission show this expansion being considered explicitly. In January 1966, an American representative proposed using the organization for peacetime counterinsurgency and suggested a course at Fort Bragg. The commission did not establish the Italian response to that proposal. It did identify an exercise near Trieste later that year involving insurgency and counterinsurgency. The invasion scenario had acquired an internal political setting.

The same files also show disagreement and limits. A December 1972 Italian memorandum recorded that American pressure for activity against internal subversion had produced no organizational follow-through. Plans, exercises, and operational use were different stages. The commission could trace the suggestion without establishing the general peacetime use the Americans sought.

The Aurisina discovery helped force a change in the weapons arrangements. General Gerardo Serravalle, who headed the organization in the early 1970s, ordered the caches retrieved with the approval of intelligence chief Vito Miceli. The service reported completing the retrieval program in June 1973. Serravalle was also concerned that some members understood their role primarily as a struggle against the domestic left. Roughly a hundred men were retired from the network.

American funding had ceased, and in December 1972 the American side revoked the old agreement in favor of annual arrangements. Subsequent Italian reports described reduced training, inadequate resources, and obsolete communications equipment. By the mid-1970s, officials were questioning the readiness of the organization they had inherited.

During the later 1970s and 1980s, the organization moved further toward intelligence and escape work. Gladio became part of the military intelligence service established in the 1977 reorganization, SISMI. Its members were now being asked to report on the society around them.

The 1992 commission described a reporting outline covering population, local administration, politics, the economy, transport, and communications. Surviving reports included information on places such as Sassari, Porto Torres, and Cervignano. These were accounts of life in Italian communities, gathered in peacetime. The commission regarded the collection as actual activity, rather than merely an exercise in writing reports for a hypothetical occupation.

An internal memorandum from July 1982 opposed general intelligence use before an emergency but allowed work against specific targets or in particular areas, coordinated through designated divisions. A 1987 memorandum approved by SISMI chief Fulvio Martini envisaged centers developing sources in workplaces and society while continuing preparations for war. The commission recovered reports concerning industrial security in Turin and possible sources for intelligence on Africa.

Such work gave Gladio a continuing purpose after its original caches had been dismantled. People recruited for an exceptional wartime condition could supply information during ordinary political and economic life. That change made the question of supervision more urgent: which authorities had approved the work, and what had they been told the organization did?

## Peteano and the protected perpetrators

At Peteano on May 31, 1972, an anonymous telephone call brought the Carabinieri to a Fiat 500. Its hood release had been rigged to detonate an explosive charge. Three men were killed: Antonio Ferraro, Donato Poveromo, and Franco Dongiovanni.

The investigation eventually reached Vincenzo Vinciguerra and Carlo Cicuttini, men associated with the neo-fascist organization Ordine Nuovo. Both received life sentences. Vinciguerra admitted responsibility in 1984. His account directed attention toward the assistance and protection available to people committing anti-communist violence.

The [parliamentary intelligence oversight committee's 1995 report](https://legislature.camera.it/_bicamerali/sis/documen/xii34_1.htm) describes an investigation repeatedly steered away from the perpetrators. An initial lead toward the left was followed by a focus on people from the local criminal milieu. Innocent suspects endured long detention. Other police were excluded from an inquiry controlled by the Carabinieri, while information useful to identifying the neo-fascist perpetrators failed to reach the proper investigators.

The report also examines a physical connection with the Aurisina cache. Pull igniters were missing from its recorded contents; Vinciguerra described using that type of device to trigger the Peteano bomb. There were discrepancies between inventories of the cache's material. Authorities had supplied misleading explanations of its origin and impeded investigation of a deposit known to belong to Gladio.

Another line of evidence concerned cartridge cases found at Peteano and at an attempted aircraft hijacking at Ronchi in October 1972, during which neo-fascist Ivano Boccaccio died. According to the committee, matching cases could have connected the episodes. The Peteano cases disappeared, and inspection reports were replaced. A potential route to identifying perpetrators had been damaged inside the investigation itself.

The committee described officials protecting an extremist milieu and obstructing justice. The missing igniters also supplied a reason to investigate whether weapons material had moved from a state-controlled cache into an attack. The record presented here does not establish a uniquely identified transfer from that cache to the bomb, or an order through NATO's command structure.

The handling of information remained a problem years after the attack. The committee reported that, in late 1978 or early 1979, SISMI chief Giuseppe Santovito received information identifying the far-right perpetrators. He favored sending it to the Carabinieri before the judiciary, keeping the inquiry with the institution whose earlier conduct required examination. Protection could consist of routing a useful report to the wrong place as well as destroying a piece of evidence.

## Who knew enough to govern?

Gladio became a public political crisis in 1990. After a parliamentary demand for information concerning the Bologna bombing investigation, Prime Minister Giulio Andreotti told a commission on August 3 that the stay-behind activity had ended in 1972. His written report of October 18 acknowledged that the organization was still active. The government dissolved it on November 27.

For an organization whose later history included years of reorganization and domestic reporting, the earlier answer concealed almost everything that now needed examination. Even after disclosure, parliamentary investigators encountered limits on access to allied committee documents. NATO-related confidentiality was invoked against the legislature trying to determine what an Italian service had been doing.

Some senior politicians had visited the Sardinian training base. Beginning in 1984, Martini used a written acknowledgment when briefing political leaders. Bettino Craxi signed one as prime minister; Giovanni Spadolini signed one as defense minister, although he had not been informed during his earlier premiership. Other political leaders were omitted. Intelligence chiefs exercised considerable discretion over whom to brief and how to describe the organization.

An acknowledgment of a resistance network could establish awareness of its existence without establishing approval of all its later activities. The description supplied to politicians therefore mattered as much as the signature. A minister informed about preparations against occupation had not necessarily been told about political information collected in peacetime.

The intelligence reform of 1977 had created arrangements for coordinating the services and for parliamentary supervision. Gladio remained outside the information shared through those channels. Continuing an old organization therefore involved more than preserving a secret from the public: the service was withholding it from parts of the machinery created to govern secret work.

The 1992 commission concluded that Gladio had developed serious constitutional and legal defects, especially in relation to those arrangements. The bilateral origin also posed a problem. A service’s agreement with the CIA did not automatically acquire the legal standing of the NATO treaty, with its parliamentary authorization. The main report challenged the assumption that a defense purpose could settle how the organization had been authorized and supervised. It also interpreted the organization as a component of a wider strategy producing political tension, while leaving criminal responsibility for particular acts to the courts.

Members dissenting from that report defended the network's legality. They argued that preparing resistance implemented Italy's existing defense commitments and that foreign-supported subversion could fall within a legitimate defense mission. They also pointed to evidence of political leaders' knowledge. The dispute concerned the lawful reach of secret defense preparations as well as the facts of their use.

P2 compounded the problem of trusting the intelligence services' accounts. This secret lodge had members at the top of several security institutions. The 1995 oversight report describes official responses dismissing concerns about senior officers' Masonic associations while military intelligence chief Giuseppe Santovito was himself a P2 member. Information on lodge leader Licio Gelli existed in service files but had not been supplied when relevant questions were asked.

The service’s denials concerned the very affiliations of people running it. Membership in P2 offered private loyalties and contacts across institutions nominally meant to supervise one another. The lodge’s connections with the financial scandals surrounding [[p2-banco-ambrosiano]] widened the potential conflicts of interest, although they did not make the lodge and Gladio the same organization.

## What Belgium could establish

Belgium had a military stay-behind organization, SDRA VIII, and a civilian component, STC/Mob. Its Senate investigated whether these arrangements had connections with extremist organizations, terrorism, or major criminal violence.

The [October 1991 report](https://www.senate.be/lexdocs/S0523/S05231297.pdf) said that no corroborating indication linked the stay-behind network to terrorism and major banditry on the information available. It also explained why the inquiry could not remove every doubt: the people running the organization refused to provide agents' identities for checking.

The proposed check was confidential. The Senate did not demand that the names be published. Three retired magistrates were to compare them with information concerning extremist organizations and unresolved attacks. In March 1991, the defense minister released military commander Bernard Legrand from secrecy and ordered him to cooperate. Legrand continued to refuse, invoking his promises to the agents and concerns for their families and social security.

His position placed a personal undertaking above a minister's instruction and a carefully restricted parliamentary investigation. The refusal also imposed a peculiar condition on scrutiny: investigators should first produce evidence against agents whose identities they were prevented from checking. A newspaper message assured members that their names would remain protected. The Senate described collective resistance to its investigation.

The Senate found no demonstrated terrorism link while recording how the names check had been blocked. Legrand’s continuing refusal showed how little practical authority his minister could exercise over the network’s inherited commitments.

Belgium also changed its oversight arrangements. A law published in July 1991 established a permanent intelligence oversight committee and an investigative service. The Senate's recommendations addressed ministerial knowledge of agreements, financial control, foreign relationships, and the danger of services developing parallel arrangements beyond political authority. The disclosure did produce institutional responses, even where the inquiry left consequential questions unanswered.

## The record after disclosure

The European Parliament took a strong position in its [resolution of November 22, 1990](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/PDF/?uri=OJ%3AJOC_1990_324_R_0186_01). It condemned clandestine networks operating outside democratic control, protested the role claimed by American military and allied authorities, and called for their dismantling. It demanded investigation of illegal activity and possible involvement in terrorism. Its request for inquiries into links, if any, between the networks and terrorism preserved a question that a parliamentary resolution could not itself adjudicate.

Italian investigators had alleged that records were suppressed or concealed to obstruct discovery of Gladio's history. A [Rome court acquitted Martini, Paolo Inzerilli, and Gianantonio Invernizzi on July 3, 2001](https://www.gerograssi.it/cms2/file/casomoro/DVD23/0348_001.pdf), in the prosecution concerning those records. A [subsequent parliamentary report](https://www.parlamento.it/application/xmanager/projects/parlamento/file/Doc_XXIII_n_37_Sez_10.pdf) records that the acquittal became final. The judgment concerned the charged conduct involving records, rather than a judicial determination of every dispute surrounding Gladio.

The concealed organization is thoroughly documented. So are changes in its purpose, serious failures of political supervision, and official interference in investigations of political violence. The investigations brought some acts into view while leaving the origins of others disputed. Each depended on particular witnesses, records, and decisions about access.

Secret defense preparations could be legitimate, and some elected leaders knew they existed. Yet the services controlled the information needed to decide whether those preparations remained within their approved purpose. In Italy, they could describe a wartime resistance network while developing peacetime information work. In Belgium, a commander could refuse a confidential check ordered by his minister. The people responsible for supervision were left negotiating for the knowledge they needed to exercise it.
