For years, Tupac Shakur and Christopher Wallace appeared in the same unfinished sentence: two young rappers, two shootings, no one convicted. Their photographs became shorthand for a feud that had consumed its participants. The resemblance between their deaths encouraged a larger assumption—that discovering who killed one would explain who killed the other.

The cases have since diverged. On August 31, 2026, a Nevada jury convicted Duane “Keffe D” Davis of first-degree murder in Shakur’s killing. He received a sentence of forty years to life on October 13. An appeal was reported in November. Wallace’s murder, six months after Shakur’s, remains unresolved. There has been no corresponding conviction in Los Angeles. [Clark County’s sentencing release](https://www.clarkcountynv.gov/adobe/assets/urn:aaid:aem:6b9b80cb-6807-4019-88bb-0128a79bbcbf/original/as/Duane-Davis-sentencing.pdf) records Davis’s conviction and punishment.

The histories still belong together. The artists had been friends, became public enemies, and worked around people whose business relationships overlapped with street gangs. Later investigations crossed jurisdictions and returned to some of the same witnesses. The central question is how far those connections explain the killings—and where the evidence begins to tell two different stories.

## How a friendship became a public story

Shakur survived an earlier shooting in November 1994, in the lobby of Quad Recording Studios in New York. In his subsequent interview with Kevin Powell for *VIBE*, he described going upstairs afterward and finding Biggie, Sean “Puffy” Combs, and others. He interpreted their behavior as a sign that they knew more than they were saying. Someone he trusted had become someone he suspected.

In [Powell’s interview](https://www.vibe.com/features/editorial/tupac-april-1995-cover-story-ready-to-live-686969/), Shakur recalled who looked surprised, who cried, and who failed to approach him. The reactions disturbed him. He had arrived wounded and found people he knew, yet felt isolated among them. He could describe what they did; he could not know from those reactions what they had discussed before he arrived. His belief in their betrayal nevertheless became a fact of the relationship. A friendship could break down before the accusations behind the break were established.

Biggie and Combs denied involvement. Subsequent records, interviews, and publicity turned an intimate grievance into a contest between labels and coasts: Combs’s Bad Boy in New York, Suge Knight’s Death Row in Los Angeles. Shakur’s alignment with Death Row gave the hostility a business setting as well as a musical one. The argument could reach people who had never met either artist and knew little about the original shooting.

“East Coast versus West Coast” was an effective headline because it made allegiance easy. It was a poor description of the actual range of people involved. Shakur himself had been born in New York. A record label, a neighborhood gang, a group of friends, and a regional music scene did not have identical membership or identical interests. Treating them as two armies erased the particular relationships investigators would later have to reconstruct.

The rivalry made a ready-made motive available after each death. But the case in Las Vegas would turn on an altercation inside a hotel, involving a man who was neither Biggie nor Combs. The public dispute supplied a background; the events of that evening supplied a more immediate reason for retaliation.

## The fight before the shooting

On September 7, 1996, Shakur attended the Mike Tyson fight at the MGM Grand with Knight and their entourage. Hotel security footage captured them taking part in an assault on Orlando Anderson near an elevator bank. Anderson was Davis’s nephew and associated with the South Side Compton Crips. The violence inside the hotel would become central to the prosecution’s explanation of what happened later that night.

Shakur left with Knight, who drove a black BMW. A white Cadillac pulled alongside near Flamingo Road and Koval Lane, and shots were fired into the BMW. Shakur died six days later. In the account Las Vegas police presented when Davis was arrested in 2023, Davis learned of the beating, obtained a gun, and joined Anderson, Terrence Brown, and Deandre Smith in the Cadillac. He then passed the weapon to the rear of the car. Police identified the attack as retaliation for the assault at the MGM. Their [press conference](https://transcripts.cnn.com/show/cnc/date/2023-09-29/segment/12) placed a specific event between the general gang hostility and the shooting.

The security footage recorded the assault that allegedly prompted the retaliation. To follow the men from the hotel to the Cadillac, they needed other evidence. The recording stopped short of the planning and shooting that Davis later described.

The [2023 indictment](https://www.8newsnow.com/wp-content/uploads/sites/59/2023/09/Davis-Duane-Keith-C377407.pdf) made Davis’s alleged role explicit. It described obtaining a firearm for retaliation and supplying it to Anderson or Smith with the intent that the killing be committed. It also alleged concerted action involving the four men. Liability did not depend on proving that Davis personally fired.

At the 2026 trial, prosecutors allowed that either rear-seat passenger might have been the gunman while seeking Davis’s conviction for arranging the attack. A person who intentionally equips and assists a planned killing can bear responsibility for the murder. Establishing the organizer’s conduct and identifying the finger on the trigger are related questions, but they can have different evidential answers. The indictment had already set out that approach years before the verdict.

## The witness who kept describing his own involvement

Davis repeatedly put himself inside the Cadillac. The statements highlighted by prosecutors at trial included a 2008 proffer to investigators, a 2009 police interview, a television documentary interview, and his 2019 memoir, *Compton Street Legend*. [Contemporary trial reporting](https://apnews.com/article/f28d5d0af3e5d8e71eb7bd2379c37f26) identifies those separate occasions. For prosecutors, this was a participant describing his own conduct. For the defense, it was a man with reasons to tell a useful story to police and a saleable one to an audience.

The passage of time made that problem harder. Details of the shooting had circulated for years. Someone repeating a publicly known account could sound knowledgeable without having witnessed every event he described. Conversely, a participant might change a detail to reduce his responsibility while continuing to disclose facts that placed him in jeopardy. Repetition had to be assessed alongside the origin of the information and whatever could be checked independently.

A [September 2023 grand jury transcript](https://ewscripps.brightspotcdn.com/02/ff/c215ae8a4445937ccf5c3bcc6f82/c-23-377407-1-transcript-of-proceedings-trans-crm-1.pdf) shows jurors pressing on these points. Detective Clifford Mogg described hours of public interviews, acknowledged inconsistencies, and said the central account remained similar. Asked whether police had recovered the murder weapon or the Cadillac, he answered that neither had been recovered. Investigators had also been unable to obtain the rental history needed to identify the car through a particular renter.

The search of Davis’s home produced records connected with the story he had told, including clippings and a copy of his book. Mogg said the electronics were collected to examine communications about interviews, the documentary, and the book, along with possible payments. It was an investigation into the making of his accounts as well as their contents. A clipping preserves what a person could have learned by reading. A hotel record could establish his presence in the city. Neither, by itself, would place him in the Cadillac. The value of a supporting detail depended on which part of his account it actually supported.

The defense also challenged the prosecution’s use of statements made under a proffer arrangement with federal investigators. Such an arrangement permits a person to provide information under negotiated conditions; its protection depends on its terms and on which authorities are bound. It need not confer permanent immunity for everything the speaker later says in a book or interview.

In November 2025, the [Nevada Supreme Court declined Davis’s request for pretrial intervention](https://caselaw.findlaw.com/court/nv-supreme-court/117916551.html). It said he had not demonstrated that protections extended to later law-enforcement interviews or bound Nevada. The court also noted gaps in the materials submitted and explained that challenges to the sufficiency of trial evidence could be raised on direct appeal after a conviction. It was a refusal to dismiss at that stage, rather than a verdict on all the evidence a jury would later hear.

At trial, prosecutor Binu Palal presented Davis’s repeated accounts as admissions supported by surrounding circumstances. Defense lawyer Michael Sanft argued that the state was relying on unreliable confessions without adequate evidence behind them. [ABC’s report of the closing arguments and verdict](https://abcnews.com/US/jury-begins-deliberating-trial-murder-tupac-shakur/story?id=136099547) records that dispute. The jury found Davis guilty. His conviction is a consequential judgment about his responsibility, rather than confirmation of every allegation ever attached to Shakur’s death. By November 2026, the [appeal was proceeding in the Nevada Supreme Court](https://www.fox5vegas.com/2026/11/10/nevada-supreme-court-accepts-duane-keffe-d-davis-appeal/). The conviction remained in place.

## Why suspicion turned toward Los Angeles police

Wallace was twenty-four when he was shot after leaving a music-industry party at the Petersen Automotive Museum on March 9, 1997. Six months had passed since Shakur’s death. Another famous rapper had been attacked while sitting in a vehicle, this time in Los Angeles. The resemblance made retaliation an obvious line of inquiry. It also created a temptation to treat a plausible motive as a complete identification of the people responsible.

Police corruption gave the Los Angeles investigation another direction. Officer David Mack was convicted of bank robbery. Rafael Perez, another officer, stole cocaine from police evidence and became a cooperating source in the Rampart scandal. Perez later pleaded guilty to federal offenses arising from the shooting and framing of Javier Ovando. Officers had used the authority of a police report and courtroom testimony to put an injured, unarmed man in prison. The [Rampart chronology](https://www.pbs.org/wgbh/pages/frontline/shows/lapd/scandal/cron.html) traces those proceedings.

An allegation about police involvement now concerned identifiable officers whose conduct warranted investigation. The LAPD also had a conflict to manage. It had to investigate a murder while facing allegations against members of its own force, and any discovery implicating an officer could deepen its legal and public troubles. The family needed those leads followed wherever they went.

Detective Russell Poole believed that evidence involving corrupt officers and Death Row had been insufficiently pursued. In a [2001 interview with FRONTLINE](https://www.pbs.org/wgbh/pages/frontline/shows/lapd/interviews/poole.html), he described a chain of leads involving Mack, gang associations, and an informant’s reference to a supposed killer called Amir. A visitor to Mack in custody, Amir Muhammad, became important to his theory. Poole also said Mack owned a black Impala of the kind associated with the murder and that permission for a further search had been refused.

The interviewer asked the direct question: did he know that Mack’s car had been used? Poole answered that they might never know. A similar vehicle was a reason to investigate Mack, and Poole believed the opportunity to examine it properly had been lost. He was left with a lead whose strongest possible test he said he had been prevented from carrying out.

The department [publicly rejected his cover-up account](https://www.lapdonline.org/newsroom/from-the-homicide-files-notorious-big-not-a-cover-up/). Neither its denial nor his experience as a detective settled the disputed identifications. His strongest criticism concerned actions that could have produced better evidence: searches, examination of a vehicle, and follow-up on relationships. The value of those actions did not require assuming their results in advance.

## What the family’s lawsuit uncovered

Wallace’s family pursued a civil case against the city. It provided a way to demand records and question officials even without a criminal prosecution. During the 2005 trial, a tip led to a search that uncovered previously undisclosed material, much of it in the desk or cabinet of the lead detective, Steven Katz.

The [July 6 mistrial order](https://www.cacd.uscourts.gov/sites/default/files/CV02-2929FMC.pdf) described interviews with a former cellmate of Perez. The informant attributed statements to Perez about his and Mack’s Death Row connections and their activities on the night of the murder. Judge Florence-Marie Cooper rejected Katz’s claim that he had forgotten the material and found deliberate concealment. She granted a mistrial and ordered fees and costs attributable to the misconduct. She declined the family’s request for a default judgment.

That ruling established a failure of disclosure, not the truth of the informant’s murder allegations. Its practical effect was substantial: the family had prepared and begun its trial without material it was entitled to examine. The city could not decide for its opponents that an informant was too unreliable to be useful.

The city subsequently argued that the family’s lawyers had already known about the informant and had misled the judge. [In June 2006, Cooper rejected deception by the lawyers and maintained her finding that the city had deliberately concealed documents](https://www.latimes.com/local/la-me-biggie10jun06-story.html).

The suit was [dismissed by agreement in April 2010, without prejudice to refiling](https://www.latimes.com/archives/la-xpm-2010-apr-20-la-me-0421-notorious-big-suit-20100421-story.html). It ended without a murder finding.

A civil trial and a criminal prosecution ask different questions and use different procedures. Disclosure can reveal a witness whose account later collapses, or a lead whose importance becomes clearer. A mistrial caused by concealed records means that the proceeding could not fairly continue as it stood. It leaves the underlying allegations to be examined. The family obtained a serious finding about the city’s conduct while still being denied an answer about who killed Wallace.

## An investigation can close without resolving the murder

The FBI opened a separate civil-rights investigation into Wallace’s killing. A January 18, 2005 closing memorandum records that federal prosecutors considered the evidence insufficient to proceed. The [memorandum appears near the end of the second released file](https://vault.fbi.gov/Christopher%20%28Biggie%20Smalls%29%20Wallace%20/Christopher%20%28Biggie%20Smalls%29%20Wallace%20Part%2002/at_download/file).

The federal case therefore closed before the discovery dispute disrupted the family’s trial that summer. The city’s conduct in that litigation would receive a judge’s condemnation while federal prosecutors had already decided they could not proceed. There was no single occasion on which all these questions were put before a jury.

A later task force led by Greg Kading developed a different account from Poole’s. Kading identified Wardell “Poochie” Fouse as Wallace’s killer and alleged that Knight had commissioned the attack. A source identified by the protective name “Theresa Swann” allegedly described passing Knight’s payment to Fouse. Those remain allegations about Wallace’s murder, without a conviction establishing them.

The interview began with a deception. In [Chris Vogel’s 2011 reporting](https://www.laweekly.com/cops-book-says-sean-combs-suge-knight-ordered-tupac-and-biggie-killings/), based on interviews and records he examined, detectives prepared a false confession attributed to Fouse. It implicated the woman herself. They presented it to her, after which she confirmed the account and described her alleged role. The interview was documented in investigators’ notes rather than an audio recording, according to Kading.

A false confession used as an interview tactic introduces information into the encounter. Afterward, investigators have to distinguish what the witness independently supplies from what they have already told her. Details volunteered before a prompt, facts unknown to the interviewer, or a payment independently traced would have a different value from agreement with an accusation placed on the table. That problem belongs inside an assessment of the account, alongside the reasons Kading found it persuasive.

In an [interview about the investigations](https://graziamagazine.com/articles/biggie-tupac-expose-interview-greg-kading/), Kading described believing the cases solved and then being removed from the work. He criticized a department he thought had lost interest after its exposure to the civil lawsuit diminished. Poole and Kading accused different people, but both came away believing their department had failed the investigation.

The commander who supervised the task force, Kevin McClure, gave Vogel a different explanation for ending its work. He said investigators had pursued viable leads and still needed corroboration that never arrived. Other cases were competing for the same detectives. Kading saw an institution abandoning a solution; McClure described an investigation that had exhausted its available leads. Their disagreement concerns both the quality of the case and a decision about resources.

By Davis’s arrest in 2023, Fouse and the other three alleged occupants of the Cadillac were dead. Dead suspects cannot be interviewed again or tried. Their deaths narrowed the possibilities for prosecution while also making claims about their conduct harder to challenge. Davis’s later trial shows why that fact did not make accountability for a surviving alleged participant impossible. It still took a separate case against him.

## The political history and the file actually released

Shakur’s mother, Afeni, was a Black Panther. The political history of his family helps explain why some listeners heard his killing through the history of [[cointelpro]], rather than through the entertainment-page account of a rap feud. His music addressed policing, poverty, family, and the pressures on young Black men. The distance between the anger of “Trapped” and the tenderness of “Dear Mama” is part of his work, not a difficulty to be edited out of his biography. Both appear in the [Rock Hall’s account of his career](https://rockhall.com/inductees/tupac-shakur/).

The person singing about his mother, the man who believed former friends had betrayed him, and the participant in the MGM assault were the same person. Understanding the last event does not erase the others. It does, however, place a choice and its consequences in the account of his death. Treating every hostile act around a political artist as someone else’s manipulation can remove his own decisions from the story.

The [released Tupac file](https://vault.fbi.gov/Tupac%20Shakur%20/Tupac%20Shakur%20Part%2001%20%28Final%29/at_download/file) is 102 pages. It includes an inquiry opened in October 1996 into allegations that people associated with the Jewish Defense League had used death threats to extort rappers, including Shakur and Eazy-E. The proposed scheme involved threatening a musician and then selling protection from the threat. Shakur appears as a deceased alleged victim.

The file contains reports of source information, investigative correspondence, and collected material. It does not substantiate the claim of a 4,000-page political campaign against Shakur or demonstrate that the MGM confrontation was manufactured by intelligence officers. A file about threats to a person can become a file about surveillance of that person when its title travels without its contents. Wallace’s posthumous investigative file is vulnerable to the same transformation.

Davis’s conviction came after decades in which he repeatedly described his own involvement. The Wallace family’s litigation exposed concealed records and secured a finding about police conduct, but never established who committed the murder. The two cases now have different outcomes. A conviction in Las Vegas has not supplied the answer still owed in Los Angeles.
