George H. W. Bush gave Congress four concrete objectives on September 11, 1990. Iraq had to leave Kuwait. Kuwait's government had to be restored. The Persian Gulf had to be secure, and American citizens abroad had to be protected. Then he added a fifth: “a new world order.”

The phrase came inside an [address about the Gulf crisis and the federal budget](https://www.presidency.ucsb.edu/documents/address-before-joint-session-the-congress-the-persian-gulf-crisis-and-the-federal-budget). Bush was asking Americans to support a military deployment and an international response to Iraq's invasion. He described cooperation with Soviet leader Mikhail Gorbachev as a chance to make collective security work. He also defended American leadership and identified control of oil resources as a vital interest.

The speech joined a moral account of collective action to a defense of American interests. Bush wanted support for the power he was exercising. What kind of international order would that exercise of power create?

In New World Order conspiracy accounts, the phrase acquires a more specific meaning: a hidden network is directing governments toward a unified system of global control. International organizations, private meetings, financial influence and crises become parts of one continuing plan. To establish that account, we would need to trace how one authority coordinates those different activities.

## The same words can describe different projects

Bush had just met Gorbachev in Helsinki on September 9, two days before the address. The Soviet Union still existed. Bush saw an opportunity in cooperation between two powerful states whose rivalry had long obstructed it.

His speech envisioned a United Nations capable of acting against aggression. At the same time, the United States would lead. The combination invites a criticism: a universal language of law can lend legitimacy to the priorities of the state best placed to organize the response. A coalition may defend an international principle and preserve the leading power's interests at once. The oil interests he identified were stated openly.

There have also been explicit arguments for replacing the system of sovereign states. H. G. Wells's 1940 book [*The New World Order*](https://gutenberg.net.au/ebooks04/0400671h.html) is one. Wells thought modern warfare and economic interdependence had made the old organization of the world intolerable. He argued for common control of political and economic life, with a much deeper social transformation than an agreement among existing governments.

Wells criticized Clarence Streit's *Union Now*, a proposal to federate democracies, for leaving too much of the economic order intact. His project demanded change from private business as well as national governments. Calling both his proposal and Bush's coalition a new world order does not make their institutions or economic purposes identical.

A proposal for federation, a treaty negotiation and an authorized operation each leave a record of people doing particular things. Repetition of a phrase tells us where to search. To connect its users, we need to follow what they did.

## What the United Nations can make states do

The [UN Charter](https://www.archives.gov/milestone-documents/united-nations-charter) contains both sovereign equality and binding obligations. Article 2 affirms the sovereign equality of member states. Article 25 commits them to carrying out Security Council decisions made under the Charter. The General Assembly's power to debate and recommend is different from the Security Council's enforcement role.

The Council's five permanent members have a special protection. On substantive decisions, a negative vote from one of them can block adoption. An abstention is different: as the Council's [explanation of its voting system](https://main.un.org/securitycouncil/en/content/voting-system) makes clear, a permanent member may abstain and allow a resolution to pass if it receives the necessary nine affirmative votes.

Under Article 24, member states confer responsibility for international peace and security on the Council, which is to act on their behalf. They gain an institution through which collective action can be organized, while accepting decisions they may not individually favor. That is a real constraint on independent action. Its justification depends on what the cooperation achieves and how the power is controlled.

These arrangements make some international decisions possible and others very difficult. They preserve an inequality written into the public rules: exceptional power for a small group of states. Describing the UN as either powerless or an already functioning world government misses the particular bargain.

A critic can object to the special veto, or demand that a government account for its decisions when acting through a coalition. Someone affected by an enforcement measure needs a way to challenge how it is applied. These concerns identify the people exercising authority and the people who need protection from its misuse.

The broader conspiracy account often treats international constraint as evidence of a single destination: every transfer of authority advances the same global government. But authority can move to different bodies for different purposes. A treaty that binds its parties, a private policy forum and an institution empowered to impose sanctions do different kinds of work. Their connections matter when a person, document or decision links them, rather than simply because each operates across borders.

## A real route from a private council into government

Private organizations can influence policy before most citizens hear about a proposal. Their lack of formal governing power offers little reassurance if their members possess money, expertise and access to officials. Its consequences depend on how the access is used.

The Council on Foreign Relations' [1946 account of its War and Peace Studies](https://archive.org/details/CouncilOnForeignRelations1946TheWarAndPeaceStudies) describes a concrete pathway. In 1939, Council leaders offered research assistance to the State Department. The Rockefeller Foundation funded the project. When the department established a Division of Special Research in 1941, it engaged the Council groups' research secretaries, who continued working part-time with their Council groups. Several participants later joined the department's advisory work on postwar policy.

The report identifies a project, funding and overlapping personnel. People framing and investigating problems worked on both sides of the relationship. The Council's account is its own report of that relationship, rather than an independent audit of every resulting decision, but the relationship it describes is substantial.

The research arrangement also helps explain why influence can persist without a private organization issuing orders. Officials need people who can turn broad aims into workable options. If one network repeatedly provides that work, its assumptions may become the starting point of the official discussion. The questions it leaves unasked can be as consequential as the recommendations it submits.

Officials still have to answer for the choice they make. But judging that choice also requires knowing how the alternatives were developed and who had a chance to question them. Expertise can improve a decision while giving a narrow circle disproportionate influence over its terms.

The [[cfr-trilateral]] essay follows those relationships more closely. It also examines a difficulty for the claim that a shared roster supplies a shared command. Jimmy Carter and Secretary of State Cyrus Vance had [both participated in the Trilateral Commission](https://history.state.gov/departmenthistory/people/vance-cyrus-roberts). Vance nevertheless resigned over Carter's decision to attempt the rescue of the American hostages in Iran. Their [published exchange of letters](https://www.presidency.ucsb.edu/documents/department-state-exchange-letters-the-resignation-cyrus-r-vance-secretary) records a disagreement consequential enough to end his service.

Their shared affiliation did not settle that decision. To establish coordination in a particular case, we would want to know what participants agreed, who could act on the agreement, and what happened when someone refused. Sometimes there will be answers in correspondence, minutes, draft proposals, funding arrangements or instructions. Replacing those questions with a diagram of overlapping names can conceal the very exercise of power the diagram is meant to expose.

## How the explanation becomes too complete

Gary Allen, writing with Larry Abraham in [*None Dare Call It Conspiracy*](https://otwc.net/pub/nonedare/), presented readers with a stark choice. History was either accidental, or events happened because somebody planned them. His account cast a financial elite as the force behind apparently opposed political movements, with the Council on Foreign Relations among its instruments.

People can also plan deliberately and fail, compete, compromise, or produce results none of them intended. An economy or a war can reflect several organized efforts without being the work of one organizer. Rejecting a universal planner does not require believing that powerful people never plan.

A network may cooperate on one objective and split on another. Evidence that officials concealed one program can establish a concealed program. It cannot silently expand into evidence that the same people engineered every crisis, or that an organization remained under one command across generations.

A theory becomes especially difficult to examine when every possible result supports it. Agreement shows coordination; disagreement shows a staged conflict. Public documents reveal the plan; missing documents reveal how well it was hidden. Delay means resistance has been anticipated, while rapid action means implementation has begun. Under those rules, the proposed explanation never has to risk being wrong about a particular event.

A claim about a particular meeting can be checked against a particular decision. If a private meeting allegedly settled a policy, compare the proposal before and after it. Look for the decision-makers' commitments, the alternatives they rejected and the steps needed for implementation. A discussion followed by a similar policy may be a useful lead. The intermediate record determines whether the resemblance amounts to agreement, influence, coincidence or something else.

## A public plan to use a crisis

The World Economic Forum's Great Reset initiative made an ambitious public case for using a crisis to change policy. In a [June 2020 article](https://www.weforum.org/stories/2020/06/now-is-the-time-for-a-great-reset/), Klaus Schwab argued that the pandemic offered an opportunity for major economic change. The appeal was public and broad. He wanted stronger coordination of policy, investment directed toward social and environmental goals, and technological innovation used for public needs.

His proposals included changes to taxes, subsidies, intellectual property and competition rules, depending on the country. They deserve examination as proposals that would create winners, impose costs and redistribute influence. Publication on a forum used by business and political leaders gives such ideas an audience that most people cannot obtain.

Using an emergency to advance a larger program creates its own problem of consent. People coping with illness, lost work or emergency restrictions may have little capacity to contest a larger political program. A measure offered as temporary relief can be used to advance a longstanding preference. Urgency may compress deliberation just when a decision's consequences call for more of it.

But a proposal to exploit an opening is a different claim from having created the event that opened it. The article supplies evidence of what its author advocated during the pandemic. Establishing responsibility for the pandemic would require evidence about its origin and the acts alleged to have caused it. The scale of the proposed reform cannot fill that gap.

Nor does a promotional program become law simply because influential people support it. A policy still needs an institution able to adopt it, funding or enforcement where required, and people prepared to implement it. Following those steps is how we learn whether a slogan became a governing arrangement and whose interests that arrangement served.

The [[great-reset]] and [[schwab-wef]] essays address those proposals and networks in more detail. An investigation needs to follow the proposal from its advocates to the people empowered to act on it.

## Secrecy, evidence and accountability

Bilderberg's [own account of its meetings](https://www.bilderbergmeetings.org/frequently-asked-questions) says that participation is by invitation, the meetings are closed to reporting journalists, and no resolutions or votes are taken. Journalists can attend in an individual capacity. The organizers present these arrangements as conditions for candid discussion. They are describing their format and purpose; their account cannot independently establish everything participants do with what they hear.

A private gathering of people with public responsibilities raises a reasonable question about access. Who gets to test an idea with a minister before anyone else sees it? What obligations govern the minister's later decisions? The absence of a formal resolution does not dispose of those questions. Informal persuasion and relationships can matter without creating an enforceable instruction.

Secrecy also makes unreliable documents attractive. A text that appears to reveal hidden proceedings can seem to supply the evidence that ordinary investigation lacks. The forgery discussed in [[protocols-of-zion]] illustrates the danger. It falsely presented an antisemitic account of world domination as the record of secret Jewish meetings. In 1921, Philip Graves's reporting in *The Times* [demonstrated borrowing from Maurice Joly's earlier political satire](https://encyclopedia.ushmm.org/content/en/article/protocols-of-the-elders-of-zion). The supposed insider record was a fabrication.

The forgery makes the need to check a document's origin particularly plain. It also exposes the harm of turning criticism of a bank, donor or official into accusations against Jews as a group. Neither a surname nor a person's religion establishes a political relationship. A concrete allegation needs evidence of the person's conduct.

The wartime Council report gives us people to follow into State Department planning. Vance's letters preserve a disagreement that a shared membership list cannot explain. Those are different findings, and both make power easier to examine.

An account that connects every institution in advance risks losing both: the route by which a private group helped frame policy, and the point at which an official decided against someone from the same circle.
