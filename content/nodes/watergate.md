Six days after police arrested five men inside the Democratic National Committee’s headquarters, Richard Nixon’s chief of staff explained the problem to him. The FBI was following the money. Investigators had found a route from the burglars toward the president’s reelection campaign, and they were about to learn more.

On the recording of their 23 June 1972 conversation, H. R. Haldeman describes this as a failure of control. Acting FBI director L. Patrick Gray needed a reason to stop part of the inquiry. Haldeman proposed getting the CIA to provide it: tell the Bureau that further investigation might expose intelligence operations. Nixon approved the approach and instructed Haldeman to press the agency’s leaders.

The recording became known as the smoking gun because it placed Nixon inside the cover-up six days after the arrests. Investigators were tracing payments and deciding whom to interview; the president was approving a way to obstruct them. His knowledge of the original burglary remained a separate question.

The burglary, the effort to hide its sponsors and the eventual prosecution involved different people at different times. Intelligence officers supplied equipment and, later, an excuse for blocking interviews. A recording system intended for Nixon’s own use became evidence against him. Following those decisions explains how a campaign operation reached the presidency—and why its exposure ended with Nixon’s resignation but no criminal trial of Nixon himself.

## Before the arrests

The Watergate burglary belonged to an effort to collect political intelligence for Nixon’s reelection campaign. G. Gordon Liddy, a former FBI agent who became the campaign committee’s general counsel, developed the plan known as Gemstone. E. Howard Hunt, a retired CIA officer working with the White House, helped put it into operation.

The factual account in the appeals court’s *United States v. Haldeman* describes the proposals and the money behind them. Liddy’s initial plans were expensive and extravagant. After revisions, the program had a proposed budget of $250,000. The court’s account placed its approval at a March 1972 meeting involving campaign director John Mitchell, his deputy Jeb Magruder and aide Fred LaRue. Mitchell had recently left office as attorney general. The scheme was being considered by officials with experience at the highest levels of government.

The burglars entered the DNC offices in late May, before the better-known June break-in. According to the trial evidence summarized on appeal, the later mission included repairing a defective listening device after campaign officials complained about the intelligence they were receiving. On 17 June, police arrested James McCord, Bernard Barker, Eugenio Martinez, Virgilio Gonzalez and Frank Sturgis inside the headquarters. McCord was the reelection committee’s security officer.

The campaign had spent money on an intelligence operation and wanted usable intelligence in return. The defective bug and complaints about the first entry help explain why the team went back. Disputes about individual participants’ intentions remain, but the court’s account gives the June mission a purpose within the campaign’s own operation.

Hunt and Liddy had already worked together in the White House Special Investigations Unit, known as the Plumbers because its assignment was to stop leaks. The publication of the Pentagon Papers in 1971 had prompted the administration to look for ways to discredit Daniel Ellsberg, who had provided the documents to the press. In September, a team under Hunt and Liddy broke into the office of Ellsberg’s psychiatrist, Lewis Fielding.

The Rockefeller Commission later reconstructed that operation. The team forced open cabinets and searched for Ellsberg’s records; the report concluded that, despite conflicting testimony, apparently no Ellsberg file was found. The office was left in disorder to resemble a burglary for drugs. The men looking for leaks had moved into a psychiatrist’s office, hoping to find private information with which to discredit Ellsberg.

The connection to Watergate runs through people, methods and a willingness to break into an office for information. The White House unit and the campaign committee were different organizations, but Hunt and Liddy carried their experience between them. The investigation of a campaign burglary could therefore lead back to work done for the White House.

## The money created a second operation

After the arrests, officials had two related problems. They needed an explanation for why campaign money had reached Liddy and the burglars, and they needed the people in custody to remain silent. They could not do either by acting alone.

The [appeals court’s account](https://law.justia.com/cases/federal/appellate-courts/cadc/4104248/559-f2d-31.html) describes efforts to pass off Liddy’s funds as money for convention security and protection of campaign speakers. That explanation needed corroboration. Magruder rehearsed his account with other participants before giving false testimony to the grand jury. Calendars recording earlier meetings then needed explanations of their own. A lie about spending became a coordinated account of meetings, duties and decisions.

Records were destroyed as well. Sensitive Gemstone materials were removed from files. Documents were burned. Materials from Hunt’s White House safe went through John Dean, the president’s counsel, and some reached Gray with instructions that they should never become public. The people trying to contain the investigation were learning what investigators knew while deciding what evidence those investigators would be allowed to see.

Supporting the defendants was expensive. Liddy had made commitments concerning bail, lawyers and living expenses. Funds were gathered through political associates and delivered discreetly, including through a courier’s use of telephone booths and airport lockers. Ordinary legal assistance was being tied to the expectation that the defendants would protect people above them. Once silence became part of the arrangement, a new demand for money could threaten the entire group.

Hunt was especially difficult to contain. He knew about the earlier White House operations and did not promise the unconditional silence Liddy offered. Money alone would not settle his future. The possibility of executive clemency entered the discussions, adding another public power to the private bargaining.

By the time Nixon won reelection in November 1972, the first indictments had reached the burglars, Hunt and Liddy while leaving the senior participants outside the case. Containment had bought the campaign time. Yet winning the election did not end the defendants’ need for money or protection. Every promise made to keep one person quiet gave that person a claim on the others. A participant could threaten disclosure, seek a shorter sentence or begin cooperating, changing the risks for everyone still defending the agreed story.

On 21 March 1973, Dean warned Nixon that the demands were growing into a threat to the presidency. He estimated that future payments might reach a million dollars. Nixon discussed obtaining the cash and meeting Hunt’s immediate demand to gain time. The conversation later became evidence that could be compared with testimony about what the president had said.

That comparison mattered. Haldeman testified that Nixon had followed discussion of obtaining the money with the objection that it would be wrong. The appeals court pointed out that the recording placed the objection many minutes later, during a discussion about clemency. Moving the objection beside the discussion of money made Nixon’s response look more favorable. With the tape, the jury could hear where he had actually objected.

## What the June 23 tape shows

In the earlier [June 23 transcript](https://www.nixonlibrary.gov/sites/default/files/forresearchers/find/tapes/watergate/trial/exhibit_01.pdf), the proposed intervention takes shape as Haldeman explains what the FBI is finding.

Haldeman explains that investigators have traced a $25,000 contribution associated with Ken Dahlberg into Barker’s hands, and are following other funds through a Mexican bank. Nixon asks who Dahlberg is and considers what the contributors might say. Haldeman warns that relying on more people creates more risk. His alternative is to have the CIA give Gray a basis for stopping that part of the investigation.

The transcript shows them discussing the benefit of the FBI’s suspicion that the burglary might be a CIA operation. They want that suspicion to divert attention from the campaign. Nixon tells Haldeman and domestic-affairs adviser John Ehrlichman to bring in CIA director Richard Helms and deputy director Vernon Walters. He suggests invoking Hunt, the Cuban participants and the possibility of reopening the Bay of Pigs affair.

The reference to the “whole Bay of Pigs thing” has attracted interpretations involving deeper secrets, including the Kennedy assassination. The phrase alone cannot tell us which older secrets each participant had in mind. In this conversation, Nixon wanted to use the prospect of exposure to persuade the CIA to block the FBI’s inquiry into campaign money.

There was a real history available for him to invoke. Hunt had worked on the CIA’s Cuban operations, including political preparations around the [[bay-of-pigs]]. Nixon’s White House had also sought classified files about that episode and other Kennedy-era decisions for potential political use. Nixon was now invoking intelligence history to enlist the agency in protecting his own campaign.

The plan had an effect, though it did not stop the inquiry permanently. Walters passed the warning about Mexico to Gray. By early July, Gray wanted a written direction if the interviews were to remain blocked. Walters instead supplied a statement that the CIA had no interest in the matter, and the interviews proceeded. The warning worked while it remained an oral claim about secret operations. Gray’s demand for a written instruction forced Walters to state the agency’s actual position. For roughly two weeks, an intelligence pretext had delayed interviews in a criminal investigation.

## The CIA connection was more than a résumé

Hunt could still obtain help from his old agency after he left it. The assistance reached beyond advice from former colleagues.

In 1971, White House officials obtained CIA assistance for him. It included disguise materials, false identification, a recorder, a camera and film processing. Hunt and Liddy used agency-supplied equipment while reconnoitering Fielding’s office. The agency also prepared psychological profiles of Ellsberg at White House request. The equipment helped them photograph the office and conceal their identities during reconnaissance.

The [Rockefeller Commission’s report](https://www.fordlibrarymuseum.gov/library/exhibits/intelligence/RockComm_Chap14_InvolveWH.pdf) found that this assistance exceeded the CIA’s authorized foreign-intelligence functions. It criticized the failure to insist on ordinary controls. A sensitive request backed by high officials had caused staff to relax the very procedures that should have established what the materials were for and how they would be accounted for.

Some people inside the agency became uneasy. Hunt’s requests expanded, an unfamiliar associate appeared, and materials were not promptly returned. In August 1971, further technical assistance was cut off. The commission found no evidence that the CIA knew in advance about the Fielding or Watergate burglaries. Its criticism concerned the services the agency had supplied and the weak controls that allowed them.

Martinez’s position adds another complication. He had worked in maritime operations against Cuba and remained a paid CIA informant when he joined Hunt’s activities. His $100 monthly retainer required reports on developments in the Cuban community. According to the commission’s account, an unsuccessful check on Hunt’s White House employment led Martinez to infer that Hunt must be carrying out a secret mission beyond the local station’s knowledge. He therefore withheld his activities with Hunt from his case officer.

Martinez understood his relationship with Hunt through his continuing CIA work. Yet he was concealing the activities from the case officer who paid him. His belief in a secret mission explains something about his participation; it does not supply the missing authorization from the agency.

The agency’s response after Watergate was also seriously deficient. It delayed disclosing relevant information and did not conduct a comprehensive internal inquiry until May 1973. The report described a package of materials, including information about assistance to Hunt, held back after a December 1972 meeting with White House officials. It reached the Justice Department in January.

Helms’s office records presented a problem that could never be fully repaired. As he prepared to leave the agency in January 1973, tapes, transcripts and their identifying logs were destroyed after a Senate request to preserve Watergate-related material. Helms described the disposal as routine and said he considered the records irrelevant. The commission criticized both the destruction and that reassurance: later discoveries might have made material relevant that an earlier cursory review had failed to recognize. The destroyed contents could no longer be checked.

The commission nevertheless did not find agency participation in the White House cover-up conspiracy. Its investigation also had limits: it was unable to obtain all the Nixon White House papers it sought after his counsel objected. Its conclusions should be read with that limitation and with its own account of missing and delayed evidence. They support scrutiny of specific CIA conduct without establishing that the agency directed the entire affair or arranged Nixon’s removal.

## Several investigations, different powers

The burglary investigation survived the first efforts to contain it. Criminal proceedings initially reached the five arrested men, Hunt and Liddy. Five pleaded guilty and two were convicted at trial. Judge John Sirica doubted that the account was complete. As sentencing approached, McCord challenged the story that the defendants had acted without pressure from higher officials.

The Senate’s investigation gave witnesses another forum. Its Select Committee on Presidential Campaign Activities, chaired by Sam Ervin, could examine the broader operation and publicly question officials. Dean described Nixon’s involvement in the cover-up. Alexander Butterfield disclosed the White House recording system. The tapes offered a way to test testimony against conversations made before the participants knew they would have to defend themselves.

Reporting helped sustain attention and identify connections. Bob Woodward and Carl Bernstein’s work for the *Washington Post* was important, and the identity of their source Deep Throat became part of the story. In 2005, former FBI deputy director Mark Felt acknowledged that role. He was providing information from inside an institution whose investigation the White House was trying to contain. In the June 23 conversation, Haldeman had expected Felt to cooperate, describing him as ambitious. He had misjudged how much help the White House could expect from inside the Bureau.

Felt’s position gave him access to investigative information and an interest in how the Bureau was being treated. Readers have reason to ask what such a source chooses to disclose. But the case also came to rest on bank records, testimony and recordings that courts could examine independently of his tips. Reporting helped open the story; it was only part of the eventual evidence.

The struggle over access was prolonged. Nixon resisted requests and subpoenas for the tapes. In October 1973, the attempt to remove special prosecutor Archibald Cox brought resignations at the Justice Department and Cox’s dismissal, carried out by Solicitor General Robert Bork. A successor prosecutor, Leon Jaworski, continued pursuing the records. The prosecutor had been dismissed, but his demand for the evidence survived him.

The Senate did not win every confrontation. Its own lawsuit for access to presidential material failed. The Supreme Court case that ultimately required production arose from Jaworski’s demand for evidence in a criminal prosecution. The committee could make testimony public and propose legislation. To obtain these tapes, the successful demand rested on the needs of a particular criminal trial.

## A privilege with a limit

In [*United States v. Nixon*](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/418/683/), decided on 24 July 1974, the Supreme Court rejected an absolute presidential claim to keep the requested conversations from judicial examination. All eight participating justices agreed; William Rehnquist took no part.

The decision recognized an interest in confidential presidential communications. It weighed that interest against the demonstrated need for particular evidence in a pending criminal trial. A general appeal to confidentiality could not defeat that need. The district judge would examine the requested material privately and protect irrelevant or inadmissible conversations. The ruling required production for that judicial examination, with safeguards for presidential confidentiality.

Nixon had lost the power to decide by himself which relevant conversations the prosecution could inspect. The recording system that had served his private purposes had produced evidence beyond his control.

By late July, the House Judiciary Committee had voted to recommend three articles of impeachment: obstruction of justice, abuse of power and failure to comply with its subpoenas. The full House had not yet voted. Impeachment and a Senate trial were still prospective when the June 23 conversations became public on 5 August.

Nixon’s accompanying statement acknowledged that the tapes conflicted with parts of his previous account. He admitted that he had understood the political advantage of limiting exposure of people connected with the reelection committee. He still argued that his overall conduct did not justify removal. His remaining political support could not survive the disclosure. He announced his departure on 8 August, and the resignation took effect the following day.

## The judgment that never came

Resignation settled who would occupy the presidency. It did not resolve whether Nixon would face a criminal trial. A [memorandum to Jaworski dated 9 August](https://www.archives.gov/education/lessons/watergate-constitution/memo-transcript) laid out arguments on both sides. Staff attorneys pointed to clear evidence of obstruction and the principle that a former president remained subject to law. They also considered whether resignation was sufficient punishment, whether prosecution would prolong political division and whether a fair trial could be secured after so much publicity.

The strongest argument for prosecution concerned unequal treatment. Aides who had acted on presidential instructions or in what they understood to be Nixon’s interests were facing criminal responsibility. Allowing him to surrender the office in exchange for escaping that process would leave the person at the top in a different position from those below him.

Gerald Ford chose a different resolution. On 8 September, his proclamation pardoned Nixon for federal offenses committed or potentially committed during his presidency. Ford argued that a trial could prolong national conflict after the resignation. The pardon prevented federal prosecution for the covered conduct. It supplied neither a trial verdict nor an acquittal.

Other defendants did face judgment. Mitchell, Haldeman and Ehrlichman were convicted and served prison time. Their appeals produced the detailed judicial account used throughout this essay. Exposure therefore had real consequences, but they were distributed unevenly. The president’s departure and the punishment of his aides did not produce a criminal judgment on his own conduct.

The investigations also exposed weaknesses beyond one campaign: misuse of intelligence resources, poor controls over White House requests, resistance to disclosure and the loss of records. The Rockefeller Commission’s proposed remedies included a single accountable channel for White House demands on the CIA and instructions to report requests arriving outside it. They sought to prevent officials from treating a presidential aide’s request as sufficient authority to provide clandestine help.

The records survived long enough for investigators to test what senior officials had said. Witnesses broke with earlier accounts, prosecutors kept demanding evidence and courts enforced those demands. That work brought the cover-up into view and helped end a presidency. Ford’s pardon then stopped the criminal process at the person who had held the greatest power. Nixon’s aides went to prison; the president who approved the obstruction never had to answer the prosecution in court.
