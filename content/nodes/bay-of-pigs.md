By the evening of April 18, 1961, ammunition was running short at the Bay of Pigs. A supply ship had finally returned from far out at sea. Its cargo was transferred into smaller vessels for a run to the Cuban beachhead, where an American-backed exile brigade was fighting Castro’s forces. The convoy could not arrive before daylight. Its commander wanted American destroyer protection and jet cover; without them, he expected to lose the ships.

CIA headquarters stopped the convoy. Officials believed a request for that protection would be refused, and they overestimated what supplies the brigade already had. The [post-invasion inquiry](https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1961-63v10/d232) found that President John F. Kennedy was not asked to approve air cover for this particular ammunition run. The next day the beachhead collapsed.

The decision became overshadowed by the argument over who had betrayed the brigade. Kennedy had cancelled a planned dawn air strike. CIA officers had presented an invasion plan whose chances depended on destroying Cuba’s surviving aircraft. The brigade had been told it could achieve a military success, while senior officials had been reassured that it could escape into guerrilla warfare if things went wrong. That escape was unavailable when the men needed it.

The invasion failed through a combination of political restrictions, bad planning, and decisions made with an inadequate picture of the battle. The records show how those problems combined on the beach and in Washington.

## A guerrilla project becomes an invasion

Eisenhower approved a program of covert action against Fidel Castro on March 17, 1960. Castro’s revolution had overthrown Fulgencio Batista the previous year, and Washington increasingly regarded the new government as a hostile power moving toward the Soviet bloc. The American program sought to replace it while concealing the United States’ role.

The initial program combined an exile political organization, propaganda, an intelligence and action network inside Cuba, and a force trained abroad. Its paramilitary component was meant to supply cadres who would organize and lead resistance within the island. It did not begin as an authorization for the amphibious invasion eventually launched in April 1961.

During 1960, the project changed. Attempts to build and supply an internal resistance were disappointing. Castro’s government consolidated its control and received more weapons from communist countries. CIA planners began developing a force that could seize a position on the coast, hold it, and attract opponents of the government. A landing would provide the visible success around which resistance might gather.

The political plan went further. An exile government would enter the secured area and claim to speak for Cuba. Recognition by the United States could then provide a basis for open logistical support. The operation needed its first military success to create the political conditions for the next stage. A beachhead that survived briefly without attracting support would leave the central objective unmet.

By November, the training program was turning from guerrilla preparation to conventional assault. Kennedy’s subsequent study group, chaired by General Maxwell Taylor, found no evidence that the assault force continued receiving guerrilla training. Later assurances that the men could become guerrillas took little account of the training they had actually received.

Kennedy inherited the project when he became President in January 1961, but he made the decision to use it. Allen Dulles headed the CIA; Richard Bissell, its deputy director for plans, held central responsibility; J. D. Esterline ran the project, with Colonel Jack Hawkins directing its paramilitary work. American military personnel helped train the Cuban volunteers in Guatemala. Aircraft and ships were assembled for an operation whose scale made concealment increasingly implausible.

The force became Brigade 2506, numbering roughly 1,400 men at the invasion. They were volunteers committed to removing Castro, with varied backgrounds and reasons for joining. American officers could provide training and weapons. The volunteers depended on those officers for a plan as well as equipment.

The exile leadership was also being assembled under American supervision. The stated aim was a broad opposition, excluding both communists and Batista’s followers. Some leaders had supported Castro’s revolution before breaking with him. Their disagreements concerned the government they hoped to create as well as who should lead it.

As military planning took over, the leaders lost access to the brigade. At the invasion, according to the later survey by CIA inspector general Lyman Kirkpatrick, members of the Revolutionary Council waited in a Miami safe house, strongly persuaded to remain there, while agency-written bulletins went out in their name. Men intended to govern after a Cuban liberation had little say in the operation being conducted for them. Bissell later defended the separation on grounds of security and military unity; Kirkpatrick believed it had weakened both Cuban participation and the claim that the project was Cuban-led.

The existence of the brigade itself began to influence the decision. If it was disbanded, men would return to Miami with a story of American abandonment. Officials also worried that Castro was becoming stronger while the trained force could not be held together indefinitely. Using the brigade began to seem easier than explaining why it had been disbanded.

## Finding a quieter beach

The first major landing plan centered on Trinidad, on Cuba’s southern coast. It offered access to a populated area, an airfield, and the nearby Escambray Mountains, where anti-Castro resistance existed. Those mountains gave some substance to a possible retreat into insurgency.

Kennedy considered the plan too conspicuous. At a March 11 meeting, he asked for an operation that looked less like an American military assault: a quieter landing, preferably at night. The State Department also wanted an airfield within the beachhead so that supporting aircraft could supposedly be operating from Cuban territory.

The CIA produced alternatives, and the Zapata area around the Bay of Pigs became the choice. Playa Larga and Playa Girón lay beside swamp country, with a limited number of roads across it. There was an airstrip. The site’s isolation could initially help the invaders: approaches were restricted, and the landing would not begin in a large town under public observation.

The same geography made recovery from failure difficult. The Escambray Mountains were far away, beyond territory the brigade did not control. A force pushed back toward the sea could not readily disperse into friendly mountain positions. It could be trapped between the coast, the swamps, and government troops on the roads.

The Joint Chiefs preferred Zapata to the other new alternatives, while still considering Trinidad more promising. Their preference for Trinidad did not reach all the senior civilians clearly. As Zapata changed, the Chiefs examined parts of it, but did not conduct a full review of its final form before the landing. Their participation nevertheless gave others the impression of military approval.

There was a similar gap over the fallback. Senior officials were repeatedly told that the Zapata area was guerrilla country and that the force could change its mission if necessary. The Taylor inquiry later concluded that the guerrilla alternative, as presented, was unavailable in the circumstances that developed. Officials accepted the fallback even after the landing had moved far from the mountains.

## An air force that had to look Cuban

The operation needed to neutralize Castro’s combat aircraft. The slow transport ships would otherwise be exposed while unloading, and the brigade’s bombers would struggle to support troops under attack from faster aircraft. Hawkins had warned in January that sending the shipping in before Cuba’s opposing air and naval forces were knocked out would court disaster.

But the political restrictions shaped the air plan. The attackers used B-26 bombers, a type already operated by several countries, including Cuba. They were based far away in Nicaragua. American jet fighters flying from conspicuously American facilities would be harder to explain as part of a Cuban uprising.

On April 15, eight B-26s attacked Cuban airfields. The raids were also given a cover story: they were supposed to appear connected to defections from Castro’s air force. The story did not withstand scrutiny. It put American representatives, including UN ambassador Adlai Stevenson, in the position of defending an account that concealed the operation their government was conducting.

The damage was less complete than the initial pilot reports suggested. Cuban T-33 jets, Sea Fury fighters, and B-26s survived. A further attack on the morning of April 17 was meant to hit the remaining aircraft before they could strike the invasion force.

According to [Cabell’s account to the inquiry](https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1961-63v10/d108), late on April 16 McGeorge Bundy telephoned Charles Cabell, the CIA deputy director, with the decision that the dawn strikes should wait until they could operate from the beachhead’s airstrip. Cabell and Bissell went to Secretary of State Dean Rusk to argue against the restriction. They explained the dangers to the ships and troops, but Rusk came away believing the strikes important rather than indispensable.

Rusk offered them a direct telephone appeal to Kennedy. They declined. Later, Cabell pressed a separate request for protective air cover: Rusk telephoned the President and put Cabell on the line. That request was refused. Cabell had passed up a chance to challenge the cancelled strike directly, then sought different protection as the ships approached the coast.

The landing continued with surviving Cuban aircraft still able to contest it. Cabell said that by the late-night meeting it was physically too late to stop the landing. A change in a condition the planners considered essential had reached them when they believed the force was already committed.

## Getting ashore was only the beginning

The ships arrived during the night of April 16–17. At Playa Girón, the principal landing area, local militia detected the movement and raised the alarm. The brigade got men, vehicles, and tanks ashore and moved to block approaches. Parachutists were dropped inland. The attackers achieved enough surprise to establish positions, but Castro’s forces responded quickly.

At Playa Larga, landing boats suffered engine trouble. Only two of nine were usable for the trip from the *Houston*, according to the Taylor narrative. Unloading slowed. Much of the Fifth Battalion remained aboard when Cuban aircraft hit the ship, which was subsequently grounded away from the landing beach. Those men could not simply step into the battle their commanders had planned for them.

At Playa Girón, an air attack sank the *Río Escondido*, taking a large ammunition reserve and other supplies with it. The remaining freighters withdrew. They had been ordered to rendezvous offshore, but two continued much farther south and had to be intercepted by the US Navy. One never became available for resupply during the fighting.

The brigade’s ammunition was now divided between stores ashore, the sunken freighter, and ships withdrawing out to sea. The issue was getting it from a moving ship into small boats, across exposed water, and ashore while Cuban aircraft could attack.

Communications failed within the brigade as well. Portable radios got wet during the landing and stopped working. Commanders sent messages by courier. Equipment lost with the *Río Escondido* compounded the problem. Washington could exchange messages with its own command network without receiving a coherent account of what individual units were facing.

The exile force fought and inflicted losses, but faced government troops, militia, tanks, and artillery that the small beachhead could not indefinitely resist. Cuban defenders were killed as well as invaders. The operation also exposed opponents inside Cuba to the government’s intensified security measures. Those losses and arrests extended well beyond the Americans’ argument over responsibility.

Air attacks continued after the cancelled dawn mission. Brigade B-26s struck government forces, and attempts were made to destroy surviving aircraft at night and deliver ammunition by air. Some missions helped; others failed to reach or supply their targets. The distance from Nicaragua, aircraft losses, and exhaustion reduced what the air crews could sustain.

## The supply run that stopped

By April 18, the situation required more than intermittent support. The brigade had withdrawn from Playa Larga toward Playa Girón, its perimeter was contracting, and supplies of important ammunition were running low. Yet officials in Washington still worked from assumptions about what had been landed and what the air drops had supplied.

The freighter *Atlántico* returned to the offshore rendezvous late that afternoon. Its cargo was transferred to smaller landing vessels for another resupply attempt. By the time they started north, they could not arrive and unload under darkness. The convoy’s warnings about air attack and its crews’ refusal to proceed without protection forced another decision.

Headquarters called the run off. The Taylor study found that officials assumed it was hopeless to seek the necessary American support and did not ask the President for this particular authorization. Subordinates anticipated Kennedy’s refusal without fully understanding the brigade’s position.

A separate request did receive approval: a one-hour period of Navy fighter cover over the beachhead on the morning of April 19. It was intended to protect supporting bombers and transports. The permission covered a short protective mission; the larger restrictions remained.

The Taylor narrative records that the B-26s arrived almost an hour early for the cover, for a reason the inquiry had not determined. Two were lost. Four Americans flying for the CIA—Pete Ray, Leo Baker, Riley Shamburger, and Wade Gray—died on the final day. Their involvement was another departure from the original rule that Americans would not fight.

The brigade commander, José Pérez San Román, eventually reported that he had nothing left with which to fight and was destroying equipment. Resistance ended on April 19. Most of the force was taken prisoner. The fallback into guerrilla warfare had not saved it, and the limited American rescue efforts did not evacuate the brigade.

## What the investigations disagreed about

Kennedy commissioned a study group chaired by General Maxwell Taylor, with Robert Kennedy, Admiral Arleigh Burke, and Allen Dulles. Its June memoranda followed the operation from the original program through the battle and examined the decisions surrounding air support and resupply. Dulles’s participation meant the inquiry included the head of the agency whose work it was assessing.

The group identified ammunition shortage as the immediate cause of defeat, with surviving Cuban aircraft central to the loss of supply ships and difficulty of resupply. It also criticized the way the government managed the operation: changing restrictions, inadequate communication of military consequences, piecemeal military review, and a misleading guerrilla fallback.

Its conclusions did not guarantee victory if the dawn strikes had flown. The narrative judged that even with air control the brigade’s eventual success was very unlikely without substantial help from Cubans or overt American assistance. Better air support might have permitted evacuation. Keeping a force supplied and overthrowing a government were different objectives.

Kirkpatrick’s separate investigation reached a different assessment. He argued that the agency had converted a covert project into an overt military effort beyond its capabilities, failed to assess the conditions realistically, and failed to keep policymakers adequately informed. He also criticized intelligence on Castro’s strength and the opposition, staffing, divided command, and relations with Cuban political leaders.

Kirkpatrick’s survey explicitly limited itself to agency performance, leaving decisions by officials outside the CIA unassessed. The Taylor group had a broader remit and disagreed with Kirkpatrick’s assessment that intelligence failure was a major cause.

Bissell disputed much of Kirkpatrick’s account. In a [January 1962 response](https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1961-63v10-12mSupp/d278d), he defended the organization and argued that policy restrictions and the cancelled strike had undermined a plan with a reasonable chance of initial success. Yet he conceded a central weakness: the plan depended heavily on eliminating Cuban air power, and even authorization to strike could not guarantee that result.

The plan had made the destruction of Cuba’s aircraft essential to its supply system. If that failed, protecting the brigade would require American combat beyond the limits under which it had been approved.

## The objective survived the failure

The CIA’s leadership changed in the aftermath: Dulles, Cabell, and Bissell left their positions. Kennedy also sought to change how paramilitary operations were assessed and assigned. [National Security Action Memorandum 57](https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1961-63v08/d34), issued in June 1961, made large operations requiring substantial military resources primarily a Defense Department responsibility, while leaving wholly covert operations within CIA capabilities assignable to the agency.

Covert action continued under the new rules. The administration approved further activity against Castro, and Operation Mongoose brought sabotage, intelligence, political action, and military contingency planning under renewed direction. Kennedy’s conflict with CIA leaders did not mean he had abandoned their principal Cuban objective.

The March 1962 Mongoose guidelines expose a continuing tension. They anticipated that final success would require American military intervention, while holding back actions likely to precipitate a revolt requiring that intervention immediately. A covering record of Kennedy’s discussion says he remained skeptical that circumstances would justify overt American action and had not approved it. The gap between the desired overthrow and the means actually authorized remained.

The brigade’s prisoners returned in December 1962 after negotiations led by attorney James Donovan. The agreement exchanged 1,113 remaining invasion prisoners for $53 million in food and medicine. Kennedy greeted members of the brigade at Miami’s Orange Bowl and promised that their flag would be returned in a free Havana. The public commitment to their cause outlived the operation that had failed them.

The President’s tribute could not resolve the grievance created by the landing. The brigade had fought under a program controlled by a government powerful enough to invade Cuba and unwilling to turn the landing into an American war. Officials had their reasons for limiting intervention, but the men they had trained bore the consequences when the promised smaller operation failed. Their return and the continuing campaign against Castro kept that contradiction in American politics.

The force had been sent ashore on the understanding that it could succeed without drawing the United States into open war. By the final night, keeping it supplied required protection beyond what its sponsors had authorized. The returning freighter had ammunition. Getting it to the men on the beach required a decision that headquarters assumed the President would reject—and did not put to him.
