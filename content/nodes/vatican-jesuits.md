In 1773, Pope Clement XIV ordered the suppression of the Society of Jesus. In 1814, Pope Pius VII restored it. Between those decisions, Jesuit life continued in part of the Russian Empire because Catherine II refused to allow the suppression decree to be promulgated there.

The episode is difficult to fit into a picture of the Jesuits secretly commanding every pope and government. Papal authority could threaten the order's existence; a non-Catholic ruler could obstruct the execution of that authority. What happened depended on several institutions with different powers.

The combined name “the Vatican and the Jesuits” joins several kinds of authority. Following an instruction from the person who issued it to the people carrying it out can show where those authorities meet, and where they part.

## Several institutions in one name

The Catholic Church is a worldwide religious community. The Holy See refers to its central authority, headed by the pope, and has its own international legal personality. Vatican City is the territorial state created through the Lateran settlement of 1929. The Holy See [maintained diplomatic relations before that state existed](https://www.vatican.va/roman_curia/secretariat_state/documents/informazione_generale/sp_ss_scv_info-generale_en.html).

The [official account of Vatican City](https://www.vaticanstate.va/en/state-and-government/general-informations.html) distinguishes its sovereignty from that of the Holy See. The small state provides a territorial basis for the independence of the Church's central government. A diplomatic relationship with the Holy See is therefore not simply a relationship with everyone who works inside Vatican City's walls.

The Roman Curia consists of the bodies assisting the pope in governing the Church. [Canon 360](https://www.vatican.va/archive/cod-iuris-canonici/eng/documents/cic_lib2-cann330-367_en.html) describes its service as performed in his name and by his authority. The Society of Jesus is a religious order within the Church, with its own rules, members, superiors and governing assembly. It is not another name for the Curia.

An official's appointment, an asset's ownership and a superior's authority over a member of the order can lead to different offices. A Catholic connection does not make those relationships interchangeable.

The Istituto per le Opere di Religione, usually called the Vatican Bank or IOR, supplies another example. It provides financial services to Catholic institutions and people serving the Church. [In its own description](https://www.ior.va/en/faq-en/), it serves the Curia but is not part of it. A religious order can be a client of an institution without governing it.

People and responsibilities cross between the institutions, with the pope in a central position. The relationship still matters: a vow, an employment appointment, a bank account and a diplomatic mandate authorize different actions.

## What the Jesuit promise requires

The Society received papal approval in 1540. Its members undertook religious life through poverty, chastity and obedience, and its work developed through missions and education. Its existence, institutions and governing texts are public. The claim that it is a secret society needs more than the observation that some of its deliberations are private.

The order is associated with a fourth vow of special obedience to the pope. The [Constitutions](https://documenta.jesuits.global/docs/C/5/), however, specify that this concerns missions. They also distinguish categories of membership, including members who do not make that fourth vow. It is misleading to describe every Jesuit as having promised unlimited obedience on every possible political question.

The ordinary demand for obedience is nevertheless stringent. In Part VI, the Constitutions ask for more than outward performance of an instruction: they seek agreement in will and judgment. Their famous image compares the obedient member to a lifeless body moved through a superior's agency. The text also limits the command to matters in which sin is not judged to be present.

The limit concerning sin leaves a demanding form of obedience. A member trained to regard a superior's direction as spiritually authoritative may experience disagreement as more than an employment dispute. The organization is seeking a form of commitment that reaches into how the person understands his own judgment.

The practical questions concern a member's ability to report, contest or refuse an instruction, and the way a superior's decision is reviewed. Those procedures determine how the demanding language bears on a person's life.

The Superior General directs the order, but a [General Congregation is its highest governing authority](https://www.jesuits.org/glossary/general-congregation/). The congregation brings together representatives to elect a general and to decide major questions. This is an internal system of government, not an office above the pope governing the whole Church.

A Jesuit who becomes a bishop also occupies a different legal position from a member in an ordinary assignment. [Canon 705](https://press.vatican.va/archive/cod-iuris-canonici/eng/documents/cic_lib2-cann607-709_en.html) says that a religious raised to the episcopate remains a member of his institute, but is subject to the Roman Pontiff alone by virtue of the vow of obedience. A religious identity should not be mistaken for an unchanged chain of command through every new office.

Francis, elected in 2013, was the first Jesuit pope. His election did not make the Superior General his superior. He died on April 21, 2025. His successor, [Leo XIV, elected on May 8, 2025](https://www.vatican.va/content/leo-xiv/en/biography/documents/biografia_leone-xiv.html), came from the Augustinian order. The papacy did not become a permanent possession of the Society of Jesus in 2013.

## Suppression and the reach of a command

By the eighteenth century, conflict between the Society and European monarchies had led to expulsions from several territories. Clement XIV's 1773 brief, *Dominus ac Redemptor*, extended suppression through papal authority. It was a blow to an order closely identified with service to the pope.

The [Jesuits' institutional history](https://www.jesuits.org/about-us/ignatius-of-loyola/) attributes the decision to pressure from European rulers. The order remembers the episode as persecution. The command to suppress it nevertheless came from a pope.

Implementation was another matter. The first partition of Poland in 1772 had placed Catholic communities and Jesuit institutions under Catherine II. [Inglot's conference summary](https://jesuitonlinebibliography.bc.edu/catalog/15301) identifies 201 Jesuits in eighteen residences within the empire. The next year, Catherine prevented promulgation of the suppression decree in her realm.

Their survival did not rest simply on ignoring a pope. Inglot describes later papal acts that tolerated and then approved their position. A community could preserve its work under a ruler's protection while still needing to resolve its standing within the Church. These were different relationships, altered through particular decisions.

A decree can be extensive in its terms and uneven in its effect. Local authorities have to communicate it, transfer responsibilities and deal with buildings, people and schools. A ruler may resist losing useful teachers. A bishop or official may face demands from more than one authority. The distance between issuing and enforcing an instruction is part of the institution's history.

Pius VII's restoration in 1814 allowed work to resume beyond the places in which continuity had survived. The history contains real disruption as well as survival.

The pope could suppress an order identified with obedience to him, while the decree's reception depended on rulers with their own interests. The future of a Jesuit community could turn on the place in which it happened to be.

## A decision made at other people's expense

The Maryland Jesuits' sale of enslaved people in 1838 shows a different relationship between an instruction and its consequences.

Georgetown's [2016 working-group report](https://eloncdn.blob.core.windows.net/eu3/sites/922/2019/10/Georgetown-2016.pdf) describes a dispute among Jesuits over plantations and financial difficulty. Some wanted to retain enslaved labor, some wanted to sell, and a smaller group favored forms of emancipation. Jesuit authorities in Rome eventually permitted a sale under conditions: families were not to be separated, religious practice was to be protected, and proceeds were to support endowment rather than operating expenses or debt. The report says none of these conditions was fulfilled.

Conditional permission still mattered. Rome had authorized treating people as property to be sold. The failure to respect the conditions added wrongdoing; it did not turn the permission itself into a decision to free them. An account concerned only with whether subordinates obeyed would miss the people whose lives the authorization placed at risk.

The June 1838 agreement began with Isaac, described as sixty-five years old, and named family members including his daughter Nelly. Some younger people's precise relationships were less clear. This was a family entered into a financial instrument. Their futures were being decided by people negotiating the institution's finances.

The sum agreed was $115,000, payable over ten years. The working group found that most of the down payment went toward the college's debt. It also acknowledged that gaps in the records made tracing all later proceeds difficult. The debt relief could be established even where the later accounting remained incomplete.

People covered by the sale were sent into a slaveholding economy in Louisiana. They could be sold again, and their families had to live with decisions from which they had been excluded. The university's continuing educational work did not make that cost disappear. Preserving an institution and respecting the people made to support it had come into direct conflict.

The familiar label GU272 comes from the number recorded in sale-related documents. The independent [GU272 Memory Project](https://gu272.americanancestors.org/frequently-asked-questions) reports that genealogical examination identifies at least 314 people affected by sales extending from 1838 to 1843. The label preserves a history; it should not be treated as an exact census of everyone whose life was changed.

The contract was written to accomplish a sale, recording the sellers' and buyers' purposes. Family reconstruction can connect people across documents, show relationships the transaction reduced to names and ages, and identify descendants whom an institutional account might leave out.

The report records disagreement and an unfulfilled proposal for emancipation. It also identifies people with enough authority to proceed with the sale. Recording the dissent makes the alternatives visible, while leaving the decision and its consequences in view.

## Who participates in the reckoning?

[Georgetown's library timeline](https://library.georgetown.edu/slavery-memory-reconciliation/university-initiatives-events) records later scholarship, student activity and descendant intervention. The history had been discussed long before the public attention of 2016; it was not wholly unknown until a single recent discovery. What changed included the audience, the people asking questions and the institutional response.

At a 2016 event, descendant Joseph Stewart called for the descendants to be included in reconciliation: “Nothing about us, without us.” That objection addressed more than the accuracy of an archive. It challenged the institution's right to decide by itself what an adequate response would be.

In 2017, the university and Jesuit representatives apologized, and campus buildings were dedicated to Isaac Hawkins and Anne Marie Becraft. The latter was a free Black woman who had established a school for Black girls. The choices changed which lives received a public place in the institution's memory.

A building name cannot restore the choices denied in 1838. It can direct attention toward them, and it can be judged alongside other commitments. The more demanding question concerns participation: whether descendants can influence the decisions made in the name of reconciliation, rather than appearing only as the audience for a completed announcement.

Descendants, researchers and students helped change what the institution had to confront. The hierarchy could respond to them, but it had not chosen the questions for them.

## An archive and a financial institution

[In 2019, Francis renamed the Vatican Secret Archive the Vatican Apostolic Archive](https://press.vatican.va/content/salastampa/en/bollettino/pubblico/2019/10/28/191028d.html). His explanation stated that *secretum* had denoted a private, separate archive under the pope's jurisdiction, while modern uses of “secret” had acquired a different implication.

Changing a name leaves the access rules and the documentary holdings to be examined. The old title alone, however, cannot establish a hidden intelligence command. A researcher needs to know which records are available, what is missing and what the surviving documents say.

The records can preserve more of the institution's decisions than of the lives affected by them. The Georgetown case makes this particularly clear: a sale agreement is evidence of a transaction, not a complete account of the lives it disrupted. An archive's authority does not give the institution exclusive authority over the meaning of its past.

The financial record has a comparable need for precision. The relationships examined in [[p2-banco-ambrosiano]] concern banks, offshore companies, officers and disputed obligations. Calling them simply a Vatican operation can conceal which promise was made by which body. Calling them a Jesuit operation requires a further connection to the order, beyond the fact that people involved were Catholic.

The separate investigation in [[pope-john-paul-i]] likewise cannot be settled by a general impression of institutional secrecy. A disputed announcement, a medical judgment and an allegation of murder involve different evidence. Institutional distrust can motivate an investigation; it cannot supply the missing act.

## Where responsibility can be found

The Holy See, Vatican City, the Curia and the Jesuits can cooperate, disagree and protect institutional interests. Their formal distinctions do not guarantee good conduct. They help identify who can act and who should answer for it.

The suppression shows how an order's future could depend on papal authority and on a ruler's refusal to enforce it. The Maryland sale shows how authorization, conditions and consequences could diverge, with enslaved people paying the price. The later reckoning shows people outside the governing hierarchy demanding a role in the account and its response.

In 1838, permission came from Rome, negotiations took place in America and debt relief benefited the college. Isaac and the other people listed in the agreement had no corresponding authority over the decision. Their place in the record tells us what this exercise of institutional power cost.
