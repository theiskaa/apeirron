In a hangar on Long Island, investigators fitted pieces of a Boeing 747 back together. They knew where the pieces had been found on the seabed and could see how torn edges met. Some of the first pieces to leave the aircraft came from beneath the cabin floor, near the center fuel tank. Other large sections, including the forward fuselage, came off later. Putting the pieces back in their original positions helped investigators work out which parts of the airplane had failed before the fireball that people saw from shore.

TWA Flight 800 left New York’s Kennedy Airport for Paris on July 17, 1996. It crashed into the Atlantic near East Moriches, Long Island, about twelve minutes after takeoff. All 230 people aboard died. Recovery crews brought up more than 95 percent of the wreckage. The National Transportation Safety Board’s [final report](https://www.ntsb.gov/investigations/AccidentReports/Reports/AAR0003.pdf), issued in August 2000, identified an explosion in the center wing fuel tank as the event that began the breakup. It could not identify the exact source of ignition. Those conclusions rested on different evidence: investigators could trace the order in which large pieces broke away even where fire had damaged the wiring they needed to examine.

## Where the breakup began

Investigators divided the recovery area according to the distribution of wreckage. The field closest to Kennedy Airport along the flight path held parts of the center wing structure, pieces of cabin flooring above it, and air conditioning equipment from below it. The location of a single piece can be misleading: different objects fall at different speeds and can drift. The case depended on the collective pattern and on what the metal showed. One of the earliest failures, the Board found, was a partition inside the tank that swung forward and struck the tank’s front wall. It left marks on that wall, which itself bowed forward. The location of the first debris pointed investigators to the tank; the direction of the damage pointed to pressure building inside it.

That tank held little liquid fuel on this flight, but the space above it contained fuel vapor mixed with air. On the ground, air conditioning equipment beneath the tank had warmed it. Investigators used a similar 747 to measure how warm the tank could get under comparable conditions, and tested the fuel vapors in a laboratory. A separate test on a retired 747 showed that internal pressure could break the tank structure, although that test used a different fuel mixture. None of those tests reproduced the precise ignition that occurred on Flight 800. The leading explanation for ignition was a short circuit outside the tank that sent excess voltage along wires used to measure fuel quantity. The Board did not recover a wire that it could identify as the site of that short, or establish where and how energy was released inside the tank.

A missile strike and an onboard bomb were investigated separately. The question was whether an external strike or a detonation elsewhere on the aircraft had left damage that would place it earlier in the sequence. The Board found no such pattern of damage. In the [[lockerbie]] investigation, by contrast, examination of damaged aircraft and baggage material helped establish an explosive device as the initiating event. It took more than the fact that both aircraft broke apart in flight to make either finding.

## What the witnesses could see

People on land, at sea and in other aircraft reported a light in the sky. Some described it as rising from near the surface. The NTSB’s witness group classified 258 accounts as involving a streak of light; 38 described a vertical or nearly vertical ascent, and 18 placed the origin at the surface. The categories could overlap. They did not record 258 people who saw the same path, much less 258 people who identified the object that made it. The group worked mostly from FBI interview summaries, some of which blurred the distinction between a moving light and a fireball. By the time the safety investigators had enough information to contact most of the witnesses, nearly two years had passed. They decided that the earlier accounts, for all their faults, were generally better evidence than fresh recollections.

One difficulty was knowing when the witnesses began watching. Sound from the first explosion took time to reach people at a distance. A person who turned toward the sound might then have seen an aircraft already breaking apart and still travelling through the air. In November 1997, the FBI presented a CIA animation at the press conference announcing the end of its active criminal inquiry. The CIA had worked with a subset of the accounts. Comparing what witnesses reported hearing with what they reported seeing, analysts argued that many were describing a fire trail in the last part of the breakup. The animation supplied a continuous view no one observer had. Its value was in showing how a rising light and a later fireball might both belong to the aircraft; the images themselves could not verify that sequence.

The NTSB used the pattern of wreckage and radar returns, together with calculations, to estimate what the rest of the airplane did after the nose separated. Its analysis supported a brief climb. The flight recorders had stopped by then, so the height of that climb was a reconstruction, not a recorded altitude. A radar return can help locate an object without establishing what it is. In this case the path proposed by the Board had to fit not just where returns appeared but where pieces from the aircraft were eventually found. The Board did not claim that every observer’s description fit that reconstruction equally well.

## Traces and their origins

The FBI laboratory found traces of explosives on three samples. Their exact positions on the wreckage were not documented. There were plausible routes for contamination: the same aircraft had been used in an explosive-detection dog exercise in St. Louis, and equipment or personnel involved in recovery could also have transferred residue. Tests showed that seawater could wash the residue away before the submerged pieces were even brought up. That made transfer during or after recovery a credible explanation, but investigators could not identify the source of the traces. The deciding evidence against a bomb or missile was the absence of corresponding detonation damage. A separate reddish deposit seen on seat parts was consistent with adhesive in laboratory analysis; it was not the same finding as the FBI’s trace results.

In June 2013, the TWA 800 Project petitioned the Board to reconsider. The group argued that the evidence supported a high-velocity explosion that the original investigation had missed. The Board assigned the review to investigators who had not worked on the original inquiry. Staff met the petitioners’ representatives and heard from a witness. On July 2, 2014, the Board announced it had denied the petition. It said the submitted evidence and analysis did not show the original findings were wrong. That decision did not identify an ignition source or turn the estimates of the damaged flight path into measurements.

## Prevention without an exact ignition point

The parallel FBI and NTSB investigations raised a practical problem: who gets to interview witnesses and examine evidence when the same crash may be both an accident and a crime? After Flight 800, the agencies made a formal agreement to coordinate evidence collection and preservation in investigations involving suspected deliberate acts. The NTSB kept the reconstruction for training and in 2021 announced it would document it in three dimensions before dismantling it.

The Federal Aviation Administration faced a different decision. Its July 2008 rule addressed the hazard that could be measured: how much time the most vulnerable tanks spent with a flammable mixture inside. It set performance requirements instead of mandating a particular inerting technology. The investigation could not tell the families exactly what ignited the vapor on Flight 800. It could tell regulators that a design which depended on keeping every possible source of ignition out of a flammable tank had failed to protect the people aboard.
