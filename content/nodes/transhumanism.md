A man with amyotrophic lateral sclerosis attempts to speak, and a computer produces a voice from the activity recorded in his brain. In a 2025 study, the system allowed him to change its intonation and sing short melodies. The achievement concerns something ordinary and important: being able to say something in a way that carries more than the words alone.

The result gives substance to a transhumanist hope: technology can remove a biological limit. Yet restoring speech does not demonstrate that a mind can leave its body, that aging can be abolished or that human beings are approaching a single decisive transformation. The restored voice is evidence for the ability the researchers tested.

Transhumanism is a commitment to using technology to extend human capacities, sometimes far beyond their present range. The Singularity is a more particular forecast: that the development of greater-than-human intelligence could accelerate further change beyond our ability to anticipate or direct it. Someone can favor an implant that restores speech without accepting that forecast. Keeping those judgments separate makes both easier to examine.

## A hope with more than one purpose

Julian Huxley's 1957 essay “Transhumanism” imagined humanity consciously developing its possibilities. Its final pages include education, an inner life and obligations to future generations. The essay is broader than a specification for replacing bodies with machines. It gives a collective purpose to human development, with all the difficulties of deciding who can speak for humanity.

Huxley’s idea of improvement also carried a eugenic commitment. He later served as president of the Eugenics Society from 1959 to 1962. The association supplies a reason to investigate how improvement was defined and imposed; it does not make every subsequent medical intervention an expression of the same policy.

The [Transhumanist Declaration](https://www.humanityplus.org/the-transhumanist-declaration) on Humanity+'s website began with a group of authors in 1998 and was subsequently modified; the displayed text was adopted by its board in 2009. It favors extending capacities, reducing suffering and allowing individuals wide choice in modification. It also recognizes technological risks and calls for concern for people's dignity and interests around the world. Reading it as an unconditional endorsement of whatever can be built misses those commitments.

A declaration can state purposes that practice fails to meet. Personal choice, access and safety can conflict. A person may accept a substantial burden to regain an ability, while another may reasonably refuse the same intervention. That disagreement is not an obstacle to be eliminated by defining one choice as the proper next stage of evolution. It is part of what autonomy would have to protect.

## What a speech interface actually does

The [2025 paper by Maitreyee Wairagkar and colleagues](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40506548/) reports a brain-to-voice system using 256 implanted microelectrodes in one participant with ALS and severe difficulty articulating speech. The decoder translated recorded neural activity into synthesized voice and returned audible feedback. The participant could modulate features such as pitch, rather than only select text to be read in a fixed voice.

Text can carry an answer; intonation can distinguish a question, convey emphasis or help the speaker sound less detached from the exchange. The extra control can give the speaker more say in how the exchange unfolds. A person can gain a way to participate without becoming a fundamentally different kind of being.

It was a trained system for a particular task in a particular participant. It was not a demonstration that any private thought could be extracted from any brain. One extended-data analysis also reported declining performance after about fifteen days when a fixed decoder was tested on later recordings. That observation concerns the evaluated decoder, rather than the inevitable lifetime of every possible implant. It shows why maintaining an interface belongs in the account alongside establishing that it can work.

An implanted system creates a relationship with its supporting equipment and software. For any future routine service, it would matter whether help remained available, whether a user could control when output occurred and what happened if the provider stopped supporting it. An ability that depends on continuing help is useful under the conditions that make that help reliable. Those conditions extend beyond the research session.

The same attention should be given to refusal. Someone who values an implant's help need not endorse compulsory implantation. Someone who refuses surgery need not deny the value another person receives. A philosophy of enhancement becomes coercive when it treats that difference as a failure to understand progress.

## Editing cells is not redesigning a descendant

Gene editing can change a patient’s cells while leaving the reproductive line untouched. The FDA's [December 2023 announcement](https://www.fda.gov/news-events/press-announcements/fda-approves-first-gene-therapies-treat-patients-sickle-cell-disease) described Casgevy, a treatment using a patient's own blood stem cells, edited outside the body and returned after high-dose chemotherapy clears cells from the bone marrow to make room for the modified cells. The altered cells increase fetal hemoglobin production, helping prevent red cells from sickling.

The announcement reported that 29 of 31 evaluable recipients reached the trial's specified outcome: at least twelve consecutive months without severe vaso-occlusive crises—episodes caused by sickled red cells blocking blood flow—during the follow-up period. Forty-four people had been treated. The evaluable group had sufficient follow-up for that endpoint; the percentage was not a statement that 93.5 percent of everyone with the disease had been permanently cured. The study was single-arm, without a randomized comparison group.

For someone living with recurrent severe crises, that period without one can matter greatly. Reaching it through treatment also involves collecting cells, undergoing chemotherapy and receiving the modified cells back. Describing the procedure only as a precise edit would leave out much of what the recipient undergoes.

This is somatic editing: it concerns cells of the treated person. Heritable editing raises another question because an alteration in the reproductive line could pass to descendants. The WHO's 2021 governance account explicitly distinguishes those situations. Using the word CRISPR for both does not make their risks, consent or consequences interchangeable.

A proposed enhancement could concern a different target, affect several traits or work differently under different conditions. The blood-cell result would supply no estimate of that new intervention’s benefit or risk. Nor can the treated person's consent stand in for the interests of people who might inherit an alteration.

The history examined in [[eugenics]] makes the choice of desirable traits especially important. Reducing suffering and ranking people by a preferred standard are different purposes, even where a proposal uses the language of improvement. The effects on existing people who have a trait also deserve attention. Supporting an intervention need not mean treating a person who declines it, or cannot obtain it, as having less claim to respect and support.

## A change in an aging measure

Researchers studying life extension face a long wait for the outcome they most want to know. The CALERIE trial randomly assigned 220 adults without obesity to a calorie-restriction program or their usual diet for two years. A [later analysis, published in 2023](https://www.nature.com/articles/s43587-022-00357-y), examined DNA methylation in blood: chemical markings used by algorithms to estimate aspects of biological aging.

The intervention slowed DunedinPACE, a measure of the pace of aging, but did not significantly change the biological-age estimates from PhenoAge and GrimAge. The reported effect sizes were small. The authors called for longer follow-up with disease and mortality outcomes. The result concerned the pace estimated by one algorithm; longer follow-up would test whether changing that measure accompanies better health.

Such measures are useful partly because a lifetime is a long time to wait for an answer. Their convenience also creates a temptation. If a test returns an age below a person's calendar age after an intervention, it is easy to describe the result as years regained. The test itself cannot supply those future years. The number becomes clinically useful if its change can tell us something dependable about the person’s later health.

A person can support research into healthier aging without accepting a promised date for defeating death. The research question is how an intervention affects function, disease and survival. A forecast of eventual immortality adds a chain of further expectations that a short trial was never designed to test.

## The person in the proposed copy

An upload would have to run independently of the living brain from which it was made. It would need to reproduce that particular brain’s functions closely enough to support a further claim: that the person continues in the new system.

Anders Sandberg and Nick Bostrom's 2008 [*Whole Brain Emulation: A Roadmap*](https://files.givewell.org/files/labs/AI/SandbergandBostrom2008.pdf#page=11) separates the engineering tasks: acquire relevant information from a brain, interpret it into a model and run the model. The report also distinguishes levels of success. Resembling someone's behavior, producing conscious experience and continuing that person's identity are separate criteria. It explicitly sets aside the philosophical consciousness and identity criteria while concentrating on other technical levels.

A detailed map of neural connections would still need an interpretation before it could run as a model. The interpretation would specify what its components do and which features matter to the result. A faster computer would not decide which features the model could omit without changing the mind it was intended to preserve.

There is a further problem even if the engineering is granted. Imagine a procedure that leaves the original person alive while creating a second system with the same memories. Both could remember entering the procedure. From that moment they could have different experiences. The new system's sincere conviction that it is the original would not, by itself, explain why the original should expect to experience life through it.

If the procedure made two copies, identifying either one as the sole continuation would need a reason that did not apply equally to the other. Their shared past would not prevent their futures from separating. That problem matters even if both systems are granted full moral standing: whether a new person deserves protection and whether an existing person has survived are different questions.

This thought experiment does not settle whether some form of continuity could count as survival. It shows why copying a pattern and preserving a person's future cannot simply be treated as the same promise. A gradual replacement proposal would pose the continuity question differently, but gradualness would still need an argument for why it preserves what matters.

The related argument in [[simulation-hypothesis]] requires another step. The possibility of implementing a conscious mind in a computer does not establish that civilizations will run enormous numbers of ancestral simulations. That additional expectation concerns what future people could and would choose to do. It cannot be read directly from the possibility of a copy.

## Where the forecast becomes the Singularity

Vernor Vinge's 1993 essay gave the Singularity a specific mechanism. Greater-than-human intelligence might produce still more capable intelligence, accelerating the process that created it. He considered several possible routes, including computers, networks, intimate human–computer interfaces and biological improvement. The proposed feedback is what joins the routes, rather than the mere presence of advanced machinery.

In a 2005 explanation of his own forecast, Ray Kurzweil placed the transformation at 2045. His account combined improving computation, understanding the brain and systems able to redesign themselves. That is a forecast with dependencies. A cheaper calculation does not establish that the required algorithm has been found, that it can be validated or that its output can be translated into an effective biological intervention.

Progress on one task can leave another scarcely changed. A system might improve at producing an answer on a benchmark while remaining unreliable at a particular task. A discovery might make a treatment conceivable while leaving years of testing and manufacturing work. A faster design cycle would need to overcome whichever constraint actually limits the next step. The constraint need not be the same from one field to another.

The forecast also leaves the direction of improvement to be specified. A system better at pursuing an objective is not necessarily better at choosing an objective people have reason to accept. Greater capability could help reduce suffering or make an unwanted arrangement harder to escape. Transhumanism's ethical aspirations do not follow automatically from a prediction about intelligence.

The resemblance to a religious promise of deliverance helps explain some of the hope invested in this future. It says little about whether a decoder or an edit works, just as the working device says little about deliverance. A speech interface can succeed while a timetable for the abolition of biological limits remains speculative.

## The freedom to remain unmodified

Enhancement could produce pressure without a government ordering it. Suppose an employer rewarded the sustained attention made possible by a hypothetical intervention and gradually organized work around that capacity. Employees might remain formally free to refuse while finding that refusal carried a growing cost. The ethical question would concern the working arrangement as well as the product's safety.

Access presents a related problem. An expensive improvement could widen an existing advantage. That possibility deserves attention before adoption, but it is not a demonstration that a permanent biological caste must result. Pricing, public provision and the effects of a particular intervention would help determine the outcome. Declaring either universal liberation or inevitable domination avoids those choices.

There is no single person authorized to decide the future of the human species on its behalf. There are patients, participants, children, workers and others with different claims on the institutions developing these tools. Some will want an intervention urgently. Others will need protection against having it imposed, or against losing ordinary support because a promised fix exists.

The man using the speech interface need not wait for a Singularity for his regained expression to matter. Taking that achievement seriously also means taking its limits, maintenance and his continuing choices seriously. The person remains more important than the future the device has been recruited to symbolize.
