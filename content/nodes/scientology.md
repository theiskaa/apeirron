The men used the government's own photocopiers. In May 1976, a librarian at the United States Courthouse in Washington noticed two visitors using machines in the United States Attorney's Office after hours. They returned. Suspicion reached the FBI, and on a third visit agents questioned them about their identities.

That small sequence helped expose a much larger operation. Scientology operatives had been obtaining government documents through covert entry and copying. After the encounter, people in the church's Guardian's Office worked to hide their involvement. The attempted protection of an operation became an obstruction case of its own.

A job could provide entry to a building; credentials could make a visitor look as though he belonged there. A copied file could be returned without its owner knowing that the information had traveled elsewhere. Snow White depended on ordinary administrative systems being used for a purpose their custodians had not authorized.

## What was being defended

L. Ron Hubbard's movement grew from *Dianetics*, published in 1950, into Scientology. The church's own history dates its Los Angeles foundation to 1954. Its religious account concerns an immortal spiritual being, the *thetan*, and the recovery of spiritual ability through prescribed practices. Members' beliefs are part of the setting of the case; they cannot by themselves explain who ordered a burglary.

Auditing is a central practice. An auditor asks questions, and an E-meter assists the session. The church describes the device as a religious artifact that helps locate spiritual distress. It interprets movements of its needle through a doctrine of mental images and accumulated “charge.” Its current explanation expressly disclaims effectiveness in diagnosing, treating or preventing disease.

In an auditing session, a person speaks about private experiences to someone trained to interpret them within a particular system. The church's [public statement promises](https://www.scientology.org/faq/scientology-and-dianetics-auditing/is-information-divulged-during-auditing-sessions-always-kept-confidential.html) that material disclosed in auditing remains confidential and will never be used against the person. That promise matters whether an outside reader accepts the spiritual interpretation of the meter or not.

An organization also holds information about itself: correspondence, financial records, legal advice and accounts written by critics or officials. Government files can affect its tax treatment, travel, litigation and reputation. An organization may have good reasons to seek them. A record can contain a false assertion, and copying that assertion into other files can make it look corroborated. A lawful means of examining and challenging the record serves a real purpose.

Scientology's defense of Snow White begins there. [STAND, a Scientologist advocacy organization](https://www.standleague.org/resources/whitepapers/the-snow-white-program-and-the-church-of-scientology-the-true-story.html), presents Hubbard's April 1973 program as an effort to expose and legally expunge false government reports. It stresses litigation and freedom-of-information requests, and attributes the program to damaging reports circulating among governments. The stated objective matters when deciding what particular instructions required.

A records request asks a public body to disclose information under rules that can be argued about in court. An operative secretly entering an office takes the decision away from the custodian. The fact that both activities seek a file does not make them the same act.

## Obtaining the inside view

The Guardian's Office carried out intelligence and protective work for Scientology. Its operatives pursued an advantage in disputes with the government by obtaining information that the other side had not agreed to share.

A [published excerpt of the stipulation of evidence](https://www.mikerindersblog.org/wp-content/uploads/2020/08/EXCERPTS-MSH-STIP-OF-EVIDENCE-PDF.pdf) describes Cindy Raymond's 1974 instruction to recruit a loyal Scientologist for employment at the Internal Revenue Service, to obtain Scientology-related records including litigation material. Employment supplied a legitimate reason to enter a building; the covert assignment gave that access another use.

In November 1974, operatives bugged the IRS chief counsel's conference room, using a wall socket for the device and a car radio outside to hear and record the meeting. They removed the equipment afterward. Officials could speak privately without knowing another party had heard their discussion.

Such access changes a legal contest. One party normally knows its own preparation and tries to anticipate the other's. Secretly obtaining the opposing party's discussions gives it knowledge beyond the arguments and evidence exchanged through authorized procedures. It can learn where an inquiry is heading, which issues worry officials and what they intend to do next.

The material also has to travel. An intrusion that produces information useful to an organization requires someone to receive it and make use of it. The distinction between the person who enters the room and the people directing or benefiting from the entry is central to an investigation of conspiracy. Finding one operative does not automatically identify the entire arrangement, but following instructions and reports can connect the actions.

The surviving documents let an investigator follow recruitment, approval and reporting separately. An order to obtain files and a report that files have been obtained can show the relationship between an official's desk and an operative's night work.

## The courthouse encounter

The two men in the courthouse were Gerald Bennett Wolfe and Michael Meisner. The [1981 appellate opinion in *United States v. Heldt*](https://law.justia.com/cases/federal/appellate-courts/F2/668/1238/413855/) recounts their copying operation and the ensuing cover-up. Wolfe worked at the IRS. The men used false IRS identification, and their activities included covertly obtaining files from an assistant United States attorney's office.

When FBI agents questioned them in June 1976, apparently official credentials initially helped them leave. The operation's protection was imperfect. A librarian had noticed the visits, the visitors had left entries in sign-in records, and investigators could check an identity against the agency that supposedly issued it.

A person with a plausible card might get past an immediate question, but the card's name, number and photograph still had to fit records held elsewhere. A cover identity that works in one conversation can fail when two offices compare information. The false identity depended on the officials stopping at the first plausible answer.

The copying itself also left the source files available. Their return could postpone discovery because an owner looking for a missing folder would find it in place. Yet an absent original was not necessary for a breach. The damage lay in another party's possession of the contents. Looking only for stolen physical objects would miss what the copying achieved.

Snow White was targeted at particular files and people. Alongside [[mass-surveillance]], it raises a question about who can obtain information about whom, under what authority and with what opportunity for the subject to know. Private organizations can build covert information-gathering capacities even when they cannot exercise a government's legal powers.

## Protecting a witness from the investigators

After the courthouse encounter, Meisner went to California. The effort to conceal him eventually became coercive. The stipulation excerpt describes instructions to sever his outward connection with the Guardian's Office and arrangements to keep him hidden. It also describes guards and an occasion when he was handcuffed, gagged and moved against his will.

Meisner's position had changed. During the operation, he had been useful because he could carry out instructions and knew how the work fitted together. Once investigators sought him, the same knowledge made his independent choices dangerous to his superiors. Keeping him available to the organization meant keeping him unavailable to the authorities.

Meisner wanted to decide for himself whether to leave. A hidden location might initially shelter someone from arrest. Guards preventing that person from surrendering serve a different interest. The organization was trying to control not only what outsiders knew but what one of its own participants could do.

In June 1977, Meisner got away from that supervision and contacted federal authorities. Roughly a year had passed since the courthouse encounter. His account helped obtain the warrants used in July 1977, and the seized documents corroborated it.

Earlier correspondence made the effort to manage him visible. The stipulation excerpt includes a September 1976 letter in which Mary Sue Hubbard proposed an alibi. Richard Weigand replied that investigators had handwriting in courthouse logs and could consult witnesses and experts. He was assessing the obstacles to making their account stick.

That exchange gives the participant's recollection something to be compared with. It shows people discussing a problem they shared and anticipating how investigators might test their explanation. An instruction, a reply and a later account can be checked for sequence and agreement; a document's place in that exchange can reveal more than an isolated alarming sentence.

The cover-up widened responsibility. Obtaining a government file and concealing a wanted participant were different decisions, made at different stages. People who took part in the second could become involved in a criminal conspiracy even if they had not entered the original office. The attempt to contain the consequences created further conduct for investigators to reconstruct.

## What the courts established

Nine Scientology officials, including Mary Sue Hubbard, were convicted in the 1979 proceeding on a stipulated evidentiary record. The defendants agreed not to dispute its factual accuracy or sufficiency while preserving specified legal challenges. The counts differed: Mary Sue Hubbard was convicted of a conspiracy covering obstruction and related offenses; Sharon Thomas of misdemeanor theft. Jane Kember and Morris Budlong were convicted separately after extradition.

The charges and disposition give the case its boundaries. They establish responsibility for particular crimes; later accusations still need their own examination. L. Ron Hubbard himself was not convicted in this proceeding.

The searches themselves were contested. In the Washington warrant litigation, a district judge initially regarded a broad provision as an unconstitutional general warrant. [The appellate court reversed](https://law.justia.com/cases/federal/appellate-courts/F2/572/321/451636/), reading it in conjunction with the particular crimes described in the supporting affidavit. Its decision also expressly considered the authority to seize material identified as Snow White files.

A search for evidence in a religious organization's offices can expose material unrelated to the alleged crimes. Authority to search for one category of records is not unlimited permission to inspect and publish everything an organization holds. The legal challenge asked whether the government's authorization was adequately confined.

The appellate rulings allowed the prosecution to proceed. They were not a finding that privacy ceased to matter once the government suspected a religious body. A criminal investigation has to establish both what the defendants did and whether the state obtained its evidence lawfully. The two questions can be examined without making either side immune from scrutiny.

## A plan against Paulette Cooper

The July searches also brought other operations to light. Paulette Cooper had written *The Scandal of Scientology*, a critical account of the movement. In their [1979 sentencing memorandum](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/7/70/Usa_vs_mary_sue_hubbard_sentencing_memorandum.pdf), prosecutors described Operation Freakout, a series of 1976 plans aimed at getting her imprisoned or committed to a psychiatric institution, or otherwise stopping her criticism.

The proposed methods included impersonating her in threats to officials and obtaining her fingerprints on paper that could be turned into a threatening letter. A planted impression would connect a real physical trace with a statement she had not made. The aim was to get authorities to treat the manufactured combination as evidence against her.

The memorandum also described an earlier episode: bomb-threat letters sent in 1972, Cooper's indictment in 1973 and the dropping of that prosecution in 1975. The later plan proposed reusing a method thought to have worked before. It was a further effort against Cooper after the earlier prosecution had ended.

The prosecutors were arguing for punishment and discussing conduct beyond the stipulated case. They cited specific operational documents, but an assertion in their sentencing memorandum remains distinct from a separate conviction for the act it describes.

The method proposed against Cooper has something in common with the harassment and discrediting examined in [[cointelpro]]. An organization could try to silence a critic by arranging for another institution to punish her. The comparison concerns the technique. It does not establish common personnel, command or identical outcomes.

Here the state would be used as an unwitting instrument. A forged threat could turn a dispute over a book into an apparently unrelated criminal matter. The critic would then have to explain the evidence against her, with the actual author of the threat concealed. Possession of a convincing-looking document could shift the burden in ordinary public discussion long before the deception was uncovered.

## Confidentiality and institutional responsibility

The files an institution receives willingly can be as sensitive as those it obtains covertly. The church's current auditing statement promises confidentiality without exception. Participants are entitled to hold it to that promise.

In [*Wollersheim v. Church of Scientology*](https://law.justia.com/cases/california/court-of-appeal/3d/212/872.html), the California appellate court found substantial evidence that private auditing information had been used in a campaign against a former member. The church offered a different explanation: information had been shared in the normal process of seeking advice from more experienced Scientologists. The court explained why the jury could reject that account and accept the retaliatory one.

The case also examined coercion, including pressure to continue participation. The United States Supreme Court sent the judgment back for reconsideration in light of a punitive-damages decision. [On remand in 1992](https://caselaw.findlaw.com/court/ca-court-of-appeal/1766542.html), the California court reaffirmed a judgment with reduced damages.

A confidential practice gives the receiving institution a responsibility because participants may disclose material they would withhold elsewhere. If that trust is broken, the injury arises from what was done with the information, whether or not the practice is recognized as religious.

## The later agreement

In 1993, Scientology and the IRS reached a closing agreement resolving their tax disputes and providing for exemption determinations. The [publicly reproduced agreement](https://www.cs.cmu.edu/~dst/Secrets/irs-closing.html) includes a $12.5 million payment, resolution of outstanding cases and ongoing compliance arrangements. It records a negotiated legal settlement.

The payment addressed potential tax liabilities through 1992. The agreement also created a Church Tax Compliance Committee, responsible for reports during a seven-year transition. Past liabilities and future compliance therefore received different treatment. Recognition for the covered entities came with arrangements for an ongoing relationship with the IRS.

Those terms explain what the settlement accomplished more clearly than a declaration of either vindication or government surrender. It resolved tax disputes; the earlier convictions remained part of the record.

Snow White shows how much could be done with access to ordinary offices and the discipline to transmit what was found there. It also shows a limit to that discipline. The participant who carried instructions knew enough to explain the operation, and the effort to keep him silent produced its own record. Once Meisner spoke, the paperwork that had helped people coordinate could help investigators follow them.
