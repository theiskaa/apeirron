On April 15, 2013, two explosions near the finish line of the Boston Marathon killed Krystle Campbell, Lingzi Lu, and eight-year-old Martin Richard. Hundreds of people were injured. Three days later the FBI released images of two suspects. By the following evening one of them, Tamerlan Tsarnaev, was dead and his younger brother Dzhokhar was in custody. The brothers also killed Sean Collier, a Massachusetts Institute of Technology police officer.

A jury later convicted Dzhokhar Tsarnaev. The trial established responsibility for the bombing, but it could not answer every question the case raised. The FBI had assessed Tamerlan before the attack. Massachusetts officials had asked residents over a wide area to stay inside during the search for his brother. And the defense wanted jurors deciding whether Dzhokhar should die to hear an account of an earlier crime allegedly committed by Tamerlan. Each question involved a different decision and a different body of evidence.

## Before the cameras had names

The images released by the FBI were part of a larger reconstruction. Investigators compared footage from the area with witness accounts and other evidence; the published pictures were an appeal for help identifying two people, not the entirety of the case. Later, the driver of a car the brothers seized escaped and reported what had happened. By the time Dzhokhar stood trial, the defense did not dispute his participation in the bombing. Its main argument was that the older brother had led him into it and that this mattered for punishment.

After the images went out, it emerged that the FBI had interviewed Tamerlan in 2011. The bureau had known his name and had a file on him. But having a file and recognizing a face in footage are separate events. To establish that FBI officials withheld his name when they released the images, it would be necessary to show that investigators had already identified him in them.

The [inspectors general’s public report](https://oig.justice.gov/sites/default/files/legacy/reports/2014/s1404.pdf) traces the warning beyond that interview. Russia’s security service told the FBI in March 2011 that Tamerlan was preparing to travel to Russia to join an underground group. The FBI interviewed him and checked records, then closed its assessment without finding a link to terrorism. Russia supplied substantially the same information to the CIA that September. The CIA passed it to the National Counterterrorism Center, and Tamerlan was added to a watchlist.

He traveled to Russia in January 2012, as the warning said he was preparing to do. The trip did not prompt further investigation. The agent who had handled the assessment told reviewers he would not have acted differently had he learned of the trip at the time; other FBI officials said the information would have mattered. The Justice Department’s inspector general identified steps that could have made the original assessment more thorough, but could not say what interviews not conducted at the time would have revealed. The warning named a person and anticipated a trip he actually took. It did not identify a plan to attack the marathon.

## A request with the force of an emergency

After the confrontation in Watertown and Tamerlan’s death, authorities still had to find Dzhokhar. The state’s [after-action report](https://www.mass.gov/doc/after-action-report-for-the-response-to-the-2013-boston-marathon-bombings/download) describes how the April 19 shelter-in-place request grew from Watertown to nearby communities, including Boston. Transit service was suspended, and police carried out door-to-door searches in part of Watertown. Advising residents to remain indoors was different from declaring martial law. Still, a request backed by the closing of the transit system and repeated by several police agencies was not a routine piece of advice. It changed what people could do even if nobody formally ordered them to stay home.

By late afternoon most of the planned searches had been completed, and officials believed the suspect might already have left the area. Extending the restrictions would impose further costs on residents and businesses without an obvious gain. At 6:03 p.m. Governor Deval Patrick announced that the request had been lifted. At 6:42 a Watertown resident reported seeing someone in a boat on his property. Dzhokhar was arrested that evening. This sequence shows that the resident’s call located the suspect. It does not tell us whether the restriction had delayed that call or prevented some other harm earlier in the day.

The distinction between the broad request and the search of a particular home also matters. The latter raises a different question: whether officers had consent, a warrant, or a specific emergency that justified going in without one. The after-action report is an account of decisions and coordination. It does not resolve the legal basis for every individual entry, and the capture of the suspect does not settle those questions retroactively.

## What the jury did not hear

The jury convicted Dzhokhar on all thirty counts originally submitted to it in 2015. During the penalty phase, the defense argued that Tamerlan had been the dominant brother. It sought to introduce statements from Ibragim Todashev, who had told investigators that Tamerlan was involved in a 2011 triple homicide in Waltham. Todashev died after an FBI agent shot him during the interview. Neither he nor Tamerlan could testify about the allegation at Dzhokhar’s trial. No conviction established Tamerlan’s role in those killings.

This was not a question of whether the defense had mentioned Waltham. It was a dispute over what the jury should be allowed to hear. The defense argued that an earlier violent crime involving Tamerlan, without Dzhokhar, could help show which brother drove the later plot. The trial judge excluded the evidence, in part because resolving the disputed account could pull the sentencing proceeding into a separate inquiry about the Waltham killings. The Supreme Court upheld that decision in 2022. The dissent saw the evidence as better supported and more important to the defense than the majority did. Neither opinion decided who committed the Waltham murders. Both addressed whether excluding the account from the sentencing proceeding had been a legal error.

## What it meant to screen a juror

The appeals also asked how thoroughly the trial judge had to screen jurors for the effect of pretrial coverage. The defense wanted prospective jurors to say what they had learned about the case from news and other sources. The trial judge rejected a blanket question of that kind but allowed follow-up questions in individual interviews. In 2020 the First Circuit set aside the death sentence on two grounds, including the refusal to ask for those details. The [Supreme Court](https://www.supremecourt.gov/opinions/21pdf/20-443_m6ho.pdf) reversed in 2022. It held that the trial judge’s broader screening process was within his discretion and that excluding the Waltham evidence had not required a new penalty hearing.

A further [First Circuit decision in 2024](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USCOURTS-ca1-16-06001/pdf/USCOURTS-ca1-16-06001-2.pdf) considered a narrower question. One seated juror said she did not believe she had commented on the case online, but her account had posted and shared messages about the bombing and its aftermath. Another had discussed jury selection on Facebook. The court noted that the apparent discrepancies could reflect ordinary failures of memory or a misunderstanding of what had been asked. It directed the trial judge to investigate rather than finding that either juror was biased or setting aside the death sentence at that stage. It held that the specific allegations could not be dismissed without further examination. A ruling that the court need not ask every prospective juror the same broad question does not decide what should happen when there is evidence that a seated juror may have answered a material question dishonestly.

The 2024 decision left the trial judge with two specific accounts to test. The earlier FBI assessment and the house searches call for the same attention to who knew what and when. A verdict about the bombing, however well supported, cannot tell a Watertown resident why officers entered a particular home.
