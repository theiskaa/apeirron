On April 25, 2013, a secret court ordered Verizon Business Network Services to deliver call records every day. [The order](https://clearinghouse-umich-production.s3.amazonaws.com/media/doc/70234.pdf) covered call records for communications within the United States and between the United States and abroad. The recipient was to keep supplying them until the order expired in July. The arrangement became public in June through documents disclosed by Edward Snowden.

The records were not recordings of conversations. They included numbers, times, duration and routing information. The order excluded the contents of calls and subscribers' names, addresses and financial information. The order required records in bulk, rather than only those already connected to a particular suspect.

By the time an investigator wanted to look up a number, the government would already have the records. A person whose calls had attracted no suspicion could enter a later search through a friend, a workplace or another contact. The privacy question began with that initial decision to copy the records.

## Records made for one purpose, available for another

Telephone companies create call records for ordinary business purposes, including billing and fraud detection. A record can exist without anyone listening to a conversation. The surveillance program gave the government a centralized copy that could be searched across participating providers.

The [Privacy and Civil Liberties Oversight Board's January 2014 report](https://documents.pclob.gov/prod/Documents/OversightReport/ec542143-1079-424a-84b3-acc354698560/215-Report_on_the_Telephone_Records_Program.pdf) describes the telephone program operated under Section 215 of the USA PATRIOT Act. The report describes the program as it then operated and says the collected records excluded call content and cell-site location information. It also explains that a number could be associated with a name through other sources.

The original order concerned specified Verizon business entities. It did not establish coverage of every Verizon service or every American call. The government could learn about callers through records that the order expressly distinguished from the conversations themselves.

A phone number acquires meaning through its pattern of contacts. Their timing and their relationship to other contacts can disclose something intimate without revealing a single spoken word.

Consider calls to a specialist clinic. The connection might be sensitive even if the record says nothing about treatment. The caller might be a patient, an employee or a relative making an inquiry. Metadata can reveal a relationship while leaving its meaning uncertain. That is a reason to protect the information and to resist treating the inference as a settled fact.

## Collecting a network before knowing whom to search

The purpose of the telephone program was to find connections involving terrorism suspects, including contacts inside the United States. A known number could become the starting point for a search of records already held.

The 2014 report describes contact chaining in terms of hops. The first hop finds numbers in contact with the starting number. The second finds numbers in contact with those numbers; a third extends the search again. In that version of the program, the initial selection required a determination of reasonable, articulable suspicion by a designated NSA official.

An investigator may have an unfamiliar number and need to know whether it connects to someone already under investigation. A broad, searchable collection can reveal a connection quickly. It can also help establish that a suspected foreign plot has no apparent domestic connection, allowing investigators to concentrate elsewhere.

But distance through a network is not a measure of guilt. The second person may know the first for an innocent reason. The next connection may be a shared business or service. A chain of calls is a lead that needs interpretation, not a chain of responsibility.

Restricting the starting number could narrow what an analyst was permitted to retrieve. That did not mean the government initially collected only the records of suspected people. Other people's communications relationships were already in the database, available for a future search.

Retention also differed between the original repository and material retrieved from it. The 2014 report describes a five-year limit for records in the repositories, but says information appearing in query results need not be destroyed at that point. Those results could be stored and analyzed under further rules. A deletion deadline for one database therefore did not describe the whole life of a record.

Access introduces further choices: who can make a query, how far it can travel, how long records remain and which results can be passed to another agency. Approval of the initial query still leaves decisions about the information retrieved.

The case for such a program has to show why the whole arrangement is necessary. Speed is a benefit, but its value must be compared with narrower ways to obtain useful records. Otherwise convenience can turn into a justification for retaining information about everyone within reach of the collection.

## PRISM was a different arrangement

The disclosures also exposed communications collection under Section 702 of the Foreign Intelligence Surveillance Act. Its procedures and powers differed from the telephone-records program.

In its [July 2014 report](https://documents.pclob.gov/prod/Documents/OversightReport/823399ae-92ea-447a-ab60-0da28b555437/702-Report-2.pdf), the oversight board described PRISM as provider-assisted collection. The government supplied a selector, such as an email address associated with a targeted person, and compelled a provider to supply communications to or from it. This was not an unrestricted ability to browse every company's servers.

The authorized targeting concerned non-US persons reasonably believed to be outside the United States. *US person* is a legal category that includes citizens and permanent residents. A person in the United States, or a US person abroad, could not intentionally be selected under those rules. The court approved categories of foreign-intelligence acquisition and targeting and handling procedures, rather than issuing an individual warrant for each selected person.

The protection against intentional targeting did not prevent Americans' communications from entering the collection. A person in the United States might communicate with someone targeted abroad. The resulting incidental collection could include their side of the exchange. Rules governing retention and later searches then mattered; the fact that a person was not the target did not make the communication disappear.

The same report described upstream collection at the communications backbone. It included some messages that mentioned a selected identifier, even when the target was not a participant in the exchange. That could bring in communications between other people. In [April 2017](https://www.nsa.gov/Press-Room/Press-Releases-Statements/Press-Release-View/Article/1618699/nsa-stops-certain-section-702-upstream-activities/), the NSA announced that it would stop upstream internet collection based solely on such mentions, following compliance problems and review of the technical and privacy concerns. The earlier report describes a form of collection the agency later narrowed.

The privacy question follows the communication through the system. Unrelated material may enter alongside a target's messages, be retained, and become available for later queries. A rule about the initial target needs to be read alongside the rules for what happens to everyone else's information.

## Oversight has to test the reason for collecting

The January 2014 board report was sharply critical of the bulk telephone program. It concluded that Section 215 did not provide an adequate statutory basis and found little unique counterterrorism value in the program as operated. Separate statements by board members Rachel Brand and Elisebeth Collins Cook disputed important conclusions and opposed shutting the program down. The disagreement concerned the particular law, evidence of value and proposed alternatives.

The dispute did not depend on finding a malicious official. A person can act in good faith within an approved program whose design remains disproportionate or whose legal basis is mistaken. An oversight body needs to examine the arrangement as well as individual misconduct.

The report also describes a concrete compliance failure uncovered in 2009. An automated alert system checked incoming records against a list that included many numbers without the required suspicion approval. It did not automatically produce contact chains for those unapproved numbers, but the matching itself violated the court’s orders. The government attributed the failure to misunderstandings about the system and the rules. The alert process was shut down after attempts to fix it failed. The system's actual operation mattered, including steps officials regarded as mere preparation.

The board reached a different overall assessment of Section 702 in its July report, recognizing substantial foreign-intelligence value while identifying concerns about incidental collection, retention and queries involving US persons. An evaluation of one program cannot simply be transferred to the other because the same agency operates both.

Courts also mattered. In [*ACLU v. Clapper*](https://law.justia.com/cases/federal/appellate-courts/ca2/14-42/14-42-2015-05-07.html), the Second Circuit held in May 2015 that Section 215 did not authorize the bulk telephone metadata program. It did not decide the constitutional issues. The distinction is consequential: a finding that an agency exceeded a statute is different from a ruling that every similar collection would violate the Constitution.

Secrecy makes these questions harder to contest. A provider served with an order may be forbidden to disclose it, while the people whose records are supplied may not know they have a reason to challenge anything. Judges and legislators can scrutinize classified material, but public debate depends on some account of the powers used and the interpretations supporting them.

Public scrutiny does not require naming every live target. It can be supported by disclosure of the legal framework, evidence of how a program performs, significant compliance failures and a way for independent arguments to reach the court. Oversight must be able to change or stop an arrangement. Merely knowing that an authorized procedure was followed cannot settle whether that procedure should exist.

## The telephone program did change

The response was not simply a new name for an unchanged database. The [2015 implementation account of the USA FREEDOM Act](https://www.intelligence.gov/ic-on-the-record-database/results/fact-sheet/fact-sheet-implementation-of-the-usa-freedom-act-of-2015) set out a change from government bulk storage to records held and queried by providers in response to specific selectors and court orders, with an emergency procedure. The old bulk collection was to end in November 2015.

The successor also encountered problems. The board's [2020 fact sheet](https://documents.pclob.gov/prod/Documents/OversightReport/7f5bfdcf-0ae0-4a10-b6ef-0c53137161ea/CDR%20Fact%20sheet%20FINAL.pdf) records that the NSA suspended the successor call-records program in early 2019 after considering intelligence value, costs and compliance and data-integrity concerns. Records were subsequently deleted with specified exceptions, including information used in disseminated intelligence reports. The board members agreed with the decision to halt collection, while differing on other aspects of the analysis.

This is a history of a particular collection and its replacement, not a claim that surveillance ended. It does show that design choices and external challenges can have consequences. Treating every reform as imaginary would obscure the changes needed to judge the next proposal.

## What the possibility of observation can change

Someone researching a terrorist organization may worry about how the interest will look in a government file. They may leave the page unread. There would be no prosecution to count, and perhaps no investigator would ever have noticed the visit. The expectation of being observed can matter before anyone actually examines a record.

Jonathon Penney's [2016 study of Wikipedia use](https://btlj.org/data/articles2016/vol31/31_1/0117_0182_Penney_ChillingEffects_WEB.pdf) examined page-view trends around the June 2013 disclosures. It began with 48 English-language articles selected using terrorism-related terms from a Department of Homeland Security list, tracking views from January 2012 through August 2014. The terms were used to select topics; the study did not assume users knew the list or were reacting to that particular monitoring program.

Penney analyzed trends and used further comparisons and refined article groups to assess alternative explanations. He found declines consistent with a chilling effect. The data counted page loads, not unique people, and did not record why any individual stopped reading. A decline after the disclosures fits the possibility that some readers became wary. Changes in news interest or other events could also affect visits, which is why the comparisons matter. The result concerns a pattern across pages; it cannot tell us what one reader thought before closing a tab.

If many people quietly avoid a topic, the loss may produce few visible disputes. It still belongs in an assessment of a system alongside the investigations that system helps complete.

The 2013 order reached people before anyone had a reason to investigate them. A later restriction on searches could limit what happened to their records, but it could not undo their collection. That first decision needed a justification of its own.
