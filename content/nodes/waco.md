On February 28, 1993, federal agents went to Mount Carmel, a Branch Davidian religious community outside Waco, Texas. They had warrants to arrest its leader, David Koresh, and to search the property in a firearms investigation. Before they arrived, an undercover agent who had just spoken with Koresh warned that the people inside knew about the raid. The commanders went ahead. Four federal agents and six Branch Davidians died that day. The FBI took over after a cease-fire. The standoff ended on April 19, when fire swept through Mount Carmel. More than seventy people died that day, including children.

Later inquiries asked who started the fire, whether government agents fired into the residence that day, and why the FBI took years to acknowledge that it had used pyrotechnic tear-gas rounds. Those are questions about evidence of particular acts. They do not resolve the decisions made before the fire, starting with the decision to send agents to execute a plan after its commanders had been told that a central premise no longer held.

## A warning before the raid

Mount Carmel was both a place of worship and a home. Koresh taught that he had a special role in interpreting the Seven Seals described in the Book of Revelation. That claim helped give him authority over the adults around him. The children lived under decisions they did not make. Evidence of sexual and physical abuse of minors later figured in arguments over how quickly the FBI needed to act; it was not the basis of the federal firearms warrants. The Bureau of Alcohol, Tobacco, and Firearms suspected violations involving weapons that federal law restricted. It chose to try to arrest Koresh and secure evidence at the residence in a single operation.

Robert Rodriguez, the undercover agent, found himself inside with Koresh on the morning of the raid. Koresh made it clear that he expected federal agents to come. Rodriguez left and relayed what he had heard. The Treasury review found that commanders took reassurance from his report that he had not seen residents arming themselves. They treated that observation as a reason to hurry, not as a limited view of what might be happening elsewhere in the building. The review concluded that they should have called the raid off. A later House report also criticised Treasury supervisors for failing to communicate clearly when the operation was to be cancelled. The warning reached the people in charge; the responsibility to act on it had been less clearly assigned.

The House report also found that the ATF had misrepresented suspected drug activity to obtain military training without paying for it. That finding concerned how the agency obtained assistance. It did not establish that military personnel fired on the residents. Who fired first remained disputed. The House investigators regarded it as more likely that the first shots came from inside, but said the evidence did not settle the matter. Reports that shots came from government helicopters were also disputed; the committee said it could not determine with certainty who fired the shots that left holes in the roof. Neither question needs to be settled to judge the decision to proceed after the warning. The commanders had put agents and residents in contact under conditions the plan was supposed to avoid.

## Negotiating under pressure

Thirty-five people left before the fire. All twenty-one children who came out were released in the first six days of the standoff. On March 2, the FBI broadcast a recording by Koresh as part of an agreement under which he was to come out. Koresh did not keep that agreement, telling negotiators that God had instructed him to wait. The broken promise gave commanders grounds for doubting later assurances. It also left them with a population inside whose willingness and freedom to leave could not be read from the word of a single man.

The same agency that was trying to secure departures by agreement was also applying pressure. Over the course of the standoff it cut power and used lights and loudspeakers. Negotiators did not all agree about the effect. Gary Noesner later said he had been seen by his superiors as an impediment to officials who wanted a more aggressive approach. By the final days, chief on-site negotiator Byron Sage told a senior Justice Department official that further talks were unlikely to help. By then, no one had left for weeks. Even among the people who had spent the most time speaking with Koresh, there was little agreement about whether he would keep talking on terms anyone could act on.

On April 14, Koresh told his lawyers that he would leave after completing a manuscript on the Seven Seals. The lawyers thought the offer differed from the earlier one: he had identified a task he could finish, and they believed he had begun it. Jeffrey Jamar, the FBI commander at Waco, knew of the offer but doubted it was serious. The House report found that Justice Department officials considering the gas plan did not get the lawyers’ assessment as a credible alternative. Waiting might not have brought a surrender. The officials deciding whether to wait were nevertheless entitled to hear the strongest account of what waiting might achieve.

## What the decision exposed people to

Attorney General Janet Reno approved the FBI’s plan on April 17. The bureau proposed to use tear gas to force people out. Its plan envisioned a gradual process but provided for a faster operation if agents came under fire. On April 19, the FBI reported that people inside fired as its vehicles approached, and the pace of the operation increased. Armored vehicles damaged the building. Around midday, fires broke out at several points and spread quickly. Nine people got out after the fire began.

The later House report took seriously the evidence that children had been abused. It did not find a basis for concluding that they faced an increased risk of abuse during the standoff that justified the timing of the operation. It concluded the gas plan put them in greater danger. The Justice Department’s own 1993 evaluation was more favorable to the decision to end the standoff. Whatever weight is given to each assessment, an operation predicted to induce adults to leave could not guarantee that they would take children with them or that everyone would be able to get out once parts of the building had been damaged.

## What the recordings and disclosure showed

In 1999, the FBI acknowledged that it had used some pyrotechnic tear-gas rounds on April 19. That contradicted earlier government accounts. Attorney General Reno appointed John Danforth as special counsel. His investigation used recordings and interviews, a recovered casing, and the chronology to identify where and when the rounds were fired. It concluded they were used well before the fires, at a separate concrete structure rather than inside the residence. On the origin of the fire, the investigation relied on several lines of evidence, including recordings of people inside discussing the use of fuel, testimony and analysis of the points where the fires began. It found that some Branch Davidians started the fires. That is not a conclusion that everyone in the building intended to die.

Attorneys and independent analysts cited flashes on infrared footage as possible government gunfire. Danforth’s investigators compared the footage with a reconstruction and photographs that showed reflective debris at many of the same locations. They concluded that federal agents did not fire into Mount Carmel on April 19. Danforth’s investigators also reviewed the autopsies and confirmed that twenty Branch Davidians died of gunshot wounds that day. They could not determine who fired every fatal shot. The conclusions about the fire and gunfire left another matter unresolved: why had the government taken six years to acknowledge the pyrotechnic rounds? The special counsel reported that agents had discussed the rounds with federal prosecutors in 1993. The problem was not that no one in the government could know what happened until 1999; it was that information already held by some officials was not reliably brought forward.

There were separate questions before the criminal courts. Surviving Branch Davidians were prosecuted. In *Castillo v. United States*, the Supreme Court ruled that the kind of firearm at issue under the statute had to be proved to a jury, rather than left to a judge to find at sentencing. That decision concerned what a jury was required to determine about the defendants’ conduct. It did not review the FBI’s choice between waiting and acting on April 19.

Danforth drew a distinction between intentional wrongdoing and bad judgment. His inquiry concentrated on the former. Finding that some residents started the fires does not tell us whether another week of talks would have brought people out. The commanders did not know that either. The point of examining their choices is to see what risks they accepted on the information they had, and what information they failed to pass on. Two years later, Timothy McVeigh invoked Waco in explaining the [[oklahoma-city]] bombing. What he chose to do to other people neither answers those questions nor makes them less important.
