In April 1991, the Yale students choosing the next members of Skull and Bones selected women. The alumni board responded by changing the locks on the society's building. [Yale’s timeline of women’s history](https://celebratewomen.yale.edu/history/timeline-women-yale) records the confrontation and a vote that July to accept women.

The students could choose whom to invite, but older members could deny them access to the building. An invitation meant less if its recipient could not get through the door. Selection and ownership were different sources of authority. A tradition that looked like an undergraduate privilege was also an institution controlled across generations.

Presidents William Howard Taft, George H. W. Bush and George W. Bush were members of Skull and Bones. The roster supplies names. Its political significance depends on what people did for one another, what they expected in return and when their private loyalties affected decisions made for others.

## A permanent home

The society began at Yale in 1832. William Huntington Russell and Alphonso Taft were among its founders. It eventually acquired the building on High Street known as the Tomb and a legal organization, the Russell Trust Association, through which its property could be held. The original Tomb was built in 1856.

David Richards' history of the building describes alumni concerned that the group might disintegrate after a bad election or an undergraduate quarrel. Incorporation and a permanent home would give it a life beyond the current class. Richards is himself listed as a member in his Yale graduation book; the magazine discloses that relationship and states that his history uses public sources.

The people who made a club enjoyable would graduate. Their successors could decide to do something else. Property and older members could preserve a name and a set of expectations the next class had not invented. Control of the meeting place gave alumni a way to insist on those expectations.

A closed exterior advertises exclusion. Ownership makes the exclusion enforceable. Members had made a place that later members could enter only on the institution's terms. In 1991, the students encountered the limits of their authority at the door.

Descriptions of the club often leap from its founding to a secret European ancestry. Similarities to German student organizations or Masonic ceremonies are treated as a transmission route from the [[illuminati]]. That requires a history of contact and adoption, not an accumulation of skulls, oaths and classical names. The Bavarian order and the Yale society have separate records. A borrowed form of ceremony would not establish inherited political objectives.

## Selecting future leaders

Skull and Bones is known for selecting a small annual group, conventionally fifteen students, who participate in the society during their senior year. It does not select fifteen people at random from the population. Its candidates have already reached Yale, and some have conspicuous campus positions or family connections before they receive an invitation.

A calculation that treats those invitations as random would be misleading. Counting presidents among a few thousand members and comparing the result with the whole United States changes the question halfway through. The relevant comparison would include people with similar educational opportunities, family resources, ambitions and early accomplishments who were not selected.

Suppose a society invites someone because he already edits the student newspaper. If he later builds a successful publishing career, the membership and the career will be associated even if the invitation supplied no useful assistance. Now suppose an older member introduces him to an investor who finances his company. The club's relationships have contributed something. Both situations can occur, and a list of names cannot distinguish them.

Recognition itself can help someone advance. An invitation can confer standing among people who take the institution seriously. A person who arrives with advantages can acquire more; identifying the earlier advantages does not cancel the later ones.

The Bush family makes the layers difficult to disentangle. Prescott Bush, George H. W. Bush and George W. Bush belonged to different generations of the society. They also shared a family, a university tradition and relationships extending well beyond the club. If someone helped the younger Bush, it would matter whether the connection arose through Bones, his father, business, politics or several of those at once.

The routes through those relationships matter. A relative making an introduction, a banker considering an investment and a society collectively directing a member are three different accounts of what happened.

## An introduction, a job, an investment

Writing in the *Atlantic* in 2000, Alexandra Robbins describes George W. Bush contacting fellow member Robert H. Gow when he needed work in 1971. Gow hired him as a management trainee. She also follows the raising of money for Bush's oil business, including assistance from his uncle Jonathan Bush and an investment by William H. Draper III, both members.

Gow could offer work. Jonathan Bush could open a route to investors, and Draper could risk his money on the venture. Each had something the younger Bush needed. None needed to convene a society meeting to provide it.

Robbins’ account does not establish that Gow hired Bush only because of Bones, that an investor expected political repayment, or that the whole society had agreed on a career plan. Those would require further evidence about the particular decisions.

The help nevertheless had a value that someone outside those relationships might never receive. Opportunity can be distributed through personal recognition before any formal competition takes place. Someone equally capable outside the circle may never learn that a position or potential backer was available. The advantage is access to consideration, which can precede the question of who is best qualified.

A record of the introduction could explain why one proposal reached a potential investor’s desk. A list of later business successes would conceal that small but consequential step.

A different sort of collaboration produced *Time*. Briton Hadden and Henry Luce, both in Yale’s class of 1920 and both Bonesmen, had worked on the *Yale Daily News* before founding the magazine in 1923. Jim Sleeper’s review of Isaiah Wilner’s history describes the new publication organizing news into departments and building pointed accounts around individual newsmakers. Their shared work produced a magazine.

Their shared university and newspaper work offer reasons they knew one another’s abilities. Membership added another relationship, but separating its contribution from their journalistic partnership would require more than knowing they both belonged. The collaboration still had consequences: a particular way of presenting news became a business. Its founders did not have to be pursuing a private society’s political program for their friendship to affect public life.

That magazine was a real achievement. Recognizing it does not require defending every advantage its founders enjoyed. A group of friends starting a magazine, members improperly favoring one another in public appointments and an organization directing editorial policy are different uses of a relationship. The public interest in each follows from the power being exercised.

## Privacy among friends

Robbins also describes personal disclosures during the undergraduate year: conversations about relationships and extended accounts of members' lives. Her interviewees offered different judgments about their significance. A participant could understand such a conversation as ordinary intimacy, while an outsider could see a group cultivating unusually strong obligations.

Both interpretations begin with a recognizable experience. Someone tells a small group something difficult, discovers that he is still accepted and feels closer to the people who listened. Repeated meetings give that trust time to deepen. A member may value the society for friendships that do not feel instrumental to him.

The same trust has conditions. A person may disclose more because he expects privacy; another member may feel obliged to protect him later because of what was shared. Neither consequence proves that the group keeps a complete, permanent file on every member or uses those files for blackmail. A story about intimate conversation cannot establish an archive, its contents or a threat to use it.

Friendship can be demanding without being coercive. A coercion claim needs an act: a threat, an imposed condition or a disclosed consequence of refusing. Converting any confidence into proof that departure is impossible would make close friendship itself look like captivity.

There is still a question for a member who later holds public office. Keeping a friend's private history confidential is one obligation. Concealing a friend's improper request for public assistance would be another. A vow of privacy cannot decide in advance that both deserve the same protection.

Ceremonies can mark out a private world, although individual members may experience them differently. Wearing a costume, receiving a society name or making a disclosure does not tell an observer how someone will vote, whom he will hire or whether he will resist a fellow member's request decades later.

## The letter about Geronimo

One of the society's most troubling stories concerns the claim that members took remains from Geronimo's grave at Fort Sill, Oklahoma. In 2006, the *Yale Alumni Magazine* reported Marc Wortman's discovery of a letter from Winter Mead to F. Trubee Davison, dated June 7, 1918. It said a skull attributed to Geronimo and other objects had reached the Tomb.

The letter is significant because it places the story among members near the alleged event. Mead was not at Fort Sill. His report of what others had done cannot independently identify the remains, establish who removed them or prove their present location. The report distinguishes the letter from a later internal history that names Prescott Bush among the alleged participants.

The correspondence is disturbing even with those limits. A member was passing along a claim of grave robbery as society business. Even a false identification could reveal an attitude toward another person's remains that deserves examination. The possibility that the skull belonged to someone else would change the identification, not make the treatment of a grave acceptable.

For Geronimo’s descendants, the question concerned burial and the treatment of remains. They did not all want the same response.

In [*Geronimo v. Obama*](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USCOURTS-dcd-1_09-cv-00303/pdf/USCOURTS-dcd-1_09-cv-00303-0.pdf), twenty descendants sued federal officials, Yale and Skull and Bones. They sought the return of remains and other relief under the Native American Graves Protection and Repatriation Act. Other descendants intervened to argue that, in accordance with Apache custom, the gravesite should not be disturbed.

The court dismissed the complaint on July 27, 2010. It found no applicable waiver of federal sovereign immunity as the case was pleaded. It also held that the provision invoked, § 3002, concerned discoveries after November 16, 1990, whereas the removal alleged in the complaint occurred around 1918. The complaint consequently failed against the non-federal defendants too.

The opinion resolved neither the remains’ identity nor whether the theft occurred. Its summary of the complaint reported allegations, not findings after a trial. The dismissal left those historical questions unanswered.

For the society, this is a question that cannot be answered by saying the rituals are private. If human remains were acquired, their identity and provenance concern people who never consented to become part of the club's traditions. Establishing those facts is a responsibility distinct from satisfying public curiosity about an initiation.

## The same membership, different claims on office

George W. Bush and John Kerry's membership attracted particular attention during the 2004 presidential campaign. In Bush's *Meet the Press* interview, broadcast on February 8, Tim Russert put their shared affiliation to him. Bush answered, “It's so secret we can't talk about it.”

Bush answered a public question with a private institution’s rule. A viewer could reasonably want a fuller answer about continuing obligations and relationships. That request would not require him to believe that the society had arranged the election.

The two major-party nominees' shared affiliation did guarantee that either one's victory would put a member in the White House. It did not show that the club selected the nominees, that their platforms were interchangeable or that the result was immaterial. To establish such a strategy, someone would have to follow the decisions by which the candidates advanced and show what the society did.

The same distinction applies to George H. W. Bush's intelligence career. His appointment as Director of Central Intelligence in January 1976 is a matter of public record: the CIA dates his swearing-in to January 30. Membership can help identify relationships worth examining. It cannot establish that a CIA operation was directed through the Tomb, or that a later policy decision was made to fulfill a society obligation.

A club can matter without acting as a single command. Members can recognize one another, return calls or recommend people they trust. The public concern grows when that assistance enters an institution whose decisions ought to answer to other standards. A public appointment or contract is not a favor that its decision-maker owns personally.

Imagine a qualified former classmate recommended for a public role. The shared history may explain why the official knows his work. It becomes a problem if the decision is protected from scrutiny by an obligation to the club, or if competing applicants are excluded for reasons the institution would not defend openly. The evidence lies in the process and the obligation, not in whether both people once attended the same dinner.

## Who could change the locks

The dispute over women’s admission exposed disagreement about what the brotherhood should be. The older members possessed leverage through the building. The students' choice of women challenged an inherited boundary, and the dispute forced members to decide whether that boundary should survive. The university, the trust and the students had different roles. The choice of members and the power to admit them to the building had come apart.

Opening admission changed who could enter the society. It did not make admission generally open, distribute its relationships to everyone else or answer how a member should handle a later conflict of loyalties. A club can become more inclusive along one line while remaining selective along others. Those are separate judgments about what changed.

The alumni board had a building and changed its locks. Gow had a job to offer; Draper had money to invest. These powers were different, and the people subject to them had different options. Following those relationships can reveal friendship, unfair advantage or misconduct. The roster gives us people to ask about. It cannot tell us in advance which account their actions will support.
