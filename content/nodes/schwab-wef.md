A Davos invitation did not get everyone through the same doors. In a 2021 study of the World Economic Forum, Christina Garsten and Adrienne Sörbom described badges that admitted participants to different spaces. The white hologram badge carried the greatest access. Other colors marked lower levels of admission. The distinctions were visible to security staff and to the people wearing them.

The [researchers' account](https://www.diva-portal.org/smash/get/diva2%3A1629182/FULLTEXT01.pdf) puts access at the center of the argument about Davos: who gets close enough to influence a discussion, and who decides? Their description concerns the period they studied, rather than a current guide to badge privileges. The institution could admit people while still keeping them apart.

The Forum selects participants, sets questions and creates relationships through its meetings, reports and partnerships. Those activities provide places to investigate private influence. Connecting them to a claim of secret command requires a further account of how the governments concerned took their decisions.

## What Schwab built

Klaus Schwab founded the European Management Forum in 1971. It became the World Economic Forum in 1987, reflecting a scope that had expanded beyond management discussions for European businesses. Davos remained its best-known gathering, but the institution developed year-round programs and communities.

The Forum is an independent Swiss nonprofit foundation, headquartered in Geneva. Its [2025–26 institutional account](https://www.weforum.org/publications/annual-report-2025-2026/our-institutional-framework/) describes a network of offices, centers, meetings and related organizations. Switzerland signed an agreement [governing its status in 2015](https://www.admin.ch/en/nsb?id=55987). Its international standing does not give its meetings the authority of a parliament.

Its corporate relationships are central to its work. The Forum describes its [partners](https://www.weforum.org/partners/) as leading companies and the driving force behind its programs, with access to networks and experts through its centers. The notes to its 2025–26 financial statements identify membership, partnership and registration income. Those arrangements make the companies participants in an ongoing institution, rather than an audience assembled once a year to hear speeches.

A government confronting a technological or economic problem needs knowledge held outside its departments. Businesses can coordinate where separate actions would fail, and researchers can bring a neglected issue to people able to act. A well-run forum can make those encounters easier.

Someone must decide which companies, experts, officials and civil-society organizations belong in a conversation. The criteria may reward expertise and achievement while also favoring people already connected to institutions with money and status. Calling the result a gathering of stakeholders does not tell us how those outside the room are represented.

## Influence begins with the invitation

Garsten and Sörbom's study drew on eighty days of observation and seventy interviews with Forum staff. They followed activities across several locations and examined selection, secrecy and status. Their account describes an organization that spends considerable effort choosing participants and deciding whether they should be invited again. Names and identifying details were generally changed to protect informants.

The invitation itself can become valuable. It recognizes a person as someone worth meeting, while that person's presence adds to the Forum's prestige. A network can confer authority on its members and borrow authority from them at the same time. The benefit begins with being recognized as someone worth consulting.

A private organizer is entitled to choose the speakers at its own event. The public question arises when that choice helps determine who gets to shape a minister's understanding of an issue. The minister remains responsible for the eventual decision, including the advice relied on and the people whose interests were omitted. The absence of a formal instruction from Davos cannot settle that question.

People who share a meeting can still disagree. An encounter might produce a partnership, expose a disagreement or achieve little. To connect a meeting with a policy, we need to follow the proposal, the people who could adopt it and the decisions they actually made. Prestige and proximity create opportunities; the outcome requires its own account.

## The cabinet remark and the program behind it

At Harvard Kennedy School on September 20, 2017, Schwab discussed leadership development with David Gergen. In the [recording of the event](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=AoBRnrtX9U4&t=1075s), he used the phrase “penetrate the cabinets” while praising the reach of the Forum's Young Global Leaders. He cited Justin Trudeau's government and claimed that half or more of its cabinet were Young Global Leaders.

Schwab presented access to senior government as an institutional success. The remark supplies no dated cabinet list or program records to verify the numerical claim. It cannot demonstrate that the people concerned took instructions from the Forum. A founder describing the success of his institution is a source for his ambition and self-presentation, not a completed assessment of its effects.

The Young Global Leaders Foundation was established in 2004. Its [2026 announcement](https://www.weforum.org/media/entrepreneurs-public-leaders-and-innovators-join-the-young-global-leaders-class-of-2026) describes a class of 118 people under forty from 55 countries, selected through a year-long process. It sets out a three-year leadership program of executive learning, exchange and collaboration. This is the program described for that cohort; earlier formats should not be assumed to remain unchanged.

Selection recognizes people who have already distinguished themselves. That creates a problem for any claim that the program produced their later success. If an organization chooses people already likely to become prominent, a list of prominent alumni cannot show what the training caused. We would need evidence about what participation changed and what comparable people did without it.

The relationships formed may still matter. An official who knows executives, researchers and foreign counterparts can reach them more easily. Shared experiences may make particular assumptions feel familiar and reasonable. Those are plausible channels of influence worth tracing through an actual decision. They do not turn a program's alumni into one political actor.

For public officials, the relevant standard is whether they disclose relevant interests, explain the advice used and remain answerable for the choice. The title of Young Global Leader supplies neither an exemption from that scrutiny nor proof of disloyalty.

## Turning a survey into an agenda

Reports offer another route to influence. A clearly named global risk can travel from a chart into a speech, a board discussion or a government briefing. Before treating the chart as a description of the world, a reader should know what produced it.

The Forum's [*Global Risks Report 2026*](https://www.weforum.org/publications/global-risks-report-2026) draws on a survey of perceptions. Its [methodology](https://www.weforum.org/publications/global-risks-report-2026/global-risks-report-2026-appendix-b) says 1,302 responses were used after filtering. Respondents were drawn from the Forum's wider network across business, government, academia and other sectors. They chose or scored risks under different questions and time horizons; not everyone answered every section.

For the immediate outlook, respondents selected the risk they thought most likely to produce a material global crisis in 2026. The [published findings](https://www.weforum.org/publications/global-risks-report-2026/global-risks-report-2026-key-findings) put geoeconomic confrontation first, chosen by 18%. That figure is the share selecting an answer. It is not an independently measured 18% probability of a crisis. Other questions asked respondents to assess severity over two or ten years, producing different kinds of rankings.

A polished chart can make a judgment look like a measurement. The survey collects informed concerns; it does not convert those concerns into observed future events. The respondents are also a selected network, not a representative vote by the world's population.

Experienced people may notice a vulnerability others have missed. There is also power in setting the list of risks and asking people to rank it. The survey used 33 predefined risks, while allowing respondents to nominate something missing. The framing directs attention before the first answer arrives.

A government can use the report responsibly by asking how a risk applies to its population, what other evidence supports the concern and what tradeoffs a response would impose. Treating the ranking as a mandate would skip those decisions. The Forum has supplied a way of organizing the discussion, while the public consequences depend on what officials do with it.

## Who speaks for a stakeholder?

Schwab's [Davos Manifesto 2020](https://www.weforum.org/stories/2019/12/davos-manifesto-2020-the-universal-purpose-of-a-company-in-the-fourth-industrial-revolution/), published in December 2019, says companies should serve employees, customers, suppliers, communities and society as well as shareholders. It also treats multinational companies themselves as participants in shaping a global future alongside governments and civil society.

The first claim offers an objection to evaluating a company solely by returns to its investors. Workers can bear risks that a share price does not adequately describe; a community can suffer consequences that fall outside a company's immediate costs. Recognizing those interests is a defensible starting point.

The difficulty comes when those interests conflict. A community needs a way to choose who speaks for it, and employees need a way to challenge a proposal made in their name. A declaration of responsibility needs institutions that let those people assert their interests. Good intentions announced by executives cannot perform that role on their own.

Schwab's technological writing places the same problem in a changing setting. His [2016 essay on the Fourth Industrial Revolution](https://www.weforum.org/stories/2016/01/the-fourth-industrial-revolution-what-it-means-and-how-to-respond/) anticipates a convergence of physical, digital and biological technologies. He discusses benefits but also inequality, pervasive surveillance and loss of control over personal data. His warnings sit alongside his enthusiasm for technological change.

His proposed response includes closer cooperation among regulators, business and civil society. Technical knowledge can help a regulator understand a system. Consider a company explaining a technology that collects personal data. Its staff may know the system better than the officials who need to regulate it. That expertise can clarify what the system does, but cannot decide what uses should be permitted or what remedy someone harmed by it should have. Those decisions concern rights and public power. The question is whose judgment prevails when consultation ends and interests conflict.

The later [[great-reset]] initiative applied broad reform ambitions to the opening created by the pandemic. The [[transhumanism]] and [[mass-surveillance]] essays address distinct issues raised by the technologies. Neither a forecast nor a manifesto establishes that a particular intervention was adopted. Each needs a decision-maker, a mechanism and an account of its effects.

## A formal partnership

The Forum also enters formal institutional relationships. In June 2019, the UN and the Forum [announced a strategic partnership](https://www.weforum.org/media/world-economic-forum-and-un-sign-strategic-partnership-framework). The published announcement described six fields of cooperation, including financing sustainable development, climate, health, digital cooperation, gender equality, and education and skills. It also described an annual review of their work together.

That is evidence of institutional cooperation, with an expressed agenda and a process for reviewing it. It gives a critic specific questions: which projects resulted, whose participation increased, what resources were committed and how the results were assessed? The announcement alone cannot answer them.

The partnership can bring resources and expertise to public objectives. It can also give private participants more influence over how those objectives are defined or pursued. Those possibilities should be examined through the work done under the agreement. Calling the relationship harmless or calling it a transfer of world government would both outrun the announcement.

The connection to [[agenda-21]] is therefore about the actual development agenda and its institutional implementation, including the later 2030 framework. Different documents, commitments and partnerships need to be distinguished. The word global does not make them one instrument.

## The institution after its founder

Schwab left the chair and the Board of Trustees in April 2025. The [Forum's announcement](https://www.weforum.org/media/world-economic-forum-announces-governance-transition) says the board noted his resignation on April 20 and appointed Peter Brabeck-Letmathe interim chair. The [2025–26 governance report](https://www.weforum.org/publications/annual-report-2025-2026/leadership-and-governance) lists BlackRock’s Larry Fink and Roche’s André Hoffmann as co-chairs. The organization now has a different leadership to scrutinize.

In August 2025, the board [announced the conclusion of an investigation](https://www.weforum.org/media/news-release) into anonymous whistleblower allegations. It said there was no evidence of material wrongdoing by Schwab, while acknowledging minor irregularities and blurred boundaries between personal contributions and Forum operations. That is the board's published conclusion. The statement alone does not let a reader reconstruct the full inquiry; it supplies no basis for treating the original allegations as established facts.

The question of public influence survives the change in leadership. Selecting participants, giving a report authority and joining a partnership are organizational activities. Their consequences do not depend on imagining one founder personally controlling every official who attended his events.

The badge is a useful reminder of where to start. Access has different levels, someone allocates it, and the people outside may still be affected by what follows. The point of investigating Davos is to trace that distance—from a private invitation or proposal to a public decision—and establish who must answer for the result.
