When a car crashed near Susurluk in Turkey on 3 November 1996, its passengers included a member of parliament, a senior security official and a wanted far-right militant. Three people died; the politician, Sedat Bucak, survived. The militant, Abdullah Çatlı, carried a weapons licence and senior official’s passport bearing the interior minister’s signature, according to the account later set out by the European Court of Human Rights.

A man wanted for serious crimes had access to official protection and was travelling with people inside the political and security establishment. The resulting investigations concerned cooperation between officials and unlawful groups, including violence and criminal finance. There were names, documents and decisions to investigate.

This is the kind of arrangement associated with the Turkish term *derin devlet*, usually rendered as “deep state”. In American usage, the same phrase can mean something much broader: permanent agencies, the influence of contractors, officials resisting a president, or a hidden command supposedly responsible for almost any disputed event. The meaning can change within a single argument, carrying evidence about one kind of power into allegations about another.

How does power escape effective scrutiny? A secret operation, an agency withholding information and an official cultivating a private employer can each create that problem. Following what people actually did lets us locate responsibility. Treating every durable institution as one actor can make it disappear again.

## The report and the failed investigation

The [judgment in Ülkü Ekinci v. Turkey](https://hudoc.echr.coe.int/eng?i=001-60614) describes several investigations following the crash. A parliamentary commission examined relations between illegal organizations and the state. A later report commissioned by the prime minister described unlawful dealings among political figures, public institutions and clandestine groups. Some of its pages and annexes were withheld.

The case before the European Court concerned the killing of Yusuf Ekinci, a lawyer, in 1994. His widow alleged state involvement. One of his clients, Behçet Cantürk, had been killed a month earlier in similar circumstances. The court found that the Susurluk material gave investigators reason to examine that connection and possible state involvement. They had not done so.

The judges reached two different conclusions. They found insufficient evidence to establish state responsibility for Ekinci’s killing to the required standard. They also found that the authorities had failed to conduct an adequate and effective investigation. The failure to prove who killed him did not make the failure to investigate acceptable.

The investigation had concentrated on Ekinci’s family, friends and professional contacts while failing to pursue the possible link with Cantürk’s death. The court could identify that omission even though it could not establish who killed Ekinci. The demand for an effective investigation remained unanswered.

Susurluk also complicates the familiar opposition between elected leaders and unelected officials. A member of parliament was in the car, and a minister’s signature was on the papers. Political power and security power were connected. A secret network need not be an enemy of the elected government; people near that government may help sustain it.

## The broader American argument

In his February 2014 essay “Anatomy of the Deep State”, former congressional staffer Mike Lofgren described a durable combination of national-security agencies and private interests. He was interested in a contrast: visible partisan conflict could paralyze parts of government while military and intelligence activity continued. Contractors, financial institutions, shared assumptions and career incentives helped explain that continuity.

Lofgren’s argument was broader than a claim about a clandestine organization with a membership list. It concerned an entrenched way of governing, extending beyond public agencies. Peter Dale Scott similarly used the term for relationships between secret government and powerful people outside it, including financial and criminal interests. They directed attention to connections that an organization chart leaves out. Whether those connections explain a decision still depends on the record of that decision.

A new president inherits contracts, alliances and organizations with work already under way. Replacing their leadership does not remove those commitments. A program with trained personnel, secure facilities and a constituency in Congress is harder to abandon than a campaign speech makes it sound.

Continuity can therefore involve decisions taken openly. Legislators may protect local jobs tied to a program, an administration may retain an alliance it once criticized, and an agency may argue that replacing its systems would carry risks. Which explanation fits depends on what the officials chose and what they were told.

If elected officials wanted the policy to continue, the democratic question concerns their choice. If advisers concealed information necessary to change it, the question concerns that concealment. The same outward continuity can have different causes.

The national-security system built after the Second World War increased the scope for these problems. The 1947 National Security Act created the CIA and National Security Council and reorganized military institutions. It supplied lasting capacity to collect intelligence and coordinate policy. Successive presidents could use those institutions, inherit their priorities or struggle to control particular activities within them.

## A government needs people who stay

Newly appointed leaders depend on people who know the records and the work in progress. They need to understand what their predecessors promised, which deadlines are approaching and why an earlier decision was made. The staff who provide that account have influence from the beginning.

Consider the Senior Executive Service, sometimes included in accounts of a permanent hidden hierarchy. Created through the Civil Service Reform Act of 1978, it includes senior managers appointed through several routes. The Office of Personnel Management distinguishes career appointments selected through merit procedures from noncareer and limited appointments. Some posts are reserved for career officials to protect impartiality. It also excludes important categories, including positions in intelligence-gathering agencies, from this particular personnel system.

Even within the SES, appointments have different purposes. OPM describes a limited-term appointment of up to three years for a position expected to expire, and a limited emergency appointment of up to eighteen months. The category includes temporary assignments as well as careers.

A senior manager nevertheless has room to shape what happens. Officials choose which options to develop, which risks to emphasize and how quickly to implement a decision. They know details a newly arrived appointee does not. That knowledge can improve a policy or make it easier to frustrate one. A minister may depend on the same staff for both an explanation of what went wrong and the evidence with which to check it.

An official who says that a proposed action conflicts with an existing law gives the political leadership an objection it can examine. A staff member who conceals information or supplies a false explanation prevents that examination. Alleging obstruction requires identifying what the official received, what they did and what reason they gave. The personnel system alone supplies none of those answers.

## Secrecy changes who can ask questions

Intelligence work creates an especially severe imbalance. People inside an agency may know the operation, the sources and the mistakes. The public may receive only a statement that disclosure would endanger national security. Even an authorized overseer can struggle if the agency decides what information reaches the oversight process.

Some of that secrecy is necessary. Sources can be endangered, ongoing operations compromised and foreign intelligence relationships damaged by disclosure. The problem is that secrecy can also protect the people making those judgments from having their judgment tested.

The 2009 classification order, Executive Order 13526, set out an explicit boundary. It allowed classification under specified conditions involving identifiable national-security harm, while prohibiting its use to conceal violations of law, inefficiency or administrative error, or to avoid embarrassment. It also established procedures for authorized holders to challenge classification and for declassification review.

Enforcing that boundary requires access to the information being protected. A person denied a document may be unable to show that its real purpose is to hide an error. A review may take longer than the public decision the information could influence. And a document can remain inaccessible for reasons that differ from the original claim of secrecy.

The challenge procedure shows how much depends on who can get inside the process. Under the order, an authorized holder who believes information is improperly classified can seek review and appeal to an interagency panel. A member of the public who has never seen the file cannot make the same informed challenge to its contents. Mandatory declassification review offers another route for a sufficiently specific request, subject to exceptions. In both cases, making the information independently assessable requires more than knowing that a review mechanism exists: someone must identify the material, press the request and obtain an answer that can itself be challenged.

Records can also disappear altogether. A withheld file may eventually be released and checked. A destroyed file leaves testimony, copies and indirect traces to carry work the original might have settled.

The history of [[mkultra]] makes that last problem concrete. The CIA program investigated means of influencing behavior, including drugs administered to people without their knowledge. The Church Committee traced its approval to CIA director Allen Dulles on 13 April 1953.

The committee also quoted the CIA’s own 1963 inspector-general report. That report identified ethical concerns, questions about legality and dangers to the rights of American citizens. Parts of the institution knew there was a problem. In January 1973, records were destroyed on orders that the committee traced through Sidney Gottlieb to director Richard Helms. Investigators were left unable to reconstruct the program’s overseas operational use fully.

The destroyed records denied later investigators the opportunity to check what had been done. Here the chain of responsibility can be followed from approval through internal warning to destruction. The remaining uncertainty concerns the activities lost with the files; it need not obscure the decision that made them harder to investigate.

## Sometimes the president is inside the secret

The Iran-Contra affair involved efforts to evade congressional restrictions and scrutiny. Yet the president was directing part of the secret policy.

On 17 January 1986, Ronald Reagan signed a covert-action finding concerning Iran. The accompanying memorandum described a proposal involving arms, contacts with Iranian elements and the release of American hostages in Beirut. It recorded disagreement among senior advisers. The finding directed that reporting to Congress be withheld until the president ordered otherwise.

Presidential authority was being used to conduct a policy secretly and restrict congressional knowledge of it. The signed finding places Reagan inside the direct American arms initiative. Whether he knew about the separate diversion of proceeds to the Contras requires a different account.

Private intermediaries added a further layer. Independent counsel Lawrence Walsh’s [account of the Enterprise’s finances](https://irp.fas.org/offdocs/walsh/chap_08.htm) describes Richard Secord and Albert Hakim using companies and Swiss accounts to handle arms transactions and payments. The arrangements could conceal sources, recipients and profits.

One device depended on a misleading company name. A shell company was given a name resembling that of a real arms supplier. Payments into its account could then make money retained as profit look like money paid to the supplier. Secrecy could protect a private margin as well as a government operation. Walsh traced the device through the financial manager’s memorandum explaining the purpose of the similarly named account. The concealed margin was recoverable from records of what participants had instructed one another to do. The memorandum supplied evidence of intent that a list of the participants’ former jobs could not.

The arrangement gave different people reasons to keep it secret: advisers sought to conduct policy, intermediaries took profits and congressional scrutiny threatened both. Their cooperation can be traced without assuming that their interests or knowledge were identical.

Presidential control over an operation does not by itself make the operation democratically accountable. Congress and courts have responsibilities of their own. A secret policy can be well controlled from the White House and still evade the checks that should constrain it.

## What a surveillance failure looks like in the record

The language of a deep state became especially prominent in arguments about investigations involving Donald Trump. One record worth examining is the review of how investigators obtained permission to surveil a former campaign adviser.

In December 2019, Justice Department inspector general Michael Horowitz reported on the FBI’s Crossfire Hurricane investigation and applications to surveil Carter Page. His [prepared Senate testimony](https://oig.justice.gov/sites/default/files/2019-12/12.11.19.pdf) distinguished the decision to open the investigation from the later use of surveillance authority.

The review found that opening the investigation complied with the relevant policies and had sufficient factual support under the applicable threshold. It found no documentary or testimonial evidence that political bias or improper motivation drove that opening decision. It also identified serious deficiencies in the applications submitted to the Foreign Intelligence Surveillance Court.

By the final renewal, the applications contained seventeen significant inaccuracies or omissions. Information questioning the reliability of source reporting had not been properly passed on. Information about Page’s relationship with another American agency was omitted. The applications made the evidence supporting surveillance appear stronger than it was.

The procedure made accurate disclosure especially important. The government presented the application without an opposing party there to challenge its account. Department lawyers and the court depended on the information investigators supplied. Internal checks meant to verify factual accuracy had also failed.

Horowitz rejected the explanations offered for the errors as inadequate and criticized failures throughout the supervisory chain. He also declined to speculate about whether correcting any particular error would have changed the court’s decision. His conclusions concerned the actions and evidence his office had examined.

One part of the record led to a criminal admission. In August 2020, FBI lawyer Kevin Clinesmith pleaded guilty to a false-statement offense after altering an email from the other agency to add the words “not a source”. A supervisor relied on that altered email in submitting the final surveillance application. This was a specific falsification by an identified official, beyond the omissions documented in the applications. It did not require treating every investigator’s decision as part of the same act.

The same investigation could have a proper basis at its opening and produce seriously defective surveillance applications later. The review located the failures in what agents supplied, what supervisors checked and what the court was allowed to know.

## The private career after public office

Influence can persist without an unlawful operation. A company may value a former official’s experience, contacts and knowledge of how decisions are made. That can be useful expertise. It can also create conflicts between public responsibilities and private opportunity, including while someone is still deciding what to do in office.

A 2021 Government Accountability Office review examined fourteen major defense contractors. Among people who had left Defense Department service between 2014 and 2019, the firms employed 1,718 former senior civilian, military or acquisition officials in 2019. This was a defined group in a selected set of companies, not a count of all former officials in the defense industry.

The [review](https://www.gao.gov/products/gao-21-104311) examined compliance with post-government employment restrictions. It found improvements in ethics advice and training, and a gap in requirements for contractor representations concerning a newer lobbying restriction. GAO recommended an assessment of whether to change the regulations; its follow-up record marks that recommendation implemented after the department undertook the assessment and began regulatory action.

These were employment relationships, not 1,718 findings of misconduct. The relevant questions concern an official’s work, the decisions in which they participated, subsequent representation and applicable restrictions. A conflict can be established through those decisions and obligations; the job title supplies a starting point.

Dwight Eisenhower’s 1961 farewell address expressed a related concern about the growing military establishment and permanent arms industry. He recognized their necessity while warning that their combined influence could acquire excessive power. The danger could arise without every participant intending the result. His proposed safeguard depended on public knowledge and democratic judgment, not confidence in the motives of whoever happened to hold office.

## What makes the claim useful

The cases turn on decisions that the label leaves unspecified. Who could authorize the action? Who carried it out? Who knew enough to stop it? Which money, document or instruction connects the participants? What could an investigator examine independently of their assurances?

These questions also distinguish kinds of power. A criminal network requires investigation of crimes and protection. An agency that withholds adverse information requires scrutiny of disclosure and review. A conflict of interest requires examination of duties and benefits. A disputed policy may require political argument rather than an allegation that its administrators secretly overthrew the government.

A structural account needs to explain how an institution’s incentives affect decisions. People approve requests, sign findings, select evidence, award contracts and destroy files. A useful structural explanation shows why certain choices became easier or more rewarding and how responsibility was dispersed. Responsibility remains with the people who exercised the available power.

An account also needs a way to recognize that it is wrong. If every missing record confirms an actor too powerful to leave evidence, an unsuccessful inquiry and a successful cover-up become indistinguishable.

The official passport, the account instructions and the incomplete application each gave investigators something to test. They also identified people who could be asked to answer for it. That is what the claim should help recover: a decision we can examine and a responsibility we can name.
