In May 1980, a State Department briefing described the route by which American support was reaching the Afghan resistance. The CIA obtained Soviet-pattern weapons, American aircraft carried them to Saudi Arabia, and Saudi aircraft took them onward to Pakistan. Pakistani personnel trained the recipients. The CIA station chief worked out operational details with Pakistani intelligence and President Muhammad Zia-ul-Haq.

One decision remained with Zia: whether to pass on newly obtained shoulder-fired anti-aircraft missiles. He feared a Soviet response. Washington could acquire the weapons, but Pakistan controlled a crucial decision about their delivery.

That division of power shaped the covert assistance program commonly called Operation Cyclone. The United States supplied money, equipment and political backing to the mujahideen, Afghan resistance fighters opposing Soviet forces and the government in Kabul. Saudi Arabia contributed support, and Pakistan provided access, training and distribution. Afghan parties and commanders fought the war, pursuing interests that extended beyond expelling a foreign army.

The Soviet withdrawal in 1989 achieved the program’s central military objective. It did not produce an agreed Afghan government or end the fighting. The supply partnership had held together governments and armed groups with different ambitions. After withdrawal, those differences became the centre of the war.

## The war before the American program

In April 1978, the People’s Democratic Party of Afghanistan took power in a coup. Its leaders attempted rapid political and social changes while carrying out repression and fighting among themselves. Resistance spread. By the following spring, the government was already asking Moscow for military help against a crisis it could not contain.

In a preserved telephone conversation from March 1979, Nur Mohammad Taraki told Soviet premier Alexei Kosygin that there was little active support for the government in Herat, where an uprising had occurred. Kosygin asked about workers who might be mobilized. Taraki replied that there were very few. He wanted Soviet personnel, including men who could pass as Afghans, to operate military equipment and help defeat the opposition.

Kosygin doubted the intervention could be concealed. He asked what had happened to Afghan officers trained in the Soviet Union. Taraki said most could not be trusted. The government had foreign equipment and trained officers, yet lacked confidence that those officers would fight for it. More weapons could not resolve that political problem.

Moscow initially resisted requests for combat troops. Its position changed as the Afghan leadership crisis deepened. Hafizullah Amin displaced Taraki, who was killed, and Soviet officials became increasingly suspicious of Amin’s intentions. An early December memorandum from KGB chairman Yuri Andropov described fears that Amin might turn toward the West and proposed supporting Afghan communists who wanted to remove him. The memorandum treated those suspicions as grounds for changing the Afghan leadership; it offered no demonstration that Amin worked for Washington.

The intervention at the end of December killed Amin and installed Babrak Karmal. Soviet forces entered what became a long counterinsurgency war. They could hold cities and launch powerful operations while struggling to turn military reach into durable political support in the countryside.

The war also changed the country the resistance hoped to govern. Agriculture was devastated, irrigation damaged and millions of people displaced. Refuge in Pakistan could save a family while separating it from land, income and local institutions. Armed organizations and their outside patrons could become sources of protection and resources in that disruption.

## What Carter authorized

The Carter administration had begun considering covert support before the main Soviet intervention. In an April 1979 meeting, officials discussed broadcasts, money and an assessment of the insurgents. National security adviser Zbigniew Brzezinski wanted the resistance sustained, even if it did not overthrow the government. CIA director Stansfield Turner warned that money could be wasted unless the Americans understood the people receiving it. Pakistan’s cooperation was already treated as essential.

In July, Carter signed two findings—written presidential authorizations for covert activity. They covered propaganda, broadcasting, cash and nonmilitary supplies. A May 1980 State Department account put the initial combined authorization at $695,000. Further authority followed in November. Lethal military aid was added on December 28, after the Soviet intervention.

The early aid gives plausibility to the claim that Washington was trying to lure Moscow into a war. Contemporary records establish a more limited purpose: sustaining an insurgency already causing Moscow difficulties. Soviet escalation was a risk American officials discussed, alongside doubts about the resistance’s strength.

[Brzezinski’s memorandum of December 26](https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1977-80v12/d97) described a grave setback and warned Carter against assuming Afghanistan would become a Soviet Vietnam. The guerrillas lacked the organization, sanctuary and large outside support that North Vietnam had enjoyed. Soviet success remained possible. Brzezinski therefore advocated more arms and money, support for Pakistan and cooperation with China and Islamic countries.

This was an argument for making the intervention costly after it had occurred. The outcome would depend on building a larger support effort. The resistance would have to remain able to fight, and Pakistan would have to remain willing to help it.

## The route became a relationship

Pakistan’s geography gave its government influence that a donor could not purchase away. The resistance needed supplies, training and refuge across the border. Pakistan faced a Soviet military presence next door and wanted American support for its own security. Cooperation could serve both governments while leaving them with different ideas about Afghanistan’s future.

In the May 1980 arrangement, the CIA bought weapons abroad and arranged delivery. Pakistani personnel trained Afghans, sometimes after receiving specialist training themselves. Zia could slow the introduction of a weapon he regarded as dangerously provocative. He also threatened to end participation after American press reports exposed elements of the cooperation.

The early effort to use Soviet-made equipment helped conceal the supplier. A rifle or machine gun of a type already common in the region revealed less than a distinctive American system would. Procurement nevertheless required money, transport, maintenance and a chain of decisions about who received the equipment. Each additional delivery renewed that dependence on Pakistani cooperation.

Pakistan’s Inter-Services Intelligence directorate, the ISI, became the key intermediary. That gave it influence over the relative strength of competing Afghan organizations. The resistance consisted of parties, local commanders and communities with differing regional bases and political ambitions. Afghan leaders who could obtain supplies had an advantage in recruiting, retaining fighters and bargaining with rivals.

Gulbuddin Hekmatyar, a Pakistani-backed Islamist leader, became an important recipient. He competed with other leaders, including Burhanuddin Rabbani and his commander Ahmad Shah Massoud, who had their own organizations and sources of authority. Rivalry could continue while all fought the Soviets. A common source of weapons did not make them a common government in waiting.

The CIA’s later institutional history describes the expansion of direct contacts with resistance commanders as the program escalated. Direct contact could improve American knowledge of a commander and his needs. Pakistan still controlled access to the border and the refugee camps, while the commanders retained their own political aims.

Pakistan also had reasons to seek a friendly, pliable government in Kabul. Its rivalry with India and concerns about Pashtun nationalism gave it interests extending beyond the Soviet occupation. An Afghan commander could be useful against Soviet forces while also being useful to Pakistan in a later struggle over who would govern. The choice of recipients was already a choice about the distribution of future political power.

## Escalation and its measures

American support grew substantially during the Reagan years. A Congressional Research Service account, citing the State Department, reported about $3 billion in American economic and covert military assistance to the mujahideen during 1980–1989. Saudi and other contributions added resources beyond that American figure; a complete total would require comparable accounts of each stream.

This program also differed from the concealed Contra operation. Congress participated in authorizing and funding Afghan assistance; early findings were reported to the relevant committees. Secrecy limited public examination, but it did not place every covert dollar beyond Congress. The question was also what classified oversight could discover about a program implemented through foreign services and divided armed groups.

The military problem was apparent before the Stinger missile arrived. A 1982 Defense Intelligence Agency assessment described the resistance’s need for heavier weapons, especially air defenses, while judging that Soviet forces could not seal the mountain passes permanently. The insurgents could sustain the existing stalemate. Reinforcements might pacify an area temporarily, only for resistance to return after the troops moved elsewhere.

An American intelligence assessment in 1985 similarly described expanding resistance and persistent Soviet difficulties. It also listed serious insurgent weaknesses: poor coordination, supply problems and fighting between rivals. Its forecast expected Moscow to persist rather than make a sharp change of strategy under the new Soviet leader, Mikhail Gorbachev. It also allowed for a wider war if resistance improvements produced a more serious deterioration in the Soviet position. Better weapons might therefore raise the cost of occupation without immediately making withdrawal more likely. The analysts could identify weaknesses in Soviet control without accurately predicting how the leadership would respond to them.

[Reagan’s March 1985 directive, NSDD 166](https://www.reaganlibrary.gov/public/archives/reference/scanned-nsdds/nsdd166.pdf), set out the American aims. The ultimate goal was Soviet withdrawal and an independent Afghanistan. Intermediate objectives included worsening Moscow’s prospects, denying it a secure regional base, maintaining international pressure and improving the resistance’s military effectiveness. Assistance was to demonstrate that the United States would oppose Soviet intervention while pursuing arms-control negotiations elsewhere.

The directive also dealt with what happened beyond the battlefield. Supplies were to be tracked, receipt and combat use confirmed, and corruption limited. Resistance-run social services were encouraged for humanitarian reasons and to sustain civilian support. Officials were to promote political coordination where possible, while recognizing that a unified resistance movement was unrealistic.

The instructions asked for progress that the supply system could measure and for cooperation it could only encourage. Confirming that a commander received ammunition could help establish military effectiveness. It could not establish that he would later share power with another commander. Cooperation with Pakistan kept the route open while limiting Washington’s freedom to reshape it.

## What the Stinger changed

The Stinger gave small teams an effective portable weapon against aircraft and helicopters. Its introduction in September 1986 was an important escalation: recognizable American military technology was reaching the battlefield. The CIA’s institutional account highlights an early engagement in which mujahideen shot down three Soviet helicopter gunships. For fighters exposed to air attack, a weapon that could threaten those aircraft had immediate value.

The Soviet leadership’s effort to leave the war had begun before the Stinger arrived. In October 1985, Gorbachev was telling Karmal that Soviet soldiers would not remain indefinitely. Adviser Anatoly Chernyaev’s diary records demands that the Afghan government broaden its support, stop treating socialism as an immediate objective and learn to defend itself.

In June 1986, Gorbachev was again discussing withdrawal and the need to avoid making it look like a humiliating defeat. In November, after the Stinger’s arrival, the Politburo debated a timetable and the government that Soviet forces would leave behind. Marshal Sergei Akhromeyev described the underlying failure: troops could pass through an area without securing lasting control once they departed.

Air defenses could increase the cost of operations within a war the Soviet leadership was already trying to leave. Withdrawal also depended on diplomatic arrangements, Kabul’s survival, relations with Pakistan and the United States, and Moscow’s willingness to accept an imperfect result. The missiles added pressure during the difficult process of turning an intention to withdraw into an actual departure.

They also created a problem after their immediate purpose had passed. Weapons distributed among commanders were difficult to recover. American attempts to buy Stingers back began in the 1990s, well before September 11. The concern was that portable anti-aircraft weapons might be transferred to other conflicts or used against civilian aircraft. Equipment acquired to improve the resistance’s freedom of action had become equipment Washington wanted to control again.

## Withdrawal left the war in place

The withdrawal of the Soviet army was completed in February 1989. Najibullah’s government remained in power. Soviet material support continued, American support for the resistance continued, and Afghan forces kept fighting. The end of the foreign troop presence was a major change within the conflict, rather than its conclusion.

In September 1991, Washington and Moscow agreed to stop military assistance to the combatants, effective at the start of 1992. The loss of Soviet backing and defections from Najibullah’s coalition helped bring his government down in April 1992. Mujahideen leaders entered Kabul without a durable agreement capable of containing their rival ambitions. Fighting between factions devastated the capital. Hekmatyar shelled a government in which he was nominally prime minister; Rabbani and Massoud defended their position; other forces pursued their own claims.

The Taliban emerged as a new movement in the early 1990s and captured Kandahar in 1994. Some participants had fought in the earlier war, but the movement was not simply the mujahideen under a new name. It recruited from a country and refugee population exhausted by conflict and gained power through fighting, defections and outside backing. It displaced former resistance leaders as well as drawing on parts of their world. Pakistani support for the Taliban represented a continuing search for influence through Afghan partners.

American attention declined after the Soviet withdrawal. Military support ended; humanitarian assistance continued. Diplomatic efforts sought a settlement, and American officials later tried to influence the Taliban over terrorism, narcotics and the treatment of women. After the USAID mission closed in 1994, assistance continued largely through international agencies and nongovernmental organizations. Relief could reach people without empowering an agreed Afghan government. Those activities did not supply the political agreement or reconstruction needed to end the fighting. Nor did ending the CIA program end the resources, organizations and foreign relationships sustained during the war.

## Where the networks went

The conflict created conditions in which narcotics could finance armed power. A later SIGAR study described devastation of agriculture, growth of illicit networks and mujahideen reliance on opium revenue. Opium could sustain an armed organization alongside foreign assistance, giving its leaders an income that aid donors did not control.

The Senate’s BCCI investigation documented extensive criminal and intelligence relationships. Its discussion of possible Afghan financing through the National Bank of Oman cited journalistic accounts; it did not reconstruct every Cyclone payment. Its evidence leaves a narrower account of this route than the familiar claim that BCCI banked the entire war. The [[bcci-nugan-hand]] and [[cia-drugs]] essays examine the relevant evidence more closely.

Foreign volunteers formed another consequence of the war. Abdullah Azzam and Osama bin Laden helped organize recruitment, accommodation and support through a services network in Pakistan. Arab volunteers acquired contacts, experience and a powerful account of what their participation had achieved. Some later carried those relationships into other conflicts and into al-Qaeda.

[The 9/11 Commission](https://9-11commission.gov/report/911Report_Ch2.htm) described bin Laden’s circle as having its own sources of support and training and receiving little or no American assistance. The American-Saudi program and the Arab volunteer networks occupied an overlapping environment, with relationships to some of the same hosts and causes. Bin Laden could benefit from the war’s opportunities while raising money and organizing fighters for purposes of his own.

There were further turns after the anti-Soviet war: bin Laden’s break with the Saudi monarchy, his years in Sudan, his return to Afghanistan and his relationship with the Taliban. The attacks of September 11 belong to that later history as well. Treating them as the programmed outcome of a 1979 finding removes the decisions through which former allies, independent extremists and governments changed their relationships.

Cyclone helped a resistance survive and made an occupation costly. The program’s own instructions recognized that military support needed political coordination and civilian backing. Its partners nevertheless found it easier to organize deliveries than to agree on who should hold power after the war.

That was the limit the Soviet withdrawal exposed. Weapons had helped prevent one side from imposing a settlement. They also strengthened several sides that expected to shape the next one. When the common enemy departed, those organizations still had fighters, patrons and reasons to refuse one another’s terms.
