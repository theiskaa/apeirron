In September 1969, the student newspaper at Drake University asked whether Paul McCartney was dead. Its evidence was available to anyone with the records: pictures on album sleeves, fragments of lyrics, sounds heard when a track ran backward. The article ended by inviting readers to send more information.

The invitation gave a reader a part in the story. McCartney had supposedly died in a car crash in 1966; the Beatles had replaced him and concealed their grief in their work. A listener could help uncover the deception without leaving the bedroom. Every new record was another place to look.

The story was false. Its history is still worth examining because some of the evidence has an unusually clear provenance. A student who helped spread it later explained which parts he had invented. McCartney gave an interview about the details being used against his identity. The records and photographs remain available. We can follow how a joke, an observation, and an inference became difficult to tell apart.

## A rumor looking for contributors

Tim Harper’s September 17 article in the *Drake Times-Delphic*, [preserved by the university library](https://content.library.drake.edu/digital/collection/p15183coll1/id/701/), shows that the story was circulating before its famous October radio outbreak. Harper assembled interpretations of *Sgt. Pepper*, *Magical Mystery Tour*, and the White Album. A flower arrangement could be a grave; a turned back could signify absence; a hand above a head could become a death symbol. He also reported a proposed replacement: McCartney’s brother.

The article also carried a more elaborate promise. One story involved telephoning at a particular time and winning a Beatles island in the Mediterranean. Harper expressed uncertainty about where the whole thing had begun. The closing editor’s note solicited further information, treating the newspaper as a meeting place for a developing mystery.

Contributions collected under this invitation would tend to favor the rumor. A reader responding to that invitation knew the kind of contribution wanted. An unusual detail fitted the column; an ordinary sleeve passed over without comment. The archive contains the selections that made a story, with little trace of all the details that failed to become interesting.

Harper’s range of material gave a contributor several ways into the story. These were records people had already studied closely and cared about. An album sleeve could be held, turned over, and compared with the previous release. Listeners knew changes in the voices and arrangements. Looking again was pleasurable before a rumor gave the looking a purpose. The death story redirected an existing intimacy with the work.

It also offered a way to connect the Beatles’ changing appearance to a single event. Compare any two photographs taken several years apart and there will be differences: hair, weight, expression, clothes, age. A replacement theory treats the differences as the event’s residue. It can then absorb resemblance just as readily, since resemblance is the quality for which a replacement would have been selected. Both kinds of observation begin to favor the same conclusion.

## The reviewer who supplied a replacement

On October 12, a caller raised McCartney’s supposed death on Russ Gibb’s WKNR-FM program in Detroit. Fred LaBour heard the broadcast while preparing to review *Abbey Road* for the *Michigan Daily*. His piece appeared on October 14, two days later.

In a [2009 University of Michigan interview with Alan Glenn](https://www.stnorberts.com/paul_is_dead_michigan_today.htm), LaBour described laying out Beatles albums and making things up. He supplied a Scottish orphan named William Campbell as the replacement. He also invented the claim that *walrus* meant *corpse* in Greek. These details subsequently circulated as discoveries about the Beatles.

LaBour had given an album review the shape of an exposé. The distinction was easily lost when a claim traveled farther than its author. A reader could encounter William Campbell through a radio conversation or someone else’s account, without seeing the college paper or knowing what kind of piece had produced him. A name made the proposed double sound independently identifiable. Repetition supplied the appearance of corroboration.

LaBour’s later recollection includes an especially revealing encounter. Before appearing in a television special hosted by lawyer F. Lee Bailey, he said he told Bailey he had invented his story. In LaBour’s account, the show still had its allotted time to fill and proceeded. This is a recollection given decades later, rather than a transcript of that conversation. It nevertheless describes a practical reason a rumor could remain in circulation after a participant had withdrawn its supposed evidence.

A program could be entertaining without reaching a defensible conclusion. Discussion of a clue gave the audience something to hear and debate; explaining that its originator had made it up threatened to end that discussion. The format rewarded continued inquiry even when the relevant inquiry had reached an answer.

LaBour’s admission does not establish that every person repeating the story knew it was false. Someone could knowingly embellish a joke, someone else could repeat it as a possibility, and a third could receive it as a report. By the time they compared accounts, each might honestly describe a different encounter with the same claim.

A person convinced by the clues could discard a particular invention while retaining the impressions it helped create. William Campbell may disappear from an account, but a suspicion that there must have been *some* double can survive him. The withdrawn detail has already done part of its work.

## A walk across a street

The *Abbey Road* sleeve offered a particularly economical story. Four men crossed a street, and a funeral could be read into their clothes: John Lennon in white, Ringo Starr in a dark suit, McCartney barefoot, George Harrison in denim. Once those appearances were assigned roles in a procession, the photograph seemed to depict an event hidden beneath an ordinary walk.

The difficulty begins with that assignment. Clothes do not label a priest, an undertaker, a corpse, and a gravedigger by themselves. The proposed funeral supplies those identities. A person who accepts the identities can then point to the clothes as support for the funeral. The same interpretation has been counted twice.

There is also a photographic sequence outside the chosen cover. The Beatles’ [official account of the session](https://www.thebeatles.com/abbey-road-anniversary-editions-out-now) dates it to August 8, 1969. Photographer Iain Macmillan took six pictures as the group crossed back and forth, with a policeman holding the traffic. The fifth became the sleeve. That production history gives the image a more ordinary setting: a small selection from repeated walks made for a photograph.

Selecting one frame is normal album design. It also leaves room for an interpreter to treat every feature of that frame as a deliberate message. The position of a foot, the hand holding an object, and the order of bodies become equally intentional. A theory can demand much greater control over a photograph than the circumstances of making it required.

The parked Volkswagen’s plate, read by enthusiasts as “28IF,” supplied a numerical clue: McCartney would supposedly have been twenty-eight *if* he had lived. An [Associated Press report printed on October 23, 1969](https://archive.lib.msu.edu/DMC/state_news/1969/state_news_19691023.pdf) already pointed out the problem: he was twenty-seven, not twenty-eight. The discrepancy was being reported during the rumor’s outbreak. Repairing the clue requires adding a rule about age or symbolism that the plate itself never supplies. The clue survives only by moving away from the simple calculation that made it attractive.

A code ought to constrain an answer. Given its rules, another person should be able to derive the same result and recognize an incorrect one. A method that changes its rules whenever a result fails gives the solver much more freedom. Its flexibility can feel like depth because a further explanation is always available.

The appeal of the sleeve is understandable. It is a strong image with an orderly composition, and people naturally ask why its parts were chosen. Art can contain jokes, allusions, and deliberate symbols. The leap occurs when the possibility of artistic intention is made to carry a specific historical claim about a death, a substituted identity, and years of cooperation by other people.

## Listening after being told what to hear

The auditory clues offered an experience a printed correction could struggle to dislodge. A listener was told that the repeated “number nine” on “Revolution 9” became “Turn me on, dead man” when reversed. Listening again could produce the apparent words. Someone who heard them might feel that the record had confirmed the story personally.

Yet the sound and the account of its origin are separate matters. Reversing recorded speech preserves some qualities of speech while disrupting the usual relations among consonants, vowels, and word boundaries. A suggested phrase gives a listener a way to organize the result. The experience can be sincere even when nobody recorded that phrase backward.

Psychologist John Vokey discusses this problem in his [chapter on subliminal messages](https://courses.edx.org/c4x/UQx/Think101x/asset/VokeySubliminalMessages.pdf), drawing on his research with J. Don Read. He distinguishes ambiguous reversals from deliberately engineered ones, including jokes that musicians really inserted. He also separates hearing an apparent message in reversed playback from the stronger claim that a backward message controls a listener who hears a record normally. He reports that their experiments found no effect of the meanings of engineered backward messages on the tested behavior.

Hearing an apparent phrase could convince a listener that it had been planted. But even a deliberate backward message would leave its purpose to be established. Musicians can play a joke in a studio. McCartney’s supposed death would still be a claim about a man outside it.

A simple listening comparison makes the first difficulty easier to understand. Let one person hear a reversal without instructions and write down anything recognizable. Tell another the proposed phrase first. Their reports address different conditions: one person is searching for words; the other has words against which to fit a sound. Agreement among people who have all received the same prompt is less informative than it initially feels.

The pleasure of hearing the message also matters. It turns an unfamiliar noise into something suddenly legible. After that moment, going back to uncertainty can be difficult: the phrase remains available on every replay. A written account saying that the meaning is supplied partly by expectation may be accurate while failing to reproduce the force of the original experience.

The connection with [[predictive-programming]] lies in this practice of decoding media. The claims differ in what the supposed messages are doing. A concealed confession about a past death, an audience-conditioning device, and an artist’s joke are different proposals, however similar the act of searching may look.

## What would identify a replacement?

The theory’s larger claim is unusually demanding. It proposes that another person continued a public identity through work, family relationships, performance, and ordinary encounters. Its central evidence ought therefore to concern the identity of those people and the circumstances of the alleged substitution.

Album clues are poorly suited to that task. Even if an image could be shown to represent a funeral deliberately, that would leave open whom it represented, why it was included, and whether it referred to an actual event. An artist can depict a death without documenting one. The interpretation has several steps left to travel before reaching an identified man in an identified accident.

The same problem arises in comparisons of photographs. Two pictures can establish that a face looks different in those pictures. Establishing two people requires accounting for how the pictures were made and what changed between them. Pose and expression are part of what is being compared; they cannot simply be treated as fixed measurements of a head.

A 2009 example has sometimes been presented as a scientific paper on the identity question. A *Wired* magazine feature by Fabio Andriola and Alessandra Gigante described photographic work by Gabriella Carlesi and Francesco Gavazzeni. The [authors’ reprint of the feature’s opening](https://storiainrete.com/chiedi-chi-era-quel-beatle-da-wired-di-luglio-agosto-2009/) describes selecting images and comparing facial landmarks. It was a magazine feature about their work, not the forensic journal paper that some accounts make it.

The opening describes a reported investigation; the linked continuation is no longer available. That surviving text cannot stand in for a complete method and set of measurements. Before accepting its conclusion, another examiner would need to know which photographs were admitted, how they were aligned, and how much variation the method allows in pictures of the same person.

The photographic argument also has an escape available. A mismatch can count as evidence of substitution, while a match is explained by the alleged double’s resemblance or surgery. Once both outcomes are accommodated, a comparison can no longer choose between the people it supposedly distinguishes.

A serious identity inquiry would follow a different trail: an accident record, a named victim, the proposed double’s earlier life, or independently supported testimony about the substitution. These are difficult things to manufacture consistently because they involve other people’s records and memories. A symbol on a sleeve can be interpreted anew by each person who encounters it; an alleged replacement’s birth and employment history have to refer to an actual life. The claim would become more concrete by following those connections.

The death story fails in its positive case, including the known invention of central details. Rejecting it does not require finding a single compulsory interpretation for every unusual sound or photograph.

## The man the story displaced

There were public answers before the *Life* cover. The October 23 Associated Press story relayed a denial through Apple, the Beatles’ business organization, and included Iain Macmillan’s account of the photograph. The photographer described a hot day and a parked car with no connection to the band. Readers were receiving mundane explanations alongside the rumors, rather than waiting years for a debunking.

McCartney answered some of the sleeve claims himself when *Life* reached him in Scotland. In the interview published in the November 7, 1969 issue, [reproduced with the contemporary text](https://www.the-paulmccartney-project.com/interview/the-case-of-the-mising-beatles-paul-is-still-with-us/), he explained the bare feet as a response to the heat and the Volkswagen as a car that happened to be there. He identified the badge used in another clue as Canadian police insignia.

He also described withdrawing from press attention and wanting time with his family. His account of the Beatles emphasized the members’ separate lives and marriages. These were changes he was willing to acknowledge, without accepting the story constructed around them.

A replacement theory can absorb such an interview immediately: naturally the replacement would deny being a replacement. At that point, appearing in public and staying out of public view both become suspicious. The man can neither answer the claim nor take time away from answering it. His conduct has ceased to be capable of changing the conclusion.

There was a real distance for fans to notice. A person they knew through records could become less available, and a group they recognized could change. The death story made that distance literal. It supplied a clean break and a secret reason where the public was receiving partial views of several people’s changing lives.

That cultural interpretation does not establish a coordinated effort to divert the counterculture. The connection to [[counterculture-psyop]] is a question about how political movements remember their losses and disappointments. The surviving campus articles and LaBour’s account already show people spreading this particular story for reasons close at hand. An intelligence origin would require a further historical connection, rather than merely a political use someone might imagine for the rumor.

McCartney eventually made the story part of his own packaging. His 1993 concert album [*Paul Is Live*](https://www.paulmccartney.com/discography/albums/paul-is-live) returned to the crossing with shoes on and his left hand conspicuous. The title made the joke plain. A man accused of communicating through hidden clues could use recognizable clues to answer back.

The older rumor had made strangers authors of his biography. An invented name could arrive as evidence after passing through a few conversations; its author’s admission could fail to catch up. The story kept offering people something to find. McCartney could keep making music, but he could not supply an ending to a search whose participants had learned to turn an answer into another clue.
