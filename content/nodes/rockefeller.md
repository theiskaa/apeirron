In 1911, the Supreme Court ordered the Standard Oil combination dissolved. The judges wanted to break the arrangement through which separate companies had been brought under common control. They also said the law was meant to protect property.

Shares in the subsidiaries were to leave the New Jersey company's control, and their owners were forbidden to recreate the illegal combination. The owners still had property from which they could receive income or make gifts. Rockefeller money could continue to support projects outside the oil business.

Rockefeller money was already financing education before that decision. [The General Education Board had been incorporated in 1903](https://resource.rockarch.org/story/the-general-education-board-1903-1964/), and John D. Rockefeller Sr.'s commitments to it had reached $43 million by 1907. Philanthropy therefore cannot be explained simply as a lesson the family learned after losing the antitrust case.

The money also gave people the means to establish a school, support research or assemble others around an international problem. In each case, someone chose the problem, the institution and the people. Following those choices shows how the influence worked.

## What the oil judgment changed

The New Jersey company held shares in subsidiaries. Ownership let it exercise control across businesses that retained separate names. A list of corporations could therefore conceal how few independent decisions were being made. The litigation addressed the use of that combination to restrain trade and monopolize petroleum commerce.

The [1911 opinion](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/221/1) distinguishes that combination from every possible agreement among its former components. A pipeline arrangement, for example, might serve a legitimate purpose without recreating the monopoly. The Court sought to prevent renewed unlawful combination while allowing the companies to function.

The remedy limited a way of exercising corporate power. A shareholder who retained valuable property could still sell it, receive income and make gifts. Ending coordinated industrial control did not settle how a fortune might influence education, research or politics through other relationships.

The judgment's effect on competition depended on what happened in the industry afterward. The size of the former owner's fortune could not, on its own, tell us whether the companies were acting independently.

The charters and grants of the philanthropic institutions show what people did with that continuing ability to act. They created organizations, selected purposes and made commitments under different rules from those governing an oil company.

## A charter for choosing purposes

The Rockefeller Foundation was incorporated in New York in May 1913. Its [original charter](https://www.rockefellerfoundation.org/wp-content/uploads/Rockefeller-Foundation-Charter.pdf) authorized a fund serving human well-being throughout the world through research, education and other charitable activities. Its trustees could spend income and principal in pursuit of those purposes.

A broad purpose had practical value. It allowed the institution to support work its founders could not have specified in advance. A fund confined to one disease or one building might become less useful as circumstances changed. Discretion let trustees respond to new knowledge.

The same discretion posed a public question. Who would decide what counted as improving human well-being, and who would select the people making that decision? [The foundation's early annual reports preserve an account](https://www.rockefellerfoundation.org/wp-content/uploads/Annual-Report-1913-1914-1.pdf) of the unsuccessful effort to obtain a federal charter before incorporation in New York.

According to that account, public discussion of the proposal brought demands for limits on accumulated funds, the selection of successors and the institution's duration, as well as stronger congressional control. Revised proposals included a mechanism allowing designated public officials and university presidents to reject new members. These were attempts to give a continuing private institution an outside check.

The foundation reported that the House passed the revised bill in January 1913, but the Senate did not bring it to a vote before the session ended. The organizers then obtained the New York charter. This is the foundation's contemporary account of the process, including its own explanation of why the federal route stalled.

The proposed check addressed appointments. Designated officials and university presidents would have sixty days to disapprove a new member of the governing corporation. They would not thereby become the staff deciding each grant. The arrangement would have put an outside limit on who could join the governing body while leaving much of its programmatic discretion intact.

The university presidents also complicate a simple division between public oversight and private judgment. Academic leaders were among the proposed guardians, alongside national officeholders. The bill addressed continuity of control by deciding who could check appointments; it did not turn every future choice into a decision by voters.

The actual charter also prohibited officers, members and employees from receiving private profits from its operations, apart from permitted compensation and charitable benefits. Donating property to such a body was not the same as keeping it in a personal spending account. The institution had a charitable legal purpose, even though its trustees retained substantial choice over how to pursue it.

Trustees could direct resources without owning them as personal wealth. Their selection, their programs and their grant conditions could still shape institutions that served the public.

## A grant can carry a design

In medical education, Abraham Flexner's *Medical Education in the United States and Canada* appeared in 1910 as a report to the Carnegie Foundation for the Advancement of Teaching. It was not commissioned by the Rockefeller Foundation, which did not yet exist.

Flexner examined schools whose resources and standards varied widely. His report criticized inadequate preparation, teaching facilities and clinical instruction. He wanted medical education connected to laboratories, hospitals and universities capable of supporting it. A recommendation to improve training, however, did not itself supply the money required to do so.

Rockefeller-funded bodies could help bridge that gap. The General Education Board and later the Rockefeller Foundation supported the changes through grants. As [Teresa Iacobelli's archive-based account explains](https://resource.rockarch.org/story/early-20th-century-reforms-of-medical-education-worldwide/), funded arrangements included relationships with hospitals and teaching staff under university control, working full time rather than dividing their duties with private practice.

Those conditions changed an institution. A university could not necessarily accept the money and carry on exactly as before. It might need to alter appointments, acquire facilities or assume responsibilities previously left to individual doctors. The grant helped make a particular model possible and gave that model an advantage over alternatives.

Students needed patients to examine, laboratories in which to work and teachers available to teach. A school could collect fees and issue degrees while providing little of this. Better facilities and clinical instruction addressed a deficiency in what students were being taught.

Meeting the standard required resources that many schools lacked. Donors had to decide which would receive help. A school with an established university behind it might be better placed to obtain a grant than a poorly funded institution serving people excluded elsewhere. A standard can be general while the means of meeting it are unevenly distributed.

The 1916 selection of Johns Hopkins for a school of public health makes the discretion visible. The survey considered Hopkins alongside Columbia, Harvard and the University of Pennsylvania. Iacobelli describes a report acknowledging advantages elsewhere, including supporting departments and city health services, while recommending Hopkins for the potential of its medical school and its full-time arrangements.

The surveyors were choosing where they thought a new school could develop. Selecting one institution meant putting money, staff and prestige behind its future. A university that became a model could train people who carried that model into other institutions.

The public-health emphasis also complicates the claim that Rockefeller medical giving was simply a plan to make medicine dependent on drug sales. The work included sanitation, prevention, bacteriology and preparation for public service. Those activities have to be included in an account of the funded program. A commercial motive cannot be established just by pointing to laboratory science.

## Whose education was worth supporting?

Flexner's treatment of Black medical education shows how much else could enter such a judgment. In chapter XIV, he recommended concentrating support on Howard and Meharry rather than sustaining all seven schools then serving Black students. He regarded the other five as inadequately equipped and trained.

[His account was also explicitly racial](https://centerforinquiry.s3.amazonaws.com/wp-content/uploads/sites/33/2021/04/22173657/flexner_1910.pdf). It described Black doctors chiefly as serving Black communities and emphasized their work in sanitation, including the protection of neighboring white populations from disease. He acknowledged individual rights while placing education within a hierarchy of expected social roles. Improving facilities did not require those assumptions, but they appeared together in his program.

This matters to the meaning of efficiency. Concentrating funds might improve the laboratories and clinical opportunities of selected schools. It could also narrow the places in which excluded students had a realistic chance to train. Counting institutions that met a new standard would not, on its own, measure the opportunities lost to people served by those that did not.

The report itself was a recommendation, not a law commanding closures. Its effects operated alongside licensing, university decisions, professional organization and the availability of money. Treating every subsequent closure as an order personally issued by Rockefeller would skip the work of identifying those decisions.

The General Education Board's support for Meharry began in 1916. Iacobelli records roughly $8 million over the following thirty years. Funding a Black medical institution was therefore part of the same philanthropic history that must be examined for unequal allocation and racial assumptions.

Meharry's support belongs in the account alongside Flexner's restrictive vision. Together they require an examination of what opportunities the choices created, for whom, and at what cost. A favorable total for charitable spending cannot answer it without attention to the institutions and people included.

## Funding heredity and eugenics

Rockefeller funding also reached German institutions and researchers involved in heredity and racial classification. Those grants supported a field whose claims about human worth could be used to justify coercion.

[Historian Egbert Klautke's account identifies support](https://discovery.ucl.ac.uk/id/eprint/1471934/3/Eugenics%20USA%20Germany%2010-2015%20HARVARD%20REVISED%20FINAL.pdf) for the Kaiser Wilhelm Institute for Anthropology, Human Heredity and Eugenics in Berlin. He describes a grant for a project on German racial structure and annual payments of $25,000 from 1930 to 1934, which helped sustain the institute during the economic crisis. He also discusses support for von Verschuer's twin studies.

The dates cross the Nazi accession to power in 1933. They require attention to what the funder supported, how it evaluated the work and what happened as political conditions changed. The word *research* supplied no guarantee that a program's categories or purposes were benign.

A grant can do more than purchase the immediate study named in its description. It can help keep an institute open, sustain a career and make a field appear worthy of professional attention. Those effects belong to the responsibility of funding, even where the donor did not direct each later action of a recipient.

Klautke also resists a simple account in which Nazi racial policy was copied whole from America. His history describes exchanges within an international eugenics movement, alongside German intellectual, legal and political developments. That distinction preserves responsibility on both sides. It neither erases American support nor makes German policy makers passive recipients of someone else's instructions.

The relevant criticism of philanthropy is therefore specific. Funding decisions helped sustain research institutions whose work included racial hierarchy. Examining a renewal, a project's terms or a warning received by foundation staff could establish what the institution knew and chose at a particular moment. A complete chain of responsibility requires those records; the documented support is already substantial enough to demand examination.

A well-equipped institute could conduct methodical work within categories that degraded the people it studied. Supporting the equipment and the staff could help those categories acquire scientific authority.

## A later generation's access

The family continued into banking, elected office, charitable work and policy organizations. Those activities were not all governed by the 1913 charter. A later Rockefeller's public role needs to be identified rather than treated as another department of the founder's original business.

[David Rockefeller chaired the Council on Foreign Relations from 1970 to 1985](https://www.cfr.org/board-directors). The Council itself had been founded in 1921, decades before his chairmanship. He also helped found the Trilateral Commission in 1973, bringing together participants from [North America, Western Europe and Japan](https://jcie.org/programs/trilateral-commission/). The two roles gave him opportunities in organizations with different histories.

Such organizations gave a private citizen opportunities to convene people with influence over policy. A meeting could introduce a proposal, establish a relationship or build agreement around a problem. The agenda and the invitations mattered because time and attention were limited. Having the resources and standing to assemble the room was a form of power.

It was different from the power of a public office. A policy group could advocate an approach without possessing authority to enact it. If a proposal subsequently became government policy, the explanation would need to include the officials who adopted it, their powers and the choices made in between.

The same applies to an internationalist's declared wish for more cooperation. Support for a more integrated world is a political position that can be examined and opposed. It is not, by itself, proof of a command structure secretly directing every state. The [[cfr-trilateral]] organizations can be examined through the people they assembled, the work they produced and the paths by which that work reached officials.

A donor's ability to convene people resembles a foundation's ability to select a program in one respect: both can shape what receives serious attention before a public decision is made. Neither guarantees the final outcome. The influence lies partly in which options become developed enough for others to consider.

## The decision inside the gift

Rockefeller philanthropy helped create durable institutions and opportunities for work. The charter controversy, medical grants and eugenics funding also show why size and good intention are inadequate measures of a gift. The choice of recipient and the terms of support can have consequences beyond the project initially described.

A public judgment should be able to recognize improved teaching and useful public-health work while examining racial exclusions and harmful research. That requires more than an accounting of money distributed. It requires knowing who could propose a program, whose needs it recognized, what conditions it imposed and what the funder did when consequences became apparent.

The 1911 oil judgment could dismantle an unlawful combination. It could not choose a future medical school's curriculum or decide whether an institute's theory of heredity deserved support. Those decisions occurred elsewhere, under different rules. Following them shows how a fortune could continue to shape public life without requiring every recipient to obey a secret order.

The discretion was often exercised before the institution opened or the research began. Selecting a school for sustained support could give its curriculum a future. Selecting an inquiry could give it staff, equipment and time. The gift made work possible; its terms helped decide which work would endure.
