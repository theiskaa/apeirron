In April 1983, a software company sent the Justice Department computer tapes containing a program called PROMIS. The department was already using the program through telephone connections to the company's computers. Now it would hold a copy of the code. The accompanying agreement was supposed to let work continue while a dispute about ownership was resolved.

The dispute lasted for years. Inslaw, the company, accused the department of taking improvements it had paid to develop and then using them without permission. A bankruptcy judge agreed in unusually forceful language. Congress investigated. Later courts and departmental inquiries reached conclusions sharply at odds with that first judgment.

Around this difficult contract history grew another account: the software had been stolen, fitted with a concealed means of access, and distributed to intelligence services abroad. Freelance writer Danny Casolaro believed that it led into a larger network of covert operations, financial crime, and political influence. He called that network the Octopus. His death in 1991 made the investigation he had not finished seem still more urgent.

The tapes tie the contract dispute to the intelligence allegations. Following them means identifying the delivered version, the rights that accompanied it, and any later copy said to have reached an unauthorized recipient.

## A system for keeping track

PROMIS stood for Prosecutor's Management Information System. It helped an office follow cases through the criminal justice process. The government's [catalog description of a 1971 system overview](https://www.ojp.gov/ncjrs/virtual-library/abstracts/system-overview-and-report-formats-promis-prosecutors-management) describes calendars, notifications, resource allocation, research, and coordination between agencies. These were practical responses to the difficulty of keeping people, charges, hearings, and decisions connected as a case moved between desks.

Imagine a prosecutor who needs to know which witnesses have been notified, which hearing is approaching, and why a case was dropped. A file may contain the answers while the office still lacks a usable picture of its work. Recording events in a consistent system makes it possible to ask questions across cases. The organizational achievement is as important as the computation: someone has to decide what gets recorded, keep it current, and give each entry a meaning that other staff understand.

The name did not even always mean a fully computerized installation. A [1976 handbook's abstract](https://www.ojp.gov/ncjrs/virtual-library/abstracts/promis-prosecutors-management-information-system-nonautomated-or) describes manual and partly automated versions for smaller offices. They offered case accountability, witness management, performance reports, and priorities through forms and procedures suited to the resources available.

Making records usable across an office could confer considerable power. That depended on people entering and organizing information, with access to the records and an understanding of their structure. The people installing and maintaining the program remained part of its operation.

An intelligence service could find case-management technology useful. A concealed access mechanism could also make an otherwise legitimate product dangerous. But those are additional capabilities and uses. They have to be demonstrated in a particular version, installation, or operation. The original administrative purpose explains why governments might buy such a system; it does not establish that buying it exposed their files to another country.

## The improvements and the contract

Inslaw's earlier nonprofit predecessor had developed PROMIS with public funding. The private company, led by William and Nancy Hamilton, maintained that it had subsequently financed valuable enhancements of its own. The government and company agreed that the old version was in the public domain. Their disagreement concerned the improvements and the rights attached to the software delivered under a 1982 federal contract.

The contract was worth approximately $9.6 million. The temporary agreement known as Modification 12 followed a government request for a copy of the software being used. Inslaw delivered source code, which programmers could read and modify, as well as the machine-oriented form needed to run it. The [1991 appellate opinion](https://law.justia.com/cases/federal/appellate-courts/F2/932/1467/288947/) reconstructs this sequence. Inslaw entered Chapter 11 bankruptcy in February 1985, seeking to reorganize rather than liquidate.

Versions had become entangled. A public-funded program could acquire privately funded additions. A version used to provide a service from the company's computers could then be installed on government machines. An improvement could be valuable without being outside the government's contractual rights. Conversely, a customer could possess a legitimate copy without having permission to reproduce every enhancement wherever it wished.

The words *the software* conceal those differences. A claim about ownership must identify the disputed material and the terms governing it. The Hamiltons believed the department had exploited the delivery arrangements to obtain something it had not purchased. The department maintained that its contract entitled it to what it received. Their disagreement was therefore about the meaning of the bargain as well as the origin of the code.

Consider an ordinary program that a vendor owns but licenses to a customer for use in every branch office. The customer may be allowed to make copies while being forbidden to sell the program to outsiders. Ownership, reproduction, distribution, and alteration are separate rights. An investigator who finds a copy in an unexpected office still has to ask whether the license permitted it. An unauthorized sale would raise another question again. Much of the force of the PROMIS accusation depends on which of those acts is being alleged.

This was no minor problem for a small vendor. If the principal customer disputes the value and ownership of its central product, the business must finance both its ordinary work and the effort to establish its rights. Bankruptcy protection can preserve the company while leaving the dispute unresolved. The fact that a company struggles under those conditions deserves explanation; it cannot by itself determine which contractual interpretation is correct.

## The judgment that became the story

Bankruptcy judge George Bason heard the dispute in 1987 and issued written [findings in January 1988](https://law.justia.com/cases/federal/district-courts/BR/83/89/1845806/). He accepted that Inslaw had created proprietary enhancements with private funds and found serious misconduct by Justice officials. His conclusions described the department obtaining the software through “trickery, fraud and deceit.” The district court subsequently upheld the judgment in substantial part.

The language came from a judge who had heard witnesses and examined records, rather than from an anonymous account of intelligence intrigue. It made the case easy to summarize: a court had found that the department charged with enforcing the law had taken a company's property.

The legal basis mattered. In bankruptcy, an automatic stay protects the debtor's estate from certain actions by creditors and others. Bason treated the department's continued use of the claimed enhancements as an unlawful exercise of control over estate property. In 1991, the appellate court rejected that application of bankruptcy law and directed that the relevant orders be vacated.

Its reasoning did not establish that every accusation of misconduct was false. It held that a disputed contract claim could not be brought within this bankruptcy power in the way Inslaw had attempted. The department already possessed copies under its asserted contractual rights before the bankruptcy filing. Resolving those disputed rights required an appropriate proceeding, rather than treating resistance to Inslaw's position as a violation of the stay.

A vacated judgment also could not remain a binding finding of theft. The phrase *never reversed on the merits* is often used to keep the first judgment intact in public discussion. It explains what the appellate court did not decide; it does not confer continuing legal force on the orders the court set aside. A reader needs both parts of that history.

There was more history to come. The case did not end with the appeals court, leaving everyone forever unable to examine the substantive dispute. Congress eventually referred Inslaw's demand for compensation to the Court of Federal Claims. That later proceeding is essential to understanding what became of the ownership allegation.

## Congress and its dissenters

The House Judiciary Committee's [September 1992 report](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/SERIALSET-14137_00_00-003-0857-0000/pdf/SERIALSET-14137_00_00-003-0857-0000.pdf) supported the earlier courts' account of misappropriation and sharply criticized the department's cooperation. It described missing documents and difficulties obtaining information. It recommended compensation, further investigation, and an independent counsel. It also called for more investigation of Casolaro's death.

The majority did not claim to have conclusively established the whole international conspiracy. It treated several allegations as serious leads whose resolution had been impeded. That distinction can disappear when a report's account of what a witness alleged is quoted as Congress's finding that it happened.

The missing-records problem was real within the inquiry. A committee cannot reconstruct every decision if the organization being examined cannot produce a complete file. Missing documents may also prevent an accusation from being resolved in the accused officials' favor. The appropriate consequence is scrutiny of the loss and the surviving records. The absence alone cannot identify the lost document's contents.

The report included extensive Republican dissent. Tom Campbell's separate statement challenged the use of the vacated bankruptcy findings as established facts. He also addressed the weight given to Elliot Richardson, the former attorney general who represented Inslaw. Campbell expressed high regard for Richardson while observing that an attorney's forceful argument for a client was not firsthand evidence of the underlying events.

Richardson's involvement made it harder to dismiss the Hamiltons as people without serious representation. It did not give him personal knowledge of every disputed transaction. Similarly, a congressional majority's alarm warrants attention without turning a disputed affidavit into a forensic examination of software.

The disagreement over Bason's failed reappointment illustrates the report's limits. His replacement by a Justice Department lawyer appeared troubling to those who believed the department had retaliated against him. The majority examined the selection process but could not substantiate his allegation that Justice had improperly controlled it. The suspicion outlasted that finding.

Congress was conducting oversight, while the courts were deciding claims within legal rules. Neither activity could replace the other. An oversight inquiry could identify unanswered questions even after litigation failed. A court could reject a claim despite a committee's strong belief that the claimant had been wronged. The later court proceeding supplied another method: inspection of the programs alleged to have been copied.

## Looking for the code

The Court of Federal Claims proceeding included a way to test allegations that other agencies possessed derivatives of PROMIS. The parties helped select a three-member expert panel. Hearing officer Christine Miller's [1997 report](https://openjurist.org/39/fedcl/307/inslaw-inc-v-united-states) lists the examined systems at the FBI, Customs, DEA, NSA, and Defense Intelligence Agency. The experts reported no evidence that those programs were copied or derived from Inslaw's program. The initial request had also included the CIA; the report’s actual examination list should not be enlarged to match that request.

The protocol gave an affidavit a specific job. A statement asserting firsthand knowledge could open the way to examination of an identified system. The subsequent technical comparison then had to establish whether the system shared the claimed ancestry. A witness's assertion and the analysis of the software were separate stages of the inquiry.

Programs designed for similar work may offer similar screens, categories, or reports without sharing code. Code, development history, and distinctive implementation choices offer ways to examine whether one program actually derives from another. The negative result applied to the systems inspected; it was not an audit of every copy that might ever have existed abroad. The hearing officer also addressed older source code and documents that the government had not retained. She required explanations of their unavailability rather than assuming the surviving material provided a complete historical archive.

Even a positive match would have answered only the first technical question. Identifying a program as a PROMIS derivative would establish ancestry. Finding an unauthorized access mechanism would require examining its behavior or the relevant code. Demonstrating that someone used the mechanism would require evidence from an installation or operation. A software comparison could narrow the investigation without automatically proving the whole account of covert distribution.

Miller also heard the compensation dispute. She concluded that Inslaw had not established the proprietary rights and government wrongdoing necessary for recovery. A [three-judge review panel in May 1998](https://ecf.cofc.uscourts.gov/cofc/opinions/INSLAW.pdf) upheld the central result. This was a congressional-reference proceeding: the court was advising Congress about legal or equitable grounds for payment, rather than conducting a criminal trial of an alleged intelligence network.

The panel's explanation returned to the delivered versions. It concluded that the company's voluntary installation of enhanced software to fulfill the contract gave the government the contractual rights at issue. It also distinguished broad government usage rights from declaring the entire product public property. Private ownership and a customer's extensive license can coexist.

The judges did not accept every possible government interpretation. They agreed that a copyright license did not necessarily authorize every kind of derivative work. But Inslaw had not proved unauthorized derivatives that would support recovery. That qualification belongs beside the outcome: the report addressed particular claims, contracts, and evidence.

The company's exceptions were considered, and the resulting findings were unfavorable to it. Those proceedings make the familiar account of a surviving theft judgment erased by a lone technicality incomplete. They also leave the covert-distribution allegation facing a practical difficulty. Where a proposed link could be tested through identified software, the examined programs did not establish it.

## The backdoor allegation

The most striking claim came from Michael Riconosciuto. He said that he had worked on modifications to PROMIS in connection with the Wackenhut–Cabazon venture in California. His account connected the software to Earl Brian and claimed intelligence uses beyond the Justice Department contract. A sworn affidavit in March 1991, followed by his arrest on drug charges, gave the story an apparent sequence of disclosure and retaliation.

Former federal judge Nicholas Bua's [March 1993 special-counsel report](https://archive.org/stream/InslawPROMISBuaReport/Inslaw%3APROMIS%20Bua%20Report_djvu.txt) rejected the central conspiracy allegations. Its examination of Riconosciuto identified changes in his account of who supplied the software, whether he performed or supervised the work, and when it took place. In one version, the claimed work began before the disputed enhanced software had been delivered to Justice. Brian denied the alleged involvement.

For the alleged copy to have reached Riconosciuto through Justice, the department must first have received it. Correcting a date may sometimes resolve such a problem. It requires an explanation that can be checked, rather than treating the witness's general association with sensitive projects as confirmation of every detail.

A working backdoor would also require a means of use. An unauthorized operator might gain concealed access or cause information to be disclosed, but the program would need some actual route by which that happened. Putting a database on a computer does not automatically make its contents available elsewhere. A convincing account would identify the altered component, the installation, and the access or transmission arrangement.

The alleged resale through intermediaries such as Robert Maxwell became part of the wider PROMIS narrative. The department's [1994 review announcement](https://www.justice.gov/archive/opa/pr/Pre_96/September94/555.txt.html) rejected the claimed intelligence distribution and Maxwell-related scheme. The dispute cannot be resolved by accepting that announcement on institutional authority alone. Nor does distrust of the department supply the missing modification or sale record.

## The unfinished investigation

Casolaro tried to assemble these allegations into the Octopus. The attraction was understandable. A recurring name in a contract dispute, an arms venture, and a covert-operation story might be an important lead. It might also be a source repeating another source's allegation. Following the name requires checking the transaction each time it appears.

His proposed connections reached toward [[iran-contra]], [[bcci-nugan-hand]], and other controversies. Several of those subjects involved genuine criminal conduct or secrecy. That made the larger possibility worth investigating while making independent corroboration more valuable. Established wrongdoing in one affair could lend an unearned sense of confirmation to a weakly sourced claim in another.

Casolaro was found dead in a Martinsburg, West Virginia, hotel room on August 10, 1991. Local authorities ruled suicide. His family and others questioned the circumstances, including the speed with which his body was embalmed and the absence of research papers they expected him to have. The House majority wanted further investigation.

The Justice Department's [September 1994 review](https://archive.org/stream/InslawPROMISBuaReport/Inslaw%3APROMIS%20Bua%20Report_djvu.txt) examined the death more extensively than Bua's report had. It acknowledged mistakes, including early embalming and failure to seal the room, while maintaining that the physical and forensic evidence supported suicide. It described samples available for examination despite the embalming and disputed the assertion that papers had been established as present in the hotel and then removed. These are the review's findings, not an independent reconstruction from original scene materials.

That record supports criticism of the handling without establishing a murder. An unfinished manuscript cannot tell us what its author would have proved, and a death cannot authenticate the witnesses on whom the research relied. Casolaro's loss left people grieving and an investigation incomplete. Giving the lost work a finished conclusion would misrepresent both.

A company supplied a government customer with code. They disagreed about what had been paid for and who could use it. Courts, congressional investigators, witnesses, and technical experts then examined different parts of the dispute, with conflicting results and unequal access to the evidence.

The international backdoor story remains unestablished in the record examined here. The legal history is more complete than its usual retelling suggests. Both conclusions make it possible to return to particular objects: a contract clause, a delivered version, a changed account, a program available for comparison. An account of what happened to the program has to follow the program.
