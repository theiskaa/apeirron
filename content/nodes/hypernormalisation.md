Inna had already read the poems. What surprised her was seeing them in an official Soviet publication.

Alexei Yurchak recounts her memory in *Everything Was Forever, Until It Was No More*. Around 1987–88, Inna encountered poems by Nikolai Gumilev in *Ogonek*. She knew them through handwritten copies but had not expected to find them in the state press.

For Inna, the place of publication was the surprise. An institution whose limits seemed fixed was now allowing something Inna had expected it to exclude. Hypernormalisation begins with that problem: how familiar forms acquire an appearance of permanence, and how people live through changes within them.

Adam Curtis borrowed the term for his 2016 BBC film *HyperNormalisation*. He used it to describe a much larger retreat: politicians, financial institutions and technological visionaries constructing simplified worlds that the rest of society comes to inhabit. His subject was the distance between public accounts and the world people experience. The film proposes a history that can be examined alongside the account from which it borrows its name.

## A ritual can do more than state a belief

Yurchak’s [opening chapter](https://pup-assets.s3.amazonaws.com/public/onix/images/9780691284484/9780691284484.pdf) explicitly questions the picture of Soviet citizens hiding their true selves behind a public mask. In his account, participation in official life could coexist with attachments, interests and meanings that a division into belief and deception misses. His purpose was to understand the conditions in which the Soviet collapse could be unexpected yet quickly seem comprehensible.

Consider an ordinary meeting in a hypothetical institution. A resolution contains an ambitious declaration of purpose and a practical decision to approve next year’s activities. Members vote for it. Their votes can authorize a budget, preserve a group’s place in the institution and let them pursue projects they value. Those consequences do not tell us how literally each member takes the declaration at the top of the page.

The resolution describes something and does something. Saying that its declaration is unrealistic may be a fair criticism. It would still leave unexplained why people vote, what the vote makes possible and what they understand themselves to be preserving. The institutional action has a meaning that cannot be reduced to a secretly held opinion about a sentence.

Yurchak draws on the study of speech acts to explain this difference. Language can make commitments as well as describe things. A vote expresses an opinion and makes a decision effective within an institution. Its descriptive and performative dimensions work together.

An official phrase can become familiar enough to repeat with little attention. Its wording may cease to invite discussion while its correct use continues to open doors. People learn which forms let them proceed. What they proceed to do may have considerable value to them.

That is why a repeated formula cannot tell an investigator everything about the speaker. A person who uses it might be frightened, ambitious, committed to part of the institution’s purpose, impatient to get on with other work, or some combination of these. Those possibilities are different social relationships. Declaring every participant a cynic would substitute a guess about private belief for an account of public life.

## When the form becomes its own authority

In their 2010 paper *[American Stiob](https://profiles.rice.edu/sites/g/files/bxs3341/files/inline-files/American%20Stiob%281%29.pdf)*, Dominic Boyer and Yurchak explain hypernormalization through the increasing repetition of authorized forms. They describe political language becoming more predictable and elaborate as writers reproduce formulations already accepted as correct. Literal meaning becomes less decisive than participation in the form.

Suppose a junior official needs approval for a report. Last year’s report passed; an unfamiliar formulation might attract questions. Copying the earlier language saves effort and reduces risk. The next official inherits a model that has now passed twice. Nobody needs to design the final accumulation of phrases. A series of reasonable local decisions can produce language that is difficult to question and increasingly difficult to use for a fresh description.

A category inherited from earlier reports may no longer fit the activity being reported. People might learn to adjust what they record rather than dispute the category. A report can then become a reliable account of compliance while growing less informative about the work itself. Comparing the reports with the activity they describe would reveal whether that distinction had become important.

Boyer and Yurchak also examine *stiob*, a kind of parody that adheres so closely to authoritative style that support and ridicule become hard to distinguish. This is a particular use of the form, rather than a name for all participation in official life. Their paper extends the comparison to Western political culture while explicitly declining to equate its institutions with those of late socialism.

That comparison appeared before Curtis’s film. Yurchak’s own collaboration asks which features have counterparts and which do not. A copied ministerial statement, a corporation’s approved wording and a one-party state’s ideological ritual may share a pattern without imposing the same consequences on a person who departs from it.

An employee may lose a promotion for questioning a report. A journalist may challenge the wording from outside the institution. A citizen may have legal protections that an official lacks in another political system. The force behind the formula, the opportunities to refuse it and the institutions able to examine it belong in the comparison. Similar language does not make those differences disappear.

## What Curtis makes of the idea

The [circulating transcript of HyperNormalisation](https://www.springfieldspringfield.co.uk/movie_script.php?movie=hypernormalisation) opens with an unusually broad claim. Over roughly forty years, the narration says, powerful groups retreated from the world’s complexity into reassuring simplifications. Artists and radicals participated too, so their opposition became ineffective. The story begins with New York and Damascus in 1975 and follows the consequences outward.

Curtis gives the argument a recognizable human motive. People who face a problem they cannot master may prefer a model in which the important forces have names and the available actions seem manageable. People subject to that model may prefer its reassurance to the uncertainty outside it. Participation in the arrangement, including by people who dislike it, becomes part of what needs explaining.

It also risks explaining too much. An elected official relying on an economic model, an activist adopting a slogan and a government concealing a fact are not doing the same thing. Their actions can interact, but the interaction has to be shown. A model is necessarily simpler than what it describes. The important distinction is whether it can be corrected when it fails, or whether an institution keeps treating its output as authoritative despite the failure.

Imagine a planning model that assumes a service can absorb a cut without reducing access. If waiting times then grow, the model can be revised and the budget reconsidered. Alternatively, managers can preserve the original target by changing how waits are classified. In the second case the account of success starts protecting a decision from the experience of the people affected. The disagreement would then concern the classification and the people empowered to preserve it, as well as the original cut.

Curtis’s New York example gives the larger thesis a test. Finance really did constrain political choices. The records also show that decisions were made, contested and revised within those constraints.

## The conditions attached to New York’s rescue

According to the comptroller general’s [April 1976 testimony](https://www.gao.gov/assets/094641.pdf), New York lost access to credit markets in March 1975. The state created the Municipal Assistance Corporation in June, then an emergency financial control board in September. Federal seasonal loans followed under legislation passed in December.

A city can have tax revenue due later and bills that must be paid now. Short-term borrowing bridges the gap. If lenders stop providing that bridge, the government can face an immediate crisis even before the longer argument over revenues and spending is resolved. Whoever can supply credit then has leverage over choices that residents might have expected elections to determine.

A temporary cash shortage is also different from spending more than annual revenue can support. In a simplified example, an administration with income of 100 and expenses of 110 has a gap of 10 before interest. Another loan carries that gap forward; it cannot eliminate it. GAO’s testimony also criticized New York’s accounting practices for obscuring its financial position. The city needed money and a more reliable account of its finances. Deciding how it would obtain them also meant deciding who would bear the costs.

Suppose a city has to meet payroll on Friday and receives a large portion of its taxes in the spring. A lender can make funds available on conditions. Residents may prefer a different distribution of cuts, a new tax, more help from another level of government or a challenge to existing obligations. Each alternative has costs and requires someone able to authorize it. The urgent need for cash gives some parties more time to refuse than others.

Emergency oversight changes that balance further. The New York board’s [institutional history](https://fcb.ny.gov/about-us) describes approval powers over financial plans, borrowing and certain contracts during the control period. A [1995 GAO account](https://www.gao.gov/assets/aimd-96-17r.pdf) identifies four public officials on the seven-member board: the governor, mayor, and state and city comptrollers, alongside three appointed members. The corporation that raised funds and the board that exercised oversight were different bodies. The latter brought state authority and elected officeholders into the city’s financial decisions.

Moving decisions from ordinary city government into an emergency arrangement can reduce the choices directly available to city voters. A mayor’s presence on a board does not give residents an unrestricted say over its conditions. It does make responsibility more specific: legislation created the arrangement, officials exercised its powers, and changes to it required political action.

One part of the rescue was successfully challenged. In [Sgaglione v. Levitt](https://app.midpage.ai/document/sgaglione-v-levitt-5529919), decided on September 29, 1975, civil-service employees’ organizations contested legislation requiring the state comptroller to buy Municipal Assistance Corporation bonds with pension funds. The New York Court of Appeals held that taking away his investment discretion impaired a safeguard of the constitutionally protected pension benefits. It distinguished allowing the investment from compelling it.

The ruling did not decide whether the bonds were good investments or dismantle the rescue arrangement. It preserved a particular decision against an emergency mandate. That is a limited result, but it identifies an institution able to refuse part of what had been presented as necessary. A history of finance gaining power should account for that refusal as well as the conditions imposed.

President Gerald Ford’s position changed too. At his [November 26, 1975 press conference](https://www.presidency.ucsb.edu/documents/the-presidents-news-conference-58), he proposed a temporary federal credit line after describing concessions and new commitments from the city, state, banks and pension system. He defended stringent conditions and the prospect of stopping aid if the plan was not followed. Questioned about his earlier refusal, he argued that the city had taken steps to rescue itself.

Ford was defending his policy and his account of why the city had earned help. His conditions also show choices about federal assistance and the treatment of existing creditors. The parties negotiating them had unequal leverage, and their decisions had consequences for residents who were absent.

Curtis’s argument is strongest when it draws attention to the presentation of those choices as unavoidable. A financing condition can be made to sound like a fact of nature. Its authority then comes partly from hiding the judgment embedded in it. But showing that a decision was presented as necessary should lead us back to the parties who could have acted differently. Describing politics as a vanished activity can make their responsibility harder to see.

## An escape from power still needs somewhere to run

The film also follows the dream of cyberspace as an alternative to ordinary political authority. John Perry Barlow’s [1996 Declaration of the Independence of Cyberspace](https://www.eff.org/cyberspace-independence) directly addresses governments, denying their sovereignty over the social world emerging online. It combines a demand for freedom of expression with claims that older systems lack the means and legitimacy to govern that space.

The demand and the prediction can be judged separately. Wanting people to communicate without arbitrary censorship is an intelligible political aim. Claiming that a new medium makes coercion ineffective is an account of how power works. The first can remain compelling when the second fails.

Networks need equipment, energy, maintenance and people who decide how access is provided. A discussion can cross borders while depending on institutions situated somewhere. The possibility of communicating at a distance does not decide who pays for the connection, who can inspect records or which participant can change the rules. Those are political and organizational questions that a declaration cannot abolish.

In a [2016 interview](https://www.theregister.com/offbeat/2016/10/13/hypernormalisation-adam-curtis-on-chatbots-ai-and-colonel-gaddafi/1346843), Curtis argued that a network can help organize a gathering without supplying a picture of what should happen once people arrive. He also criticized online environments that reflect users back to themselves. Rapid coordination concerns an organization’s capacity to assemble people; governing afterward concerns decisions, authority and a durable relationship among participants.

A group can agree to meet while disagreeing about what it wants. The disagreement may remain invisible while the immediate task is sharing a time and location. Once the group must decide how to negotiate, choose representatives or allocate resources, it needs procedures that its communication channel does not supply automatically. A channel that works well for gathering people can leave these decisions unresolved.

The related claim about personalized information needs its own account of effects. A recommendation that holds someone’s attention, an encounter that changes their belief and a message that helps them act are different outcomes. An inquiry into a feed’s effects has to find out which of these happened. The [[dead-internet]] thesis adds another question, whether apparent participation comes from real people. That question cannot be answered by the feeling that an online environment is repetitive or alienating.

## A large explanation should leave room to act

A story spanning finance, ideology and digital culture can make relations visible that a narrower account would miss. Its risk is to turn a recurring resemblance into a single cause. Once every disappointment means that unreality has absorbed another challenge, the explanation loses the ability to distinguish an organization that failed from one that changed something.

The difference matters to someone deciding what to do. If a local service is closing, saying that politics has become theater offers little guidance about who can reverse the decision. Following the budget, the financing conditions and the officials’ powers may reveal a narrow opening. The opening can be inadequate and the contest unfair. Knowing where it is still provides more leverage than a general certainty that every institution is a facade.

A campaign that saves the service may leave the funding arrangement intact. That would be a limited victory, not a demonstration that every larger criticism was mistaken. Equally, the persistence of the funding arrangement would not make the saved service unreal. An account of containment needs to say what kind of change it means. Otherwise, it can absorb every outcome: defeat proves the system’s power, while success proves its skill at granting concessions. People trying to improve a particular part of their lives deserve an explanation that can recognize both an achievement and its limits.

The same standard applies to a documentary. A sequence can persuade us that two developments belong in a common story. To explain one through the other, it needs a route between them: a decision, a transmitted method, an incentive or a dependency. The relation may be indirect. Making it explicit gives someone a chance to challenge it without being dismissed as unable to see the whole picture.

Inna already knew the poems. Their appearance in an official magazine changed what she could expect to find there next.
