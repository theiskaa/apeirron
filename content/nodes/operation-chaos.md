Grove Press published a book by Kim Philby, the British intelligence officer who had secretly worked for the Soviet Union. That gave the CIA an intelligible reason to notice the publisher. What followed was harder to explain.

According to the Rockefeller Commission, the company acquired a file in Operation CHAOS, the CIA's inquiry into foreign influence on American dissent. Once Grove Press was marked as a subject of interest, analysts collected available information about it. The company had also released the sexually explicit film *I Am Curious (Yellow)*. Reviews of the film were clipped and filmed for the intelligence file.

A connection to a Soviet spy had become a reason to preserve cinema criticism. The reviewer's opinion did not have to reveal espionage or a foreign subsidy. It concerned a company already in the system, and that could be enough.

CHAOS operated from 1967 to 1974. Its stated assignment was to determine whether foreign powers were directing or supporting domestic unrest. It accumulated much more than an answer to that question: files on thousands of Americans, a much larger index of names, and reports about political activity that had little apparent foreign significance. The findings repeatedly frustrated the premise behind the inquiry. The collection kept growing.

## A question from the White House

The program developed during a period of urban violence, expanding opposition to the Vietnam War, and confrontation over civil rights. President Lyndon Johnson wanted to know how much of the unrest had foreign backing. International contacts were real: American activists traveled, attended conferences, met foreign organizations, and shared political causes with people abroad. Establishing what those contacts meant required distinguishing sympathy, cooperation, financial help, and control.

The [Church Committee's reconstruction](https://aarclibrary.org/publib/church/reports/book3/pdf/ChurchB3_9_CHAOS.pdf) places the first recorded authorization on August 15, 1967. Thomas Karamessines, the CIA's deputy director for plans, instructed counterintelligence chief James Angleton to designate an officer for the new work. The memorandum followed discussions with Director Richard Helms. There was no surviving written order from Helms establishing the program, and Helms did not claim that Johnson had specifically ordered him to create it.

He described persistent presidential questioning instead. Johnson wanted to know whether the agency had found anything and how its inquiry was progressing. Helms treated the creation of a coordinating unit as a way to improve the agency's response. The pressure did not need to arrive as a written order to influence what the agency did.

Angleton selected Richard Ober to lead what became the Special Operations Group. Ober already had experience investigating possible foreign ties around *Ramparts*, the magazine that had exposed CIA relationships with American institutions. He had begun indexing people and opening files in that narrower inquiry. The new assignment gave him a much wider field.

Overseas stations were asked to report on radical students, Black expatriates, and travelers. The initial instructions concerned possible exploitation of American problems by the Soviet Union, China, and Cuba. Special communication channels restricted who could see the reporting. The name CHAOS was applied later, as the reporting arrangements were consolidated.

The antiwar movement soon became central. After the October 1967 protests at the Pentagon, the White House requested a study of the peace movement's international connections. CIA analysts prepared it, with Ober coordinating contributions from the operational side and other agencies. In November, Helms delivered the result to Johnson.

The study found little evidence of foreign direction or control. International consultation and coordination did not add up to a movement commanded from abroad. Yet Helms's covering message also emphasized gaps: insufficient knowledge of finances, contacts with embassies, and activists' daily activities. A negative finding arrived accompanied by reasons to keep looking.

## What counted as foreign intelligence

The CIA's founding law denied it police and law-enforcement powers and internal security functions. The prohibition mattered, but it did not mean that every CIA activity inside the United States, or every report mentioning an American, was forbidden. Training for overseas work, obtaining foreign intelligence from willing sources, and protecting agency facilities presented different questions from investigating domestic political opposition.

Counterintelligence supplied the rationale for CHAOS. If a foreign intelligence service was attempting to exploit American unrest, the CIA could argue that it had a foreign target to investigate. Ober also treated helping the FBI obtain information abroad as an established responsibility. The Bureau could identify an American's upcoming travel and ask the CIA to seek information through stations or foreign services.

An investigation of a foreign service and evidence of its operation might identify an American participant. CHAOS could begin with an American dissident and search for a foreign connection. That approach gave political activity a role in selecting the subject before evidence of foreign direction had been found.

Even the word “connection” could expand the assignment. An activist might speak at an international conference because participants opposed the same war. A foreign government might publicize that speech for its own purposes. Neither fact established that the government had instructed the speaker. To determine whether something more existed, however, officers could seek the person's itinerary, acquaintances, organization, and sources of money.

Trying to settle an ambiguous relationship could fill a file. Once domestic information was accepted as useful background, there was no obvious stopping point. More knowledge of a group could always be said to improve the assessment of its foreign ties.

The attraction of that background was real. Imagine a report that an American organizer met a foreign official at a peace conference. Knowing who arranged the meeting, what they discussed, and whether money changed hands could alter its significance. Merely listing the contact would be poor intelligence. But collecting every acquaintance and domestic activity of the organizer would not automatically supply the missing explanation either. An assessment needs evidence of the relationship it claims to describe, not simply an increasingly complete biography.

The CIA's sensitive reports did sometimes describe particular instances of assistance or contact, including reported grants and speeches abroad. The broad finding of little foreign control did not mean that every report was empty or that no foreign government wanted to exploit the protests. It meant that those instances did not support the larger explanation the White House was seeking. A government could welcome, publicize, or occasionally help a movement without having created or commanded it.

The [Rockefeller Commission's 1975 conclusions](https://www.fordlibrarymuseum.gov/library/document/0005/1561496.pdf), in chapter 11, reflected this problem. It accepted the declared mission of investigating foreign influence abroad, while finding that domestic activities had exceeded the CIA's authority. It also judged that the volume of domestic information retained went beyond what was reasonably necessary. A foreign-intelligence purpose had not kept the collection within the agency's authority.

## A name in HYDRA

CHAOS gathered information from CIA stations, the FBI, other CIA offices, agents, and interception programs. Much of its domestic material came from reports already produced by the FBI or from public sources. By June 1970, the Senate report says, FBI reports were arriving at more than a thousand a month.

The office indexed names in a computer system called HYDRA. A person mentioned in a report could be indexed even when the report concerned someone else. A later search could then locate the document in which that name appeared. If there was enough material or sufficient interest, a separate personal file could be opened.

The inquiries counted several different things. The Rockefeller Commission reported roughly 13,000 files overall, including personal files on about 7,200 American citizens, and more than 300,000 names of people and organizations in the computerized index. The Senate staff used somewhat different estimates and explained that file volumes and organizational counts could also vary. An indexed name was not necessarily a separate dossier, and neither category proves that its subject was under continuous surveillance.

Someone could enter a searchable government record because another person mentioned them, without any decision that they posed a threat. The index made those incidental references useful long after the original report arrived.

HYDRA was primarily a means of finding records. It did not turn an unverified report into an established fact. When analysts retained incoming material without first removing irrelevant domestic details, the system made that surplus easier to retrieve. An irrelevant item became easier to find without becoming more useful.

The Grove Press file shows what could happen after a subject entered the system. The original intelligence association supplied a reason to open the file; subsequent material was gathered because it concerned the subject. Each item did not have to renew the justification. The collection could become detailed in precisely the areas least relevant to its stated purpose.

In a [January 1975 memorandum about a meeting with CIA leaders](https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1969-76v38p2/d20), a Justice Department official recorded William Colby's account of the accumulated holdings. Colby could not explain why the agency had retained a category of FBI reports on Americans with no foreign travel or other foreign element. He suggested a tendency to keep paper. That was his retrospective explanation, but it identifies a practice that the inquiries could examine: information admitted as background had remained after its purpose became difficult to state.

## The findings did not end the search

CIA analysis repeatedly rejected the idea that the American protest movements were being run from abroad. Presidential interest did not force every analyst to produce the desired conclusion.

*Restless Youth*, prepared in 1968, examined student unrest internationally. It found domestic social and political sources for unrest in different countries rather than an international conspiracy organizing it all. Its version for the White House included a section on American students. When Helms sent the study to Henry Kissinger in February 1969, he explicitly acknowledged that the domestic section lay outside the agency's charter and warned about the sensitivity of its existence.

Helms could recognize a boundary in a covering memorandum while sending the material across it. The warning protected knowledge of the report more effectively than it protected the people described in it. It also leaves a contemporary record that the problem was understood.

The Nixon White House renewed the demand. In June 1969, aide Tom Charles Huston asked for a report on foreign Communist support for revolutionary protest and instructed the CIA to construe support broadly. He also questioned whether its collection capabilities were adequate. CHAOS itself prepared the response, unlike the earlier studies led by the Intelligence Directorate.

The answer again described very limited assistance and no evidence of foreign direction or control of an American revolutionary protest movement. Later assessments reached substantially the same conclusion. Foreign encouragement and propaganda existed; they did not explain the movement's origins or supply its principal impetus.

The demands for better coverage continued. More sources could make the agency more confident in its negative conclusion and might make the White House accept it. But that argument also made expansion compatible with almost any result. Evidence of foreign direction would justify further investigation. A failure to find it could be attributed to incomplete sources and justify further investigation as well.

Officials had not settled how much of Americans' political lives could be collected while trying to establish confidence. An agency cannot demonstrate that a connection exists nowhere. Without a threshold for opening or continuing an inquiry, uncertainty becomes a standing request for more information.

## Preparing an agent, collecting on everyone nearby

In the autumn of 1969, CHAOS began running agents of its own. The Rockefeller Commission described fewer than thirty directly used by the operation, alongside reporting received from many other sources. Approximately forty potential recruits were assessed. About half came through the FBI, and many were used for a single trip abroad.

A route into the work was to develop an agent's credibility in radical circles in the United States before an overseas assignment. Attending meetings and knowing the people and language of a movement could make that agent more believable abroad. Case officers also used debriefings to judge how accurately recruits observed and reported.

Those sessions generated information about the Americans around the trainee. Names, plans, views, and relationships could be written into reports and passed to CHAOS for indexing. A domestic collection task did not always need to be stated as a separate order: the preparation for a foreign assignment produced material that another office chose to retain.

A related undertaking, identified in the inquiries as Project 2, had explicit limits. Its officers instructed agents not to operate against domestic targets during training. Nevertheless, copies of their contact reports went to CHAOS, where relevant names and information entered the files. One agent became an officer of a domestic dissident group; another advised a congressional campaign and briefly reported behind-the-scenes activities to CHAOS.

The inquiries also found cases that went beyond incidental reporting. In 1971, a newly recruited CHAOS agent was sent to Washington to get close to leaders preparing the May Day demonstrations. The assignment included finding out about groups operating behind the scenes and reporting plans. The case officer met the agent repeatedly, wrote contact reports, and passed significant information promptly to the FBI. The Rockefeller Commission found this domestic use beyond the agency's authority.

Its report did not find evidence that CHAOS agents or officers directed domestic wiretaps, unauthorized entries, or physical and electronic surveillance of dissidents through these agent operations. Their reporting came from observation and personal access. The files also held information received through separate interception programs.

CHAOS supplied names to the CIA's international mail-opening operation and to the NSA for watchlisting. These were identifiable relationships between programs, rather than grounds for attributing every method used by one agency to every other. What entered the CHAOS files, how it had been obtained, and what CHAOS requested are three questions the records allow us to separate.

## The neighboring projects

MERRIMAC and RESISTANCE supplied some information to CHAOS, but they belonged to the CIA's Office of Security and had their own stated purposes. Treating them as subdivisions of CHAOS loses an important part of the history: more than one rationale could bring the CIA into domestic political activity.

RESISTANCE developed from efforts to help CIA recruiters visiting campuses assess the likelihood of harassment or violence. It broadened into reporting on radical organizations. Field offices drew on newspapers, local police, campus officials, and other contacts. Headquarters produced situation reports and calendars of events that might involve disruption.

MERRIMAC used people placed in Washington-area peace and activist groups to obtain advance warning of demonstrations threatening CIA facilities. The Office of Security worked through a company providing commercial security services as cover. Its part-time sources included workers and their relatives, rather than a large force of expert clandestine officers.

The instructions expanded. Reporting on an anticipated demonstration became reporting on organizations' finances, sources of support, relationships, attitudes, and discussions. The agency could receive an account of political life around Washington under an assignment nominally concerned with its own physical protection.

Protecting an office against an identified threat and maintaining a general picture of groups that might someday organize a demonstration impose different limits. The second can continue indefinitely because the possibility of future protest never disappears. RESISTANCE ended in 1973; its distinct files and index should not be added to or substituted for the CHAOS totals without saying what is being counted.

The FBI's [[cointelpro]] provides another comparison. It authorized active disruption of organizations and leaders. CHAOS principally collected and disseminated information. Intelligence sharing connected institutions, but the existence of a CHAOS report in FBI hands does not, by itself, establish which disruptive operation used it. Conflating the two conceals both the CIA's actual conduct and the decisions for which the Bureau was responsible.

## Secrecy from the people meant to review it

CHAOS was highly restricted even inside the CIA. Reporting channels and layers of access protected its holdings. Some officers defended the secrecy as a way of safeguarding the Americans named in files or preventing misunderstanding of a sensitive assignment. Those protections also impeded independent examination.

The Rockefeller Commission found that an inspector general's team had been denied access to specific CHAOS files and operations during a 1972 survey. An Office of Management and Budget inspection team was not told about the operation. The commission found no indication that the CIA's general counsel had been consulted on its propriety, and no normal annual review comparable to that of most clandestine programs.

Some CIA officers objected. Field officers questioned reporting on prominent Americans whose alleged subversive connections seemed vague. A group of fourteen middle-level officers raised concerns in 1971. Some objections involved jurisdiction or administrative friction; others concerned whether the work belonged in the CIA at all.

Officers had raised concerns while there was still an opportunity to change course. Helms's assurances that it was a legitimate counterintelligence responsibility helped sustain it.

The inspector general's concerns eventually led to a senior review and an instruction to give terrorism a clearer priority. In 1973, James Schlesinger's request for accounts of activities potentially outside the CIA's charter brought CHAOS into the internal inventory later called the Family Jewels. Colby imposed narrower instructions that August: focus on foreign organizations and individuals, with Americans encountered incidentally.

Colby terminated CHAOS as a specified collection program in March 1974. The Senate report dates the termination order to March 5; the Rockefeller chapter gives March 15. Both describe its ending months before Seymour Hersh's December reporting and the 1975 inquiries. The press exposure did not cause an operation still running in December to shut down.

The termination instructions continued to allow certain reporting about Americans encountered in foreign intelligence work and responses to specified FBI requests. Ending the project therefore did not mean that no CIA report could subsequently mention an American. The intended change concerned targeting, authority, and approval, which are more useful things to examine than whether the name CHAOS remained in use.

## Exposure and the difficulty of a remedy

Hersh's December 22, 1974, *New York Times* article brought the CIA's domestic intelligence activity to a broad public. President Ford appointed the Rockefeller Commission in January 1975; the Senate created the Church Committee that month. The inquiries turned the agency's explanations into a record that could be compared with directives, files, and testimony.

People named in the records still faced a difficult path to a remedy. In *Halkin v. Helms*, antiwar activists and organizations sought relief over CHAOS and the CIA's submission of names to the NSA. The litigation demonstrated the distance between knowing that a program existed and being allowed to establish what it did to a particular plaintiff.

In its [1982 decision](https://law.justia.com/cases/federal/appellate-courts/F2/690/977/107545/), the appeals court upheld the government's state-secrets claims restricting discovery. Protected details included sources, foreign liaison relationships, and circumstances of surveillance. That left plaintiffs unable to prove important elements of their claims. For plaintiffs whose exposure to CHAOS was acknowledged, the court separately considered a declaration about the past surveillance and an injunction against future conduct. It upheld denial of the injunction for lack of an imminent threat, and treated the requested declaration as moot.

The outcome was not a merits judgment declaring all of CHAOS lawful. The problem was that information needed to litigate specific injuries could itself be withheld as a secret. The court recognized that this could leave violations without a judicial remedy and pointed to Congress's role.

The Rockefeller Commission had also addressed the next decision for the accumulated files. It recommended destroying material without foreign-intelligence value after the investigations and as permitted by law. Keeping records could preserve evidence of abuse; keeping irrelevant political information indefinitely could perpetuate the intrusion. Ending collection left a responsibility to account for what had already been gathered.

The analysts' findings remain one of the most valuable parts of this history. They described Americans forming political judgments about their own country, alongside foreign governments trying to exploit those judgments. The two activities could coexist without one explaining the other. Officials received that answer repeatedly and continued to ask whether wider collection would uncover something else.

In the Grove Press file, publishing Philby could raise a foreign-intelligence question. A film review answered a different question, but still found a place in the file. Somewhere between receiving the item and preserving it, someone needed to ask what justified keeping it. CHAOS made retrieval routine while allowing that prior decision to remain unsettled.
