Four rolls of film sat undeveloped in the police file on Kurt Cobain’s death for twenty years. When Seattle detective Mike Ciesynski had them processed in 2014, the photographs had acquired a green tint. The laboratory tried to correct it. Some of the views repeated images already available to the original investigators; others gave the public more of the scene than it had seen before.

The discovery has become part of the dispute over Cobain’s death. To critics, the rolls exemplify an investigation that decided too soon and left work unfinished. For the detective reviewing the file, developing them was a way to preserve aging evidence, and their contents supported the existing suicide determination. The same action could be received as belated diligence or another reason for distrust.

Cobain, Nirvana’s singer and principal songwriter, was found dead at his Seattle home on April 8, 1994. The medical examiner attributed his death to a shotgun wound and classified it as suicide. That remains the official determination. Private investigator Tom Grant and later researchers have argued that the death was a staged homicide.

The documents tell a less tidy story than many accounts of the dispute. The note was examined. The credit-card entries came with a warning about their times. A newer forensic paper contains qualifications absent from some of its publicity. And a police release in 2026 records the destruction of an important physical exhibit.

## What was found, and what was decided

Cobain’s body was discovered in a room above the detached garage, commonly called the greenhouse. The medical examiner’s [scene notes and surviving autopsy documents](https://www.autopsyfiles.org/reports/Celebs/cobain%2C%20kurt_report.pdf) describe a firearm, a note, drug paraphernalia, and recent as well as older signs of injection. The estimated death date was April 5, several days before discovery.

These observations did different kinds of work. The injury established the immediate physical cause of death. The circumstances of the room, the weapon, the writing, and the investigation informed the decision about manner: whether the death was self-inflicted, accidental, or caused by another person. A medical description of an injury and a reconstruction of responsibility are related judgments, with different questions to answer.

Police plainly favored suicide from the outset. Their initial major-investigation summary said that the wound appeared self-inflicted at that stage of the inquiry. It is reasonable to ask whether that early interpretation narrowed what followed. It is inaccurate to say that all work stopped that day. The released file contains subsequent interviews and laboratory examinations, including the April 22 handwriting report and May firearms work.

The early assessment could have shaped those later inquiries. Their existence nevertheless has to be accounted for in a criticism of the investigation. The surviving medical determination is certified June 20. That document is distinct from the initial police assessment on discovery day.

A record of completed tests still leaves questions about work that was omitted. The early suicide assessment and the later laboratory findings need to be examined together: whether the findings supported the assessment, and whether investigators gave a competing explanation a fair test.

## The number that became a verdict

The most frequently repeated challenge concerns the toxicology result. The laboratory sheet records morphine at 1.52 milligrams per liter, measured by radioimmunoassay, a laboratory screening technique. It also lists codeine, diazepam, nordiazepam, and a positive result for monoacetylmorphine. The copy is legible enough to check the numbers: codeine is 0.11 milligrams per liter, rather than the 0.93 sometimes repeated in accounts of this case.

A concentration in a blood sample is not the quantity injected. It is a measurement made after a sequence of absorption, metabolism, and other changes. Turning it into a precise amount administered, or a stopwatch measuring the person’s remaining coordination, requires further information. The distinction becomes especially important when a report does not separately establish every drug fraction being compared with a published threshold.

A [2002 study by Christoph Meissner and colleagues](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12427450/) illustrates the problem without deciding Cobain’s case. It compared 207 heroin-related deaths with 27 surviving drivers affected by opiates. The researchers measured both free morphine and total morphine, which includes conjugated forms produced through metabolism. Concentrations overlapped between the groups, and the authors found that either concentration alone could not reliably predict survival. They also found that all cases in their sample at or above a particular free-morphine concentration were fatal. The distinction between free and total was consequential, rather than a technical footnote.

The study does not show that an intoxicated person has ordinary coordination. Its surviving drivers were selected because they had come to attention while intoxicated. Nor does a study of outcomes establish exactly how quickly one particular person lost consciousness. It gives reasons to reject a universal calculation in which a single reported concentration proves that any person must have been incapable of any further act.

Ciesynski addressed the high result in his [written 2014 review](https://spdblotter.seattle.gov/wp-content/uploads/sites/11/2014/03/Follow_Up_Report_Photos.pdf). He recorded consulting chief medical examiner Richard Harruff, who emphasized the importance of tolerance when assessing opiate concentrations. This was a medical explanation offered in support of the existing determination. It was not a new measurement of Cobain’s condition before death.

An argument about incapacity has to establish what the measurement represents and why that degree of impairment follows in this case. Repeating a multiple of a generic “lethal dose” skips those steps. On the other side, mentioning tolerance is the beginning of an explanation, not a complete reconstruction of the final minutes.

## What the note can establish

Grant’s case has long included a second proposal: the note was principally about leaving music, and its last lines were added to turn it into a farewell to life. The distinction is central to his account because the note was among the circumstances supporting suicide. If its meaning or authorship changed, part of that account would have to be reconsidered.

The [Washington State Patrol report dated April 22, 1994](https://meixatech.com/COBAIN-NOTEBOOK1.pdf#page=151) records a comparison between the questioned note and three pages of known writing on Hotel Excelsior stationery. Examiner Janis Parker concluded that Cobain wrote the questioned note. There was therefore a forensic document examination, not merely an assumption by the responding police.

That conclusion does not make later examination illegitimate. It establishes what the earlier inquiry did and what a later challenge has to address. An examiner disputing it should identify the material used, the features compared, and the reason the earlier identification fails. The visual impression that one part looks larger or less controlled cannot do all that work on its own.

A [June 15, 2025 report by document examiner James Green](https://www.meixatech.com/CobainGreenReport.pdf) provides a more limited result than a declaration of forgery. Green examined copies supplied by Michelle Wilkins, comparing the final four lines with Cobain samples and a disputed practice sheet. His executive summary calls the examination inconclusive. He notes features shared with both sets of comparison material, and later identifies the questioned note’s poor resolution as the greatest limitation. Additional samples or an original could change the opinion.

Green did not identify another writer or exclude Cobain. His finding leaves the later challenge unresolved; it does not change what Parker concluded from the material she examined in 1994.

There are two questions even if authorship is accepted. Who wrote the words, and what did the writer intend? A person can express distress about work and relationships in the same document. Those subjects do not divide neatly into a professional resignation and a personal farewell. Reading the note as chiefly about retirement remains an interpretation, rather than a laboratory result.

The alleged practice sheet presents another chain of questions. A resemblance between letterforms does not establish when a sheet was made, who possessed it, or whether it was used to alter the note. Those questions require a history of the document. A comparison can examine an allegation without authenticating every premise supplied alongside the comparison material.

## The credit-card trail

Grant became involved before the body was discovered. In his [published account of the search](https://cobaincase.blogspot.com/p/summary-of-events.html), he says Courtney Love hired him on April 3 and that he worked with investigator Ben Klugman to trace activity on a canceled credit card. He later searched in Seattle with Cobain’s friend Dylan Carlson. They visited the house but did not discover the body in the room above the garage.

Grant’s account developed into an accusation against Love. He interpreted conversations, marital difficulties, the card activity, and the note as parts of a homicide case. His proximity to the search gives his observations historical value. His interpretation of another person’s motives still has to be distinguished from those observations.

The credit-card claim is a useful example because a contemporary police follow-up records the investigators discussing its limitations. In an April 11 conversation, according to detective Steve Kirkland’s [report](https://meixatech.com/COBAIN-NOTEBOOK1.pdf#page=176), Klugman explained that the listed times were when attempts were noted in the computer, rather than the actual times of the attempts. The later attempts were described as telephone transactions; the canceled card prevented completed charges. The investigator also relayed that the family sometimes lent cards to other people.

The report preserves the warning alongside the dates. A list of entries might look like a trail of someone shopping after a death, but Klugman was explaining why these entries could not be read that way. No purchase was completed, and the listed time was not necessarily when anyone tried to make it.

The report does not identify every person who may have possessed the card or its details. It leaves an unresolved practical question. But possession of a missing card would still not identify a killer without another connection. Theft, lending, and attempted misuse are alternatives that a murder allegation has to distinguish, rather than facts it can bypass.

Grant’s larger argument also invokes an anticipated divorce and an inheritance. Financial interests can give investigators a reason to examine relationships and communications. They do not make the outcome of a death investigation a calculation of who stood to gain. An inheritance argument has to begin with the actual will or estate arrangements and the actual terms of any marital agreement. The identity and rights of other beneficiaries matter. A story in which a widow automatically inherits everything can erase those people before the financial comparison even starts. Evidence of planning or participation would still be needed to connect an interest in money to a death. Assertions about what a spouse would have received under an unexamined agreement are an unstable foundation for an accusation of murder.

Neither anger in public mourning nor a decision not to sue an accuser fills that gap. Grief supplies no reliable script against which a widow’s guilt can be measured. Declining litigation is not an admission. Such interpretations can make a narrative more suspicious without adding evidence about the death itself.

## A review with answers and limits

A later allegation about a proposed killing also reached the police file. Musician Eldon Hoke, known as El Duce, claimed he had been offered money to kill Cobain. The released record includes a March 1996 conversation in which Jack Briggs relayed that allegation to Ciesynski. A [March 10 memorandum from Sergeant Don Cameron](https://meixatech.com/COBAIN-NOTEBOOK1.pdf#page=65) directed follow-up, including requests for statements and polygraph material and coordination with Los Angeles authorities.

Those documents record an allegation and a proposed response, rather than proof of the alleged offer or a complete investigation of it. They do contradict the categorical claim that Seattle police never pursued the subject. The existence of a memorandum is not evidence that all its instructions were carried out. Establishing that would require the subsequent record.

Ciesynski’s review was prompted by the approaching twentieth anniversary and expected media questions. The department [explicitly described it as a review, rather than a reopening](https://spdblotter.seattle.gov/2014/03/31/detectives-reviews-cobain-case-which-remains-closed/). The four undeveloped rolls were found during that process. Police already had Polaroids and medical-examiner photographs, so undeveloped film did not mean the scene had gone entirely unphotographed.

Ciesynski had taken the 1996 statement and acknowledged that small earlier role in his report. His written account records consulting Harruff and retired original investigator Jim Yoshida, reviewing the autopsy and toxicology, and seeking an armorer’s interpretation of the firearm’s position and the spent casing. He proposed a mechanical explanation consistent with the original determination. Whatever its adequacy, this was a response to a particular question, not a report that ignored the firearm altogether.

The original [May 1994 fingerprint result](https://meixatech.com/COBAIN-NOTEBOOK1.pdf#page=137) is also more precise than the usual claim that the weapon was clean. Four cards of latent impressions were lifted; the examiner reported no legible prints. That result cannot identify the person who handled the weapon. It does not itself demonstrate that someone wiped it, much less identify a person who did so.

A lifted impression can be too poor for identification without having been wiped. That leaves the handling of the weapon unresolved by fingerprints. Turning the result into a finding of deliberate cleaning adds an action the examination did not establish.

Police can make the opposite mistake by treating an old conclusion as an answer to every new objection. A useful review needs reasons that someone outside the institution can examine. Releasing the report made some reasons inspectable. It did not turn that report into an independent assessment or replace tests that were never performed.

## The newer forensic challenge

A paper by Bryan Burnett and colleagues, [published on November 6, 2025](https://medwinpublishers.com/IJFSC/a-multidisciplinary-analysis-of-the-kurt-cobain-death%2A.pdf), argues for homicide and staging. It reinterprets blood patterns, firearm behavior, toxicology, and a partial autopsy. Its authors worked from released material, rather than a new examination of the body.

Some of its qualifications deserve as much attention as its conclusion. The toxicology discussion acknowledges that a longstanding heroin user could possibly tolerate the reported concentration and remain functional. Its discussion of microscopic findings assumes that *necrosis*, meaning tissue death, describes injury occurring before death rather than decomposition. That section says a definitive interpretation requires the full data and histological slides, which were unavailable to the authors.

The qualifications affect the reconstruction. If a claim of prior incapacity depends on a particular reading of a term in an incomplete record, that reading remains a step to establish. Several conclusions that share that assumption do not become independent confirmations simply by appearing under different headings.

The paper assembles a cumulative argument, in which several findings are meant to reinforce one another. To evaluate that accumulation, another examiner would have to check whether its parts are independent. A proposed injection, a proposed period of incapacity, and a proposed staging may form a continuous account while still relying on the same uncertain reading of the medical record.

The official response has remained resistant. [Mike Carter’s February 2026 reporting](https://www.spokesman.com/stories/2026/feb/20/debate-over-kurt-cobains-death-reignites-with-new-/) describes the researchers presenting their findings to Seattle police in November, and a January response maintaining the original determination. The medical examiner’s office also said the material did not warrant reopening. Former senior prosecutor Mark Larson, supporting the researchers’ request, argued that the original inquiry was inadequate.

The exchange documents disagreement about the adequacy of the investigation. The paper has not changed the official manner of death, and its homicide claim has not established responsibility by Love or another named person. Equally, the official refusal does not constitute a new experiment answering every part of the paper.

## What remains available to examine

Access to the records has involved interests beyond the homicide dispute. In [*Lee v. City of Seattle*](https://www.courts.wa.gov/opinions/pdf/758152.pdf), decided in 2018, the Washington Court of Appeals upheld orders preventing release of additional death-scene photographs and protecting other confidential material. Love and Frances Bean Cobain described the harm that publication would cause them. The ruling addressed disclosure and family privacy; it was not a trial of the murder theory.

The legal history makes a blanket claim that the entire case was sealed misleading. Many documents and photographs were released. Other material remained protected. A family can have a legitimate interest in preventing public circulation of a relative’s body while an investigator has a legitimate question about the examination. Public distribution and an appropriately authorized professional review are different kinds of access.

Physical preservation raises a separate issue. On September 1, 2026, Seattle police added administrative records to their existing release page. The [firearm’s custody record](https://spdblotter.seattle.gov/wp-content/uploads/sites/11/2014/03/1_Property-_-Evidence-SEMI-AUTO_SHOTGUN-1994-156500-Chain-of-Custody-1.pdf) contains a destruction entry posted September 29, 2022, with a note recording destruction on September 27 at Nucor Steel. The listed serial number matches the firearm identified in the original laboratory report.

That record materially changes what a future examiner could do. Photographs and earlier laboratory work remain, but they cannot provide every test or observation available from the original object. Researchers can debate a proposed reconstruction using records; they cannot now inspect that firearm’s present condition. The custody entry records disposal, not a demonstrated motive to conceal a murder. The loss of an opportunity for examination is real without that further accusation.

The chronology needs care. Photographs showing police possession of the firearm in 2015 could rebut a claim that it had already been destroyed then. They cannot rebut a record of destruction seven years later. An argument can be wrong about an earlier date while later events create a preservation problem of their own.

Cobain’s death became part of the [[celebrity-27-club]] story, giving a generation’s loss a place in an older pattern. That pattern does not decide the forensic questions. Nor does the idea that an oppositional musician’s death benefited an industry establish an operation of the kind proposed in [[counterculture-psyop]]. The people making a death determination had a particular room, body, set of statements, and exhibits to examine.

On the available record, suicide is the better supported account. The scene and injury, the note’s contemporary identification, and the medical examiner’s assessment fit that conclusion. The public homicide case has not shown that these findings require staging or established participation by a named person. Several of its familiar claims depend on misdescribed tests or interpretations of behavior. There are also legitimate questions about the scope of review and preservation of evidence. A discussion of [[control-systems]] is useful only if it stays with those actual institutional decisions.

The destroyed firearm gives the argument a concrete stopping point. Whatever a future review concludes from the surviving record, one opportunity has been lost. The unresolved argument will now have to proceed without it.
