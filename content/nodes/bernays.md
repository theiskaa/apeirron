The Library of Congress [catalogues two photographs from Easter Sunday, 1929](https://www.loc.gov/item/2015647632/), identifying women holding cigarettes on Fifth Avenue as participants in Edward Bernays’s “Torches of Freedom” promotion. The record gives the pictures names, a date and a connection to a campaign. It cannot tell us how many people began smoking because of them.

The familiar success story credits Bernays with breaking the taboo on women smoking in public and transforming the cigarette market. That claim needs more than the photograph, a memorable slogan or the consultant’s account of his success. It needs evidence about the publicity, the audience and the behavior that changed.

In his writing, Bernays describes a profession that arranges the circumstances in which people form an opinion: which authority they hear, which event becomes news and which motives become attached to a purchase. The problem is how to recognize that influence without mistaking its ambition for unlimited power.

## Before the famous parade

Bernays’s wartime work supplies a small, concrete example of organized persuasion. The State Department’s [published account of a 1918 document](https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1917-72PubDip/d39) identifies a catalog insert attached to an October report from him in the Committee on Public Information’s Foreign Section. The insert was supplied to American firms for inclusion in commercial catalogs sent abroad.

The [Portuguese-language sheet](https://static.history.state.gov/frus/frus1917-72PubDip/Document%20A.7.pdf) presented statements about American war aims attributed to Woodrow Wilson alongside figures about troops, shipping and production. Commercial distribution could carry a government’s message to readers who had opened a catalog for another purpose.

Here the distribution route is concrete: an official message travels with a commercial catalog. Establishing how readers responded would require another part of the record. It would be a large leap from the existence of the insert to a claim that Bernays personally converted a nation to the war.

His work belonged to a communications system involving firms, officials, printers, distributors and readers. The individual consultant mattered within that system. He did not replace it.

## Why he thought persuasion belonged in democracy

In [*Propaganda*](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/78634/78634-h/78634-h.htm), published in 1928, Bernays defended the organization of public opinion as a practical necessity. People could not investigate every product and public question for themselves. Leaders and institutions narrowed what they encountered. His “invisible government” was a provocative account of that influence, not the name of one demonstrated command center.

No one has time to investigate every decision from the beginning. A person buying bread cannot audit the flour mill, reconstruct nutrition science and inspect every bakery before breakfast. Trust and division of labor make ordinary choices possible. Political judgment involves similar dependence on other people’s knowledge.

Dependence on other people does not settle which people deserve trust. A buyer relying on a food inspector and a buyer encountering a manufacturer-funded expert are both relying on others. They are not necessarily in the same position. The expert’s obligations, evidence and relationship to the seller affect how the advice should be weighed.

Bernays was selling a service to the people who wanted acceptance for a product or policy. His defense of the profession therefore has two audiences: the public asked to trust the arrangement and the client asked to pay for it. An account of extraordinary power can serve the second audience even while it claims to explain the first.

The book also describes influence as dispersed across groups and settings. Someone can be influential within a profession and have little authority over another person’s religious or political choices. A successful appeal to one group does not establish control over every decision its members make.

## Getting someone else to carry the message

A seller can state its own case, or it can find someone whom the audience already trusts and arrange circumstances in which that person carries a favorable message.

Bernays’s 1928 hypothetical discussion of selling bacon makes the contrast explicit: a direct appeal to buy can be replaced by advice transmitted through physicians. His account of soap-sculpture competitions describes another route, bringing a branded product into art activities supported by educators and artists. In both, an existing relationship gives the message a way into the audience’s attention.

The intermediary may value the activity for reasons of their own. A teacher may genuinely value an art activity, just as a physician may sincerely recommend a particular kind of meal. The commercial sponsor can benefit from that sincerity. People can cooperate for different reasons without sharing the same overall purpose.

Consider an invented example. A company funds a school competition using its product. The school wants a worthwhile activity, families want children to enjoy it and the company wants familiarity with its brand. These interests can overlap. The ethical question concerns what participants are told and what the sponsor is entitled to influence: the materials, judging, publicity, access to children or later use of the work.

The school activity can be worthwhile while still needing an account of the sponsor’s role. A genuine benefit can become the vehicle for a message whose commercial purpose is less visible than the activity itself.

A consultant can prepare the story before a journalist arrives: assemble people, choose a date and create something worth photographing. The journalist may accurately report every visible detail while leaving the sponsor’s purpose out of view. The event has happened, but it happened partly to secure that report.

Social motives deserve more respect than the language of manipulation sometimes allows. Bernays’s car example contrasts the buyer’s stated technical reasons with possible motives involving status or other people’s approval. Those are his proposed explanations, not an experiment establishing what every buyer secretly wants.

A person may know perfectly well that they want a car partly because it conveys success. That preference can be questioned, but discovering its social meaning has not exposed a hidden mechanism. The buyer may be choosing that meaning as deliberately as they choose the size of the car.

The deception could lie elsewhere: inventing an endorsement, concealing a payment or presenting a claim about quality that cannot be supported. A campaign can also encourage someone to confuse one property with another, as though a fashionable owner established a machine’s reliability. The audience then needs a way to examine the implied connection.

A persuader does not begin with an empty mind to fill. The audience’s interests may be genuine. The campaign changes the circumstances in which they are expressed and tries to attach them to the client’s objective. What the audience understands about that attachment is part of the ethical question.

## What the Easter parade can tell us

The 1929 campaign used an association between public smoking and women’s freedom. That association made the cigarette more than a product with a price. It gave reporters a cultural conflict and offered participants a gesture that could be described in political language.

In [her account of researching the event](https://www.american-journalism.org/teaching-our-journal/teaching-our-journal-edward-bernayss-1929-torches-of-freedom-march/), historian Vanessa Murphree describes examining more than 250 articles and using Bernays’s scrapbooks at the Library of Congress. She reports that some editors recognized the publicity tactic and challenges histories that relied too heavily on Bernays’s version of its effectiveness.

The publicity produced a response, but the character of that response needs to be examined. Several newspapers printing the same wire story would show distribution, not several independent confirmations that the message persuaded their readers.

The Library of Congress photograph record identifies participants and connects the pictures to the campaign. It does not measure cigarette purchases. The image and the later account belong in the investigation, but neither can stand in for a sales study.

Suppose cigarette sales to women rose after the event. We would still need to consider advertising, prices, distribution, existing changes in public behavior and other campaigns. A trend beginning before March 1929 would complicate the claim that one march created it. A rise in sales would not tell us how much resulted from this particular appeal without a defensible comparison.

The fact that the story is memorable can make that last step disappear. A consultant stages an event; a newspaper reports it; later consumption increases; the event becomes the cause. The sequence offers a plausible mechanism, but the size of its contribution remains a separate question.

The appeal gave a commercial interest a political vocabulary. The campaign tried to borrow the language of liberation, and the press could either transmit that frame or identify the promotional interest behind it. The audience’s information depended partly on which of those things an editor did.

## The engineering metaphor and its limits

Bernays’s [1947 essay, “The Engineering of Consent”](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/1/1b/The_Engineering_of_Consent_%28essay%29.pdf), presents persuasion as planned work: establish objectives and resources, research the audience, identify its leaders and organize activity across media. It urges practitioners to create news rather than treat a campaign as a collection of isolated press placements. Research can also require changing the original objective.

Much of this is advice about preparation and coordination. The word engineering lends it a certainty the audience may not supply. In his earlier book, Bernays acknowledged a margin of error and circumstances outside the practitioner’s control. Yet in the same chapter he also described people following physicians’ advice as a “mathematical certainty.” His language moves from uncertainty to a promise of dependable influence. The confident phrase is not a quantified result accompanied by measurements of how many people followed the advice. A reader should be able to recognize the method while reserving judgment about the certainty attached to it.

The bridge metaphor can therefore mislead. A campaign has opponents, people interpret its message differently and another event can displace it from attention. Exposure does not guarantee belief, belief does not guarantee action and an action may have reasons the campaign did not create.

Different measures can produce different accounts of the same campaign. Counting press mentions measures visibility. A survey might measure recall or a change in expressed opinion. Purchases, votes and attendance measure other things. A campaign can improve on one measure and fail on another.

A report to the client should say which result was sought. If the purpose was sales but the only result reported is a pile of clippings, the evidence has changed halfway through the account. Attention may be useful to the client, but it is not the same achievement as the purchase the client wanted.

The essay’s author note also identifies Bernays as working in partnership with Doris E. Fleischman. The familiar story of a solitary inventor can hide the ordinary work of research, writing, negotiation and organization on which a campaign depends. That work is part of the explanation, not an inconvenience to the legend.

## His ethical defense leaves a question open

Bernays did not argue that any deception was acceptable. His 1947 essay says persuasion should supplement rather than replace education, recognizes the danger of antidemocratic uses and calls for responsibility in choosing clients and causes. His 1928 account also describes source identification to editors as proper practice. That is his professional prescription, not evidence that every campaign met it.

Someone who knows how to explain a complicated issue can improve public understanding. A campaign for a useful public service does not become illegitimate merely because it is planned, emotionally engaging or professionally written. Spontaneity is no guarantee of truth.

The difficulty lies in who decides that a cause is socially beneficial and whether the audience can inspect the decision. A consultant’s confidence in a client is not a substitute for information about the relationship. Editors knowing where a press release came from does not necessarily mean readers know how the resulting story originated.

The client can be asked to change, too. In the 1928 book, Bernays describes public relations as involving changes to a client’s conduct, not only messages defending it. That possibility is easy to lose when the profession is treated as nothing but deception.

Suppose a firm is disliked because customers cannot obtain repairs. It could pay for sympathetic stories, or it could improve the repair service and make the improvement known. Both responses involve reputation. The second changes the experience on which the reputation rests. A critic should be able to distinguish them without assuming that all favorable communication is a cover story.

Identifying who paid for a survey or an event lets the audience consider the sponsor’s interest. They still need to assess the evidence. A disclosed study can be badly designed; an interested participant can say something true. Identifying a motive does not resolve the factual question in either direction.

Imagine a manufacturer funding a survey that asks whether homes should be safer. Agreement with that proposition does not establish support for the manufacturer’s particular product. If the resulting publicity converts the first answer into an endorsement of the second, the distortion lies in the inference. Listing the sponsor would be useful, but it would not repair the mismatch between what was asked and what was claimed.

Professional competence and ethical permission are different judgments. A campaign can be carefully researched and still deprive people of information needed to evaluate it. The more skillfully the consultant works through trusted intermediaries, the more important their independence and obligations become.

## A career is not a universal chain of command

Bernays worked across commercial and political settings. The Library of Congress [description of his papers](https://findingaids.loc.gov/repositories/19/resources/2467) identifies a wide client record, including United Fruit, and describes files documenting that company’s anticommunist activity in Central America. That relationship warrants examination through its correspondence, publicity and government contacts.

The finding aid tells a researcher where to look next. Establishing Bernays’s contribution to a particular political outcome requires following what he supplied, who used it and how it entered decisions. A company’s interest, a consultant’s campaign and a government’s covert operation cannot be merged merely because they concerned the same country.

The same care applies to claims that every later intelligence operation or online influence campaign descends from him. Similar techniques can be borrowed, rediscovered or developed within different institutions. A shared use of symbols and intermediaries is not a personnel record or an order linking the operations.

It is possible to take public relations seriously without granting its most confident spokesman the power he advertised. Indeed, accepting that advertisement can repeat one of his profession’s methods: let a vivid example establish a reputation that outruns what the record can measure.

## The judgment left to the audience

Bernays draws attention to choices that usually occur before a reader encounters a message. Someone chose the event, supplied the expert, paid for the research or gave a journalist a convenient set of materials. Those choices can affect what seems important or widely believed.

Recognizing them does not require believing that every opinion has been installed from outside. It gives judgment more work to do: checking the evidence, identifying the interested party and asking whether an apparent independent endorsement is independent in the relevant sense.

The consultant can arrange the appeal; the audience should be able to find out how it was arranged. Who commissioned the survey, selected the expert or organized the event belongs in that account. Bernays’s claim to professional skill gives him no special authority to keep those relationships from the people whose agreement he seeks.
