On 5 February 2003, Colin Powell told the United Nations Security Council that Iraq had mobile factories for producing biological weapons. He described an eyewitness, then other sources he said confirmed the account. Drawings showed tanks, fermenters and vehicles. The facilities, he argued, could move before inspectors found them.

The drawings looked like a description of something known to exist. Much of the underlying account came from one source, codenamed Curveball, whom American intelligence officers had been unable to interview directly for their assessments. Other reporting supplied far less corroboration than the presentation suggested. Warnings about the source had circulated inside the intelligence system. They did not reach Powell before he spoke.

How did evidence this weak reach the Security Council as a confident account of Iraqi weapons? The answer runs through intelligence agencies that mistook plausible stories for well-supported findings and political leaders who wanted Saddam Hussein removed. Their work met in the case for war, but they did not all make the same decisions or know the same things.

Some of those leaders had advocated regime change through the Project for the New American Century years before the invasion. Its letters and reports let us read their ambitions before they joined the Bush administration. The later intelligence record shows what happened when those ambitions acquired the authority of government.

## A policy before a weapons case

In January 1998, PNAC sent President Bill Clinton a letter arguing that containment of Iraq was failing. Its signatories included Donald Rumsfeld and Paul Wolfowitz, who would later lead the Defense Department under George W. Bush. They wanted Saddam removed from power and were willing to use military action.

The [letter](https://noi.org/wp-content/uploads/2016/01/iraqclintonletter1998-01-26-Copy.pdf) made uncertainty part of the argument. If sanctions and inspections weakened, the authors believed, the United States would lose the ability to know whether Iraq possessed prohibited weapons. That uncertainty would endanger American forces, regional allies and oil supplies. Their proposed remedy was regime change. The case rested on the danger they expected, rather than a new weapons discovery.

This preference extended beyond one think tank. In October 1998, Clinton signed the Iraq Liberation Act. Congress declared support for efforts to remove Saddam’s regime and replace it with a democratic government, and authorized assistance to opposition organizations. Apart from its military-assistance provision, the act specified that it should not be construed to authorize or otherwise address the use of American armed forces. Supporting regime change and authorizing an invasion were separate decisions.

PNAC’s September 2000 report, *Rebuilding America’s Defenses*, addressed a wider ambition: maintaining American military predominance. Its much-quoted reference to a “new Pearl Harbor” appears in a discussion of how slowly armed forces adopt new technologies and organization. The surrounding text concerns research budgets, aircraft production, existing commitments and the disruption involved in replacing expensive weapons systems.

The passage anticipates that a catastrophe could accelerate military transformation. It supplies no plan to cause such an event. Reading it as an admission of responsibility for [[nine-eleven]] moves beyond what the document says. Longstanding advocates of regime change entered government, and the attacks gave their arguments for preventive action a new hearing.

A preference can change the question an official asks of intelligence. Someone considering whether to remove a government may ask what the evidence implies for several possible policies. Someone already committed to removal may ask how to make the case convincing. The July 2002 British records would describe a government wrestling with that second question.

## Why the suspicion seemed plausible

Iraq’s earlier weapons programs were real. Saddam’s government had used chemical weapons, concealed important work and obstructed inspectors. After the 1991 Gulf War, investigators discovered that its nuclear effort had been much further advanced than American intelligence had understood. Analysts had a humiliating example of underestimation behind them.

The [2005 WMD Commission](https://georgewbush-whitehouse.archives.gov/wmd/text/report.html) described how this experience shaped the next assessment. Analysts expected deception. When information was scarce, they inferred that prohibited activity continued. Evidence fitting that view was readily accepted; evidence pointing away from it could be explained as another Iraqi attempt at concealment.

Deception gradually became an explanation that could accommodate almost any observation. If a suspected facility appeared active, activity might indicate a weapons program. If inspectors found no weapons, their absence might indicate successful concealment. An assessment built this way becomes difficult to correct, even when its original suspicion was reasonable.

In October 2002, the intelligence community produced a National Intelligence Estimate, its principal coordinated assessment. Most agencies judged that Iraq was rebuilding its nuclear weapons program. The estimate also asserted that Iraq had biological weapons, mobile production facilities and substantial chemical stocks. Those judgments gave policymakers an apparently authoritative foundation for public claims.

The commission later found serious failures in collection, source checking, technical analysis and communication. The errors had been built into the investigation. Weak material had been given weight it could not bear, and the finished assessment did not adequately show how much depended on inference.

The form of the reporting added to the problem. The commission found that short articles in the President’s Daily Brief left little space for qualifications, while attention-grabbing headlines left even less. Repeated items could make a developing account look increasingly well confirmed, although the items depended on the same source. A reader encountering the judgment several mornings in succession might receive a stronger impression without receiving additional independent evidence. As reports were shortened for senior readers, the dependence on one source needed to remain visible.

## What the tubes could tell them

The aluminum tubes became an important part of the nuclear argument. Iraq had sought large quantities of high-strength tubes. In a uranium-enrichment centrifuge, a rapidly spinning rotor helps separate uranium isotopes; suitable tubing could therefore be relevant to a nuclear program. But Iraq also used tubes in conventional rockets. The question was which explanation best fitted these particular purchases.

CIA analysts favored the centrifuge explanation. The Department of Energy’s specialists judged the tubes poorly suited to that use and more plausibly intended for rockets. The State Department’s Bureau of Intelligence and Research, known as INR, agreed with the technical objection.

Yet Energy and INR still disagreed about the overall nuclear assessment. Energy rejected the tubes argument but accepted the estimate’s broader conclusion that Iraq was rebuilding its nuclear program, relying on other indicators. INR dissented from that broader judgment. A technical disagreement over one procurement and a disagreement over the existence of the entire program were different positions.

Energy’s endorsement of the broader judgment could be mistaken for agreement on its principal evidence. Its actual position was more awkward: the program was supposedly being rebuilt, but the tubes were probably for rockets.

Energy’s agreement depended partly on the other indicators listed in the estimate, including procurement and personnel activity. Yet those indicators also needed to be examined for alternative explanations. A magnet or a machine with civilian and military applications does not disclose its intended use merely because a government wants to buy it secretly. Analysts needed a connection between the purchase and an enrichment effort. Accumulating ambiguous purchases could suggest an avenue for investigation without establishing that the program had restarted.

Powell acknowledged the tubes controversy in his [Security Council presentation](https://www.globalsecurity.org/wmd/library/news/iraq/2003/iraq-030205-powell-un-17300pf.htm). He mentioned the rocket explanation. He then argued that unusually exact specifications, repeated procurement attempts and other purchases supported the nuclear interpretation. He gave listeners the alternative, then used his own judgment as a former soldier to dismiss its plausibility.

Inspectors were testing that interpretation before the invasion. On 7 March, Mohamed ElBaradei reported that an international team of centrifuge experts had examined documents, samples and Iraq’s procurement history. They had reconstructed the rocket project and the changes in its specifications. The IAEA found no evidence that the tubes were intended for a project other than reverse-engineering rockets, and judged conversion for centrifuges highly unlikely to be practical.

The inspectors could explain why specifications had changed: successive Iraqi attempts to get the rockets working had produced revisions. Details Powell had presented as suspicious now had a documented explanation.

## One source made to look like several

For the mobile biological laboratories, the crucial weakness was the sourcing. Curveball’s reporting was detailed, and the proposed facilities sounded technically plausible. Mobility also fitted analysts’ expectation that Iraq would adapt to inspections by making production harder to detect.

Defense intelligence collectors failed to validate the source adequately. CIA analysts accepted his account too readily because it fitted what they already expected. A foreign service controlled access to Curveball, leaving American assessments dependent on reports they could not test through their own questioning.

Powell described four sources on mobile facilities. According to the commission, Curveball had supplied roughly a hundred detailed reports; two other sources had supplied one report each. Another source associated with the Iraqi National Congress had already been identified as a fabricator. A warning about that source had been issued, but his reporting had not been properly recalled and continued to appear in finished assessments.

Calling this a body of corroborated reporting concealed the imbalance. A hundred reports from one person are still dependent on that person. Two brief accounts do not necessarily provide independent confirmation of the machinery, production runs and accidents he describes. And a report already judged fabricated should have ceased to support the case altogether.

Concerns about Curveball himself accumulated. The commission distinguished early warnings about unstable behavior from more serious doubts raised later about his truthfulness. Some officers conveyed those doubts within the CIA before Powell’s speech. The record includes conflicting recollections about who passed which warning to whom and how senior managers understood it. The clear outcome was that Powell was not told the source’s reliability had become a serious concern.

Satellite imagery offered a check on what the sources said. Imagery revealed a wall at a claimed site that did not fit the described movement of mobile facilities. Analysts entertained the possibility that the obstacle itself was part of Iraqi deception. Their expectation of concealment helped protect the source from contradictory evidence.

Postwar examination discredited Curveball’s account. It also failed to establish that the Iraqi National Congress had directed him to mislead American intelligence. The INC supplied other unreliable reporting, but combining all defectors into one centrally managed pipeline obscures the separate failures. In the mobile-lab case, source validation, report withdrawal, analysis and warning transmission each broke down.

## The forged documents and the inspection clock

The uranium story had its own chronology. Intelligence reporting alleged an agreement under which Niger would supply Iraq with uranium. The October 2002 estimate mentioned efforts to obtain uranium from Africa, although the claim was not among its key judgments. President Bush referred to British reporting about African uranium in his January 2003 State of the Union address.

Documents purportedly supporting the Niger transaction reached the American embassy in Rome in October 2002. INR raised doubts about their authenticity. Yet the CIA did not promptly subject them to the examination such a consequential claim required. Requests from senior officials continued to receive accounts that omitted those doubts.

When the IAEA obtained and examined the documents, it found errors in format, signatures and content. ElBaradei told the Security Council on 7 March that they were not authentic and that the particular transaction allegations were unfounded. The CIA subsequently recalled the original reporting tied to them.

Someone had manufactured the documents. The failure inside American intelligence was to keep passing on the account without resolving doubts about its supporting evidence. The documents had arrived in October; Bush invoked British reporting about uranium from Africa in January; ElBaradei announced the finding about the Niger documents in March. Authentication came after months in which the allegation could influence the argument for war.

ElBaradei’s [March report](https://www.globalsecurity.org/wmd/library/news/iraq/un/elbaradei-7mar03.pdf) also described the broader nuclear investigation: inspections, environmental sampling, radiation surveys, interviews and examination of procurement records. After three months of intrusive work, the IAEA had found no evidence or plausible indication that Iraq’s nuclear weapons program had revived. It intended to continue investigating and monitoring.

The inspectors were still working, with methods for pursuing unresolved questions. Their findings challenged a major part of the public argument. The choice was whether to give that process further time or proceed to military action.

## When the policy shaped the presentation

British officials were meanwhile trying to reconcile their own legal and political requirements with American plans. A minute of the 23 July 2002 meeting recorded MI6 chief Richard Dearlove reporting that Washington was moving toward military action and that “the intelligence and facts were being fixed around the policy”. In the same discussion, ministers considered military plans, an inspections ultimatum and the legal difficulty of making regime change itself the basis for war.

The later Iraq Inquiry examined both the record and the participants’ disagreements about it. Dearlove challenged the wording and explained that he had been objecting particularly to attempts to link Iraq with terrorism. Matthew Rycroft, who recorded the meeting, checked his notes and retained his account. Even with the disagreement over wording, the record shows senior officials discussing how to justify a war they thought Washington already wanted.

The Inquiry found that British public argument conveyed more certainty than the underlying assessments justified. It also concluded that diplomatic options had not been exhausted when Britain joined the invasion. Officials had to decide what the remaining uncertainty permitted them to do. Continuing inspections was still an available choice.

American investigators asked a related question: did officials accurately describe what intelligence agencies were telling them? The Senate Intelligence Committee’s [2008 report](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/CRPT-110srpt345/pdf/CRPT-110srpt345.pdf) compared major speeches with contemporary intelligence. Its findings were mixed. General statements about possession of chemical and biological weapons were supported by assessments that later proved wrong; some claims about ongoing chemical production failed to convey contemporary uncertainty. Nuclear statements generally reflected the prevailing estimate but inadequately conveyed substantial disagreements. Claims suggesting an Iraqi partnership with al-Qaeda went beyond the intelligence.

Repeating an assessment and deciding how to use it were separate acts. An official could repeat a false assessment without inventing it. The same official could then leave its uncertainties out, imply a stronger relationship with terrorism or present the danger as requiring immediate war. Showing that an agency supported a claim answers only the first question.

The committee’s minority members disputed its methods and conclusions, including its choice and comparison of statements. The committee did not speak unanimously. Its comparison nevertheless helps locate a second failure: speeches could misrepresent even an assessment that was itself mistaken.

## What reached the reader

Much of this argument arrived through news organizations whose readers could not inspect the classified reporting. An official’s account of intelligence could therefore carry more authority than a reporter could independently establish. Publishing the claim accurately as a statement by an official did not settle whether the underlying evidence deserved belief.

In May 2004, the *New York Times* acknowledged weaknesses in its Iraq coverage. An editors’ note conceded that controversial information had sometimes received inadequate qualification or challenge, and that the paper should have pressed harder as evidence failed to emerge. Reporters and editors had made choices about how firmly to state the claims and how hard to test them.

The placement and emphasis of a story affected what a reader would take from it. A dispute over the tubes or a source’s credibility could look like a minor technical objection beside a confident warning about a nuclear threat. Giving the warning the strongest emphasis and the uncertainty little attention changed the account readers received. The press’s responsibility included judging the relationship between the two, rather than treating prominence as something automatically earned by an official’s rank.

## What the search found

The Iraq Survey Group’s postwar findings contradicted the central weapons case. Its report concluded that Iraq’s nuclear program had ended in 1991 and found no concerted effort to restart it. It judged that the undeclared chemical stockpile had been destroyed in 1991 and found no credible indication of resumed chemical-munitions production.

The group had more direct access to Iraq than prewar analysts, including documents, sites and detained senior officials. Its account combined those sources rather than relying on a weapons search alone. It also identified limits: some detainees had reasons to minimize their roles, some refused meaningful cooperation, and the enormous conventional-munitions inventory could not be searched in full. Its conclusions rested on that incomplete but far more extensive record.

The biological history was different. The group judged that Iraq preserved and developed parts of its biological capability into the mid-1990s, then abandoned the existing program. It found no direct evidence of a new program or military biological-weapons work after 1996. The biological program had continued beyond 1991.

The [report’s key findings](https://nsarchive2.gwu.edu/NSAEBB/NSAEBB418/docs/11%20-%20Duelfer%20report%20-%20excerpted%20key%20findings%209-30-04.pdf) also described retained scientific knowledge, dual-use equipment and Saddam’s ambition to recover capabilities after sanctions. Old, abandoned chemical munitions were found. Those findings left a country with dangerous knowledge and ambitions, but without the stockpiles, active production and nuclear reconstruction alleged as the basis for urgent action in 2002–03.

The WMD Commission found no evidence that political pressure had caused analysts to change their judgments. It nevertheless described an atmosphere inhospitable to skepticism and a system that had repeatedly failed to check its assumptions. Its remit did not include deciding how policymakers subsequently used the assessments. That second question required the separate scrutiny of speeches and policy decisions.

The accounts invented by sources survived failures of checking, analysis and management, then reached a political argument that made too little room for uncertainty. Each stage had people responsible for it. By the time investigators reconstructed those choices, invasion and occupation had made their consequences impossible to undo.

PNAC’s preferred outcome was achieved: Saddam was removed. The weapons case that helped justify achieving it did not survive examination. There had been opportunities to slow down: technical dissent, questions about sources, doubtful documents, inspectors asking for time. The existence of those objections in the record shows the limits of simply registering doubt. They were recorded, but the decision went ahead before they were resolved.
