On December 21, 1988, a bomb destroyed Pan Am Flight 103 over Lockerbie, Scotland. All 259 people aboard the New York-bound aircraft died, along with 11 people on the ground. The [British accident investigation](https://www.gov.uk/aaib-reports/boeing-747-121-n739pa-21-december-1988) could say where the explosion began. Damage to wreckage from the forward cargo hold led examiners to a baggage container. Luggage recovered from the debris carried traces of a high-explosive detonation. The flight recorders had stopped abruptly; taken alone, they offered no positive evidence that there had been an explosion. The pattern on the aircraft and its contents supplied what the recordings could not.

The criminal case had to work backward from that container. Its questions were where the suspect suitcase entered the baggage system and who was responsible for putting it there. None could be answered from the damage to the aircraft. Investigators could trace fragments of clothing to a shop. Connecting the sale to a named person meant relying on a shopkeeper’s recollection, his efforts to date the sale, and evidence of who was in Malta at the time.

## How the case reached Malta

Police initially pursued a lead involving the Popular Front for the Liberation of Palestine–General Command. West German police had raided members of the group near Frankfurt two months before the bombing and recovered explosive devices. The discovery gave investigators reason to examine a possible link. Another fact suggested a possible motive: in July 1988, the USS *Vincennes* shot down Iran Air Flight 655, killing all 290 people aboard. To turn a retaliation theory into an attribution, investigators still had to connect people and material from that inquiry to this flight. The Scottish trial judgment considered the group’s activities but did not find that it had carried out the bombing.

The evidence that led to Libya included a circuit-board fragment found among the debris. The trial court accepted evidence identifying it as part of a timer made by the Swiss firm MEBO, which had supplied timers of that type to Libya. That was evidence about a component. It did not identify who used it. Clothing found with the device led to Mary’s House, a shop in Sliema, Malta. Its proprietor, Tony Gauci, recalled selling the items to a man he believed was Libyan. He later said that Abdelbaset al-Megrahi greatly resembled the customer. The judges expressly recognised that the identification was not unequivocal.

The prosecution put those findings together with evidence that Megrahi had worked for Libyan intelligence, was in Malta on December 7, the date the court accepted for the sale, and was at Luqa Airport on the day of the bombing while travelling under another name. At Frankfurt, baggage records included an entry consistent with an item arriving from Malta and going on to the Pan Am feeder flight for Heathrow. There was no direct evidence showing how the suitcase entered the baggage system at Luqa, a gap the judges called a major difficulty. They nevertheless concluded that the combined evidence established the route and Megrahi’s involvement.

On January 31, 2001, three Scottish judges sitting without a jury at Camp Zeist in the Netherlands convicted Megrahi of murder and acquitted his co-defendant, Lamin Fhimah. They accepted the Libyan defector Abdul Majid Giaka’s description of his former intelligence service and its staff, but did not accept his testimony about the alleged operation. Fhimah’s diary contained references to collecting baggage tags. The prosecutors read them as signs of participation in the plot. Even if he had supplied the tags, the court reasoned, that was not enough to show he knew what they would be used for.

## What the appeals tested

In Megrahi’s first appeal, the defence introduced evidence from Heathrow security staff. A padlock securing a door to the airside area at Terminal 3 had been forced in the early hours of the day of the bombing. The breach mattered because a suitcase introduced at Heathrow would not have needed to pass through Malta or Frankfurt at all. In March 2002, the appeal court treated the security breach as potentially significant but found no evidence linking it to the suitcases a baggage handler had seen in the container later that day. The court refused the appeal. The evidence showed an opportunity for unauthorised access, not that anyone had used the door to put the bomb on the aircraft.

The Scottish Criminal Cases Review Commission turned to the basis for fixing the sale at December 7. Gauci’s recollection of the Christmas lights mattered: if they were not yet on, and the lights had been switched on before that date, the sale may have happened earlier. December 7 was the only date supported at trial on which Megrahi could have been the purchaser. The commission also found evidence not disclosed to the defence that Gauci had seen a magazine photograph linking Megrahi to the bombing four days before selecting him in an identification parade. Recognising a face after seeing it in a magazine is not equivalent to recognising it from a shop visit years earlier.

In 2007, the commission referred the conviction back to court because it believed a miscarriage of justice might have occurred. Its published summary did not accept every challenge. It said its inquiries had found nothing to support an allegation that the timer fragment had been fabricated, and nothing to support claims that passenger Khaled Jaafar was involved in the bombing. The referral meant the court should hear another appeal; it was not a finding of innocence. Megrahi abandoned that appeal in 2009 before his release on compassionate grounds. He died in 2012.

The commission referred the case again in 2020, and Megrahi’s family pursued a posthumous appeal. In January 2021 five judges refused it. On the challenge to the identification, the question was whether the original verdict was one a reasonable court could reach on the evidence, not whether other judges would have made the same findings about every witness. The conviction remains in force. That does not convert the shopkeeper’s identification into a certainty or show exactly when the suitcase changed hands.

## What a settlement could settle

In August 2003, Libya wrote to the United Nations Security Council accepting “responsibility for the actions of its officials” and saying it had arranged compensation. The council lifted its sanctions the following month. The commission later considered the letter and did not take it as confirmation of Megrahi’s individual guilt. In [[hypernormalisation]], Adam Curtis reads the settlement as a transaction in which Libya accepted the role its former adversaries assigned it. That is an interpretation of a political bargain. The bargain itself proves neither that the trial was arranged to secure it nor that Libya’s statement settled what every individual had done.

The United States is pursuing a separate criminal case against Abu Agila Mas’ud, the man prosecutors accuse of making the bomb. He pleaded not guilty. In October 2026, a federal judge set jury selection for January 19, 2027. The American case asks what Mas’ud did, a question the Scottish judges did not have to decide to convict Megrahi or acquit Fhimah.

Investigators could point to the part of the aircraft where the bomb went off. The judges could not point to a comparably direct piece of evidence showing where the suitcase was first loaded. The conviction survived appeal because the judges found the evidence sufficient taken together. The gap at the start of the suitcase’s journey was there even in the judgment that accepted the prosecution’s account.
