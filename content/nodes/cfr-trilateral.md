In September 1939, two representatives of the Council on Foreign Relations went to the State Department with an offer. War had begun in Europe. The department needed research, and its staff and budget were stretched. The Council could organize experts to study questions the government would soon have to answer.

The Council found funding and organized the War and Peace Studies project. Its researchers prepared papers for officials who were trying to anticipate the conflict and the settlement that would follow. Research secretaries later worked for both the Council's groups and a new State Department division.

Helping prepare a government's choices is consequential work, even when the final authority lies elsewhere. The Council and the later Trilateral Commission have brought expertise and relationships close to those decisions. Their influence is easier to examine when we can follow a particular paper or appointment, rather than infer a century of policy from shared memberships.

## An organization, a journal and a meeting

The Council on Foreign Relations, or CFR, was founded in New York in 1921. It combines a membership organization with research, meetings and publishing, including *Foreign Affairs*, established the following year. Its own [description of its work](https://www.cfr.org/about) is explicit about seeking an impact on American engagement with the world.

The organization grew partly from relationships formed around the Paris peace negotiations after the First World War. [George Gavrilis's institutional history](https://static.cfr.org/sites/default/files/pdf/cfrcentennialbook.pdf) describes American and British participants discussing an international affairs institute in Paris in 1919. Separate organizations emerged on the two sides of the Atlantic. People who had worked together wanted to keep talking after their official task ended.

The opportunity to meet a future minister or ambassador can outlast the occasion that brought people together. Someone outside that circle may have equally useful knowledge and no comparable chance to be heard. Being present when the relationships form has value of its own.

The CFR's [membership criteria](https://www.cfr.org/membership) include experience, achievement, standing among peers and the ability to contribute to its work. Its board elects members. There is a term program for younger people as well as life membership. These are processes of institutional selection, rather than representation through a public electorate.

In its corporate program, CFR advertises [briefings for executives and small discussions involving business leaders and policymakers](https://www.cfr.org/membership/corporate-membership). A company can value those occasions for understanding risks and testing assumptions. A citizen can reasonably ask whether the interests represented in such discussions are too narrow. The advertised benefit gives the criticism something concrete to address.

An exchange need not involve a bargain to be valuable. An executive who can ask an official a question has an opportunity to explain why a problem matters to the company. The official may learn something useful; the company may gain a hearing unavailable to people affected by its decisions. Repeated over time, that opportunity can make some interests much more familiar to an official than others.

An individual member, a researcher, a director and an author in *Foreign Affairs* have different relationships to the institution. Publishing an argument does not make it the view of every member. Treating all four roles as a single voice makes it harder to discover who actually advocated a policy.

## What the wartime arrangement did

The Council's [1946 account of War and Peace Studies](https://archive.org/details/CouncilOnForeignRelations1946TheWarAndPeaceStudies) describes the September 1939 offer by Hamilton Fish Armstrong and Walter Mallory. The State Department encouraged a plan; Rockefeller Foundation funding helped put it into operation. Experts worked in groups, discussing drafts before sending finished statements and accounts of the discussion to Washington.

Each paper involved choices before it reached an official: the problem chosen, the specialists invited, the evidence assembled and the discussion preserved in the accompanying account. By the time an official read the paper, much of the work of framing a decision had already happened.

The relationship became closer in 1941. The State Department formed a Division of Special Research and engaged the research secretaries serving the Council's groups. They were allowed to continue their Council work part-time. In 1942, participants also joined the department's advisory machinery for postwar policy.

The shared personnel and regular supply of analysis gave the Council a working role in official planning. State Department officials had helped establish the arrangement and retained responsibility for the decisions. Both parts of the relationship belong in the account.

The responsibilities extended beyond the final signature. An official who chooses an outside adviser exercises judgment. A foundation that supports one research program enables certain questions to receive sustained attention. A researcher who presents alternatives can make some courses of action appear practical and others remote. Each can influence an outcome without possessing all the others' powers.

Gavrilis's later history reports 682 memoranda. Its Greenland example shows what one of them could do. A March 1940 memorandum considered the territory's importance for transatlantic aviation and the danger if Germany overran Denmark. Gavrilis recounts the Council's claim that the paper reached Roosevelt, who consulted Isaiah Bowman, one of the group's members. A geographical argument had reached someone able to act on it.

The consultation does not establish how much the paper changed Roosevelt's thinking. As Gavrilis acknowledges, assessing how policy would have differed without the project is difficult. A comparison with official deliberations would need to establish whether other advisers were making the same case and what alternatives the president considered. A paper can originate an idea, improve it or reinforce an existing preference; those are different contributions, even when the eventual policy resembles its recommendation.

## Why the Trilateral Commission was different

The Trilateral Commission was founded in 1973, more than half a century after the CFR. David Rockefeller helped establish it, and Zbigniew Brzezinski was its first full-time director. Rockefeller also chaired the CFR from 1970 to 1985, a position recorded in the Council's [historical leadership lists](https://www.cfr.org/board-directors). The overlap came decades into the Council's existence.

The Commission's original three regions were North America, Western Europe and Japan. The [Japan Center for International Exchange](https://jcie.org/programs/trilateral-commission/), which served as its Asian secretariat until 2024, describes a forum organized through regional groups, task forces and an annual plenary. The Asian grouping later widened beyond Japan. Its account also specifies that current national cabinet ministers are excluded from membership.

The choice to include Japan changed whom the discussion brought together. An Atlantic conversation could no longer be treated as sufficient for problems involving three major industrial regions. This was a proposal about where cooperation should begin and who should participate in designing it. It was also selective: representing the industrialized regions in a private forum is different from representing everyone affected by their policies.

The Commission's organizational material, reproduced in a [1981 Congressional Research Service information packet](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/19810501_IP0092_2d3ea09e2c6068af730f41d315f4ea490bc91878.pdf), places task forces at the center of its program. Authors from the regions prepared reports for discussion at a plenary before publication. Responsibility for their analyses and conclusions rested with the authors.

A report could introduce a proposal across national boundaries and give participants a common vocabulary for discussing it. Its reception could also expose a disagreement. One of the Commission's best-known reports did both.

## An argument about too much participation

*The Crisis of Democracy* appeared in 1975. Michel Crozier, Samuel Huntington and Joji Watanuki wrote its regional studies. The introductory note assigns responsibility to them; it does not present the book as a unanimous declaration by all Commission members.

In the American chapter, Huntington argued that expanded political participation and demands on government could weaken its capacity to govern. He called for moderation of an “excess of democracy” and described some political noninvolvement as helping a democratic system function. He acknowledged that marginalization was undemocratic, and called for self-restraint from all groups as excluded people became participants. These arguments appear openly in the [published report](https://www.luttedeclasse.org/dossier45/Commission_Trilaterale_crise_democratie_1975.pdf).

There is a real problem behind the argument. Governments have limited resources. Citizens can make demands that cannot all be satisfied together, and a decision can become harder when more organized interests contest it. Expertise also has a legitimate role: knowing how to build a bridge does not depend on winning a vote.

A preference for political restraint goes further. People previously excluded from a decision may create difficulties precisely because the existing arrangement imposed costs on them. Describing those difficulties as excessive demands risks treating the comfort of established decision-makers as the measure of a healthy democracy. A system can become harder to govern because more people finally have a chance to object.

The report also printed Ralf Dahrendorf's criticism in its account of the Kyoto discussion. He questioned whether the emphasis on governability was really seeking increased government power rather than restoring something democracies had lost. His objection exposes a choice within the discussion itself: how much direction a democratic government should be expected to give society.

Huntington's argument deserves criticism for its treatment of participation. Dahrendorf's objection deserves to remain beside it. Turning the volume into a statement of what every member wanted would erase the disagreement the Commission chose to publish.

## Carter's appointments, and a decision they did not settle

The Carter administration brought several former Trilateral participants into senior office. The packet's April 1980 *U.S. News & World Report* account identifies Carter, Vice President Walter Mondale, Secretary of State Cyrus Vance, Defense Secretary Harold Brown and national security adviser Brzezinski among them. Such a forum can help people become familiar with one another before they serve together. Those relationships may influence whom an incoming president trusts and what his advisers take for granted.

Carter publicly mentioned his Commission work while campaigning. In a [March 1976 foreign-policy speech in Chicago](https://www.presidency.ucsb.edu/documents/remarks-the-chicago-council-foreign-relations), he described it as a body making recommendations on international problems. He also called for policy shaped with Congress and broad public participation. The [Chicago council he addressed](https://globalaffairs.org/about-us/our-history) was a different organization from the New York CFR.

His speech tells us how he presented the experience to voters. It cannot establish how much a particular meeting changed his mind or who caused him to appoint a particular adviser. Those questions require correspondence, accounts of the selection and records of the policy under consideration. A membership list provides names to investigate, not a completed explanation of the administration.

One consequential disagreement survived those shared connections. On April 21, 1980, Vance submitted his resignation because he could not support Carter's decision concerning the rescue operation in Iran. When the letters were released on April 28, Carter expressly identified that disagreement and accepted the resignation. Their [exchange](https://www.presidency.ucsb.edu/documents/department-state-exchange-letters-the-resignation-cyrus-r-vance-secretary) records a secretary of state unable to back his president on an issue both regarded as exceptionally important.

The letters do not make the preceding relationships irrelevant. They show why relationships alone cannot tell us what the participants will decide. Vance's earlier association with Carter and the Commission did not resolve his judgment about the operation. Carter's authority as president allowed him to choose a course his secretary of state opposed. The disagreement ended with Vance leaving office.

Contemporary reporting also described proposals failing to gain ground. William Greider's June 1978 *Washington Post* report, also preserved in the packet, found well-connected participants frustrated by differences among countries and the limited progress of proposals on money and energy. He described the usefulness of contacts alongside the difficulty of translating discussion into action. The contacts remained valuable even when proposals stalled.

## Private conversation, public consequences

There is a defensible reason for allowing an official to think aloud without treating every tentative observation as settled policy. There is also a public cost when the people able to hear and question that official are selected privately.

The CFR's [published rules in its 2009 annual report](https://static.cfr.org/sites/default/files/report_pdf/CFR_annual_report_2009_0.pdf) describe nonattribution rather than a ban on using what participants learn. The explanatory text allows an official to report to colleagues through a limited-circulation memorandum, while prohibiting public circulation of an attributed statement under the rule. The rules also say the Council itself takes no foreign-policy position.

That arrangement permits ideas to travel while limiting the public record of who said what. It can encourage candor, as the institution argues, and make influence harder for an outsider to reconstruct. The absence of an institutional policy does not prevent an influential author or participant from advancing one.

The organizer's choice of participants helps determine whose concerns the official hears. To judge the consequence, we need to follow the advice into the decision and identify where it encountered objections, budgets or legal limits. The same examination is useful for [[bilderberg]], though its arrangements need their own account.

An official may leave a private conversation better prepared to make a public decision. People affected by that decision still have a claim to know why it was made and whose interests received a hearing. The discretion that helps a discussion succeed should not become a permanent shelter from explaining its consequences.
