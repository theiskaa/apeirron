By the time federal investigators were looking for the man who had rented a truck in Junction City, Kansas, he was already in an Oklahoma jail. On the morning of April 19, 1995, a state trooper had stopped Timothy McVeigh because his car had no license plate. The trooper arrested him on a weapons charge. Independently, investigators working at the ruined Alfred P. Murrah Federal Building recovered a truck axle with an identification number. They traced it to the shop that had rented out the truck, and accounts from the shop helped lead them to McVeigh.

The bombing killed 168 people, including nineteen children, and injured hundreds more. The axle established a link from the blast site to the rental counter; it could not show who else had known what McVeigh planned. The search for a second man began with a recollection of who had stood at the counter, not with a second name on the paperwork.

## What the trials established

Prosecutors had more than a drawing of the renter. Records connected McVeigh to the truck and to other preparations. Michael and Lori Fortier described conversations with him before the bombing, including his intention to attack a federal building. Michael Fortier cooperated under a plea agreement, which gave him a reason to help the prosecution. The jurors could assess his account alongside evidence that did not depend on his testimony, including rental and storage records. They found McVeigh guilty on eleven federal counts in 1997. Eight of those counts were for murder; the indictment covered the killing of eight federal law enforcement officers, not every person who died.

Terry Nichols was tried separately. A federal jury convicted him of conspiracy and involuntary manslaughter; an Oklahoma jury later convicted him on 161 counts of first-degree murder. The two verdicts concerned distinct charges and proceedings. The government never needed to claim that McVeigh planned the attack without help. It had to prove what the defendants it charged had done. McVeigh was executed in 2001. Nichols was sentenced to life in prison.

McVeigh had told the Fortiers that the bombing would fall on the anniversary of the end of the [[waco]] siege. For him, the federal building stood for the agencies he blamed for the deaths at Mount Carmel. It was a workplace and a place where other people went to get services, and it housed a day care center. His explanation helps account for the date and target. It does not turn the people who died into agents of the government decisions he opposed.

## What a sketch could show

The FBI circulated sketches of two men after interviewing people at the Kansas rental shop. The second, known as John Doe No. 2, was portrayed as someone who might have accompanied the renter. In June 1995 investigators said they had cleared Todd Bunting, a soldier who resembled the sketch. Bunting had been at the same shop on another day. That gave investigators a concrete explanation for how details from two visits might have been combined. It did not establish that everyone who reported a possible companion had seen Bunting or had made the same mistake.

The distinction matters because a witness can be right about seeing a person and wrong about which visit the person was part of. Checking rental dates could tell the investigators something about that particular account. It could not establish that McVeigh had no contact with anyone else. Conversely, an unidentified person in a sketch is not independent evidence of a conspirator. To make that case, investigators would need to connect a person to the preparations or the attack, not simply find someone who fit the drawing.

## What the informant account did not establish

McVeigh’s lawyers sought to introduce testimony from Carol Howe, a federal informant who had visited Elohim City, a white separatist community in eastern Oklahoma. The appellate opinion describes what the defense expected her to say, not testimony the jury heard. According to that account, Howe would have described men there discussing possible attacks on federal buildings, including potential targets in Oklahoma City or Tulsa. Howe had also said that two men there resembled the sketches. The defense offered records it said showed that investigators curtailed the Elohim City inquiry after McVeigh’s arrest. It hoped to show both that other people could have carried out the bombing and that agents had not followed a plausible lead.

The trial judge excluded the proposed evidence. On appeal, the judges considered what it might show about other perpetrators and about the quality of the investigation. They concluded that the proposed testimony did not sufficiently connect the men Howe described to this bombing. They also read the records differently from the defense: the inquiry remained open after agents had focused on McVeigh. The judges upheld the trial court’s decision to leave both out. That decision did not certify how every tip had been handled. It did not establish that Howe had given agents a timely, specific warning about McVeigh’s plan either. The record identifies reports of threatening talk and a dispute about pursuing the leads, not proof that the government knew who was bringing a bomb to which building on April 19.

The government did fail to give the defense records it was required to disclose. In May 2001, a week before McVeigh’s scheduled execution, federal officials told the defense that more than 700 investigative documents had not been turned over before the trials. The government acknowledged violating a discovery order, and the attorney general delayed the execution by a month. The Justice Department’s inspector general traced the failure to problems in how the FBI gathered and tracked records. It found no evidence that government personnel deliberately withheld exculpatory material. ([Inspector general’s report](https://oig.justice.gov/special/0203/exec.htm).)

The public’s access to the records was a different matter. Jesse Trentadue filed records requests concerning the bombing, including possible involvement by foreign nationals. In 2010, in one of his cases, a federal judge upheld the CIA’s withholding of twelve documents under exemptions in the Freedom of Information Act. The judge relied on the agency’s descriptions and sworn explanations. The ruling left the contents unavailable to Trentadue; it did not determine whether anyone else was involved in the bombing.

## The law that followed

Congress was debating counterterrorism legislation before the bombing. The Omnibus Counterterrorism Act had been introduced in the Senate in February 1995. A separate bill became the Antiterrorism and Effective Death Penalty Act in April 1996, just over a year after the attack. It set a general one-year filing deadline for state prisoners seeking federal habeas review and created a process for designating foreign terrorist organizations. Section 810 ordered a study of existing electronic surveillance and reporting on how those powers were used. Which powers the act changed has to be read in the enacted text, not inferred from the fact that Congress acted after a bombing. The law that followed [[nine-eleven]] was a different statute passed in different circumstances.

The most concrete answer investigators found about the second man in the sketch was a date: Bunting had been in the shop on another day. They did not need to explain every sighting to establish who rented the truck. But they did owe the defense the records that would let it test how they followed that lead and others.
