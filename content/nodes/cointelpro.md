In January 1969, the FBI's Chicago office wanted to send a letter to Jeff Fort, leader of the Blackstone Rangers. It would warn him that the Black Panthers were planning to kill him. The writer would pretend to be an unaffiliated Black man passing along information from Panther friends.

There was already tension between the groups. Fred Hampton and Bobby Rush had met Fort to discuss cooperation, but neither side wanted to accept the other's leadership. Meetings had broken down; members had been arrested after a shooting. The FBI proposed adding a fictitious assassination plot to that dispute. Its internal explanation anticipated retaliation against the Panthers or their leaders. Hoover authorized the letter on January 30.

The Senate investigators who later examined the operation could not connect that letter to a subsequent clash. They could, however, read the proposal and authorization. Federal officials had considered how to provoke an armed group against another organization, put their reasoning in writing, and approved the attempt.

The proposed letter used information gathered by the FBI to make a local conflict more dangerous. Its recipient would be left to act on a threat invented in a government office. This was the kind of work COINTELPRO authorized.

## What the Bureau set out to do

COINTELPRO was the FBI's abbreviation for Counterintelligence Program. Between 1956 and 1971, the Bureau used the designation for a series of domestic campaigns intended to weaken organizations it regarded as threats. Agents spread anonymous accusations, cultivated hostile reporting, interfered with employment and alliances, and encouraged suspicion inside groups. Targets usually had no way to identify the government as the source of their trouble.

The [Church Committee's staff report](https://aarclibrary.org/publib/church/reports/book3/pdf/ChurchB3_1_COINTELPRO.pdf), published in 1976, reconstructed five principal programs from Bureau records and testimony. They began with the Communist Party USA in 1956, followed by the Socialist Workers Party in 1961, white supremacist organizations in 1964, organizations classified as Black nationalist in 1967, and the New Left in 1968. The Socialist Workers program ended in 1969; the others formally ended in April 1971. Investigators also found additional operations, including a campaign against Puerto Rican independence organizations.

The headings reflected the Bureau's view of the people it investigated. The Southern Christian Leadership Conference, led by Martin Luther King Jr., appeared among the targets of the Black nationalist program despite its commitment to nonviolence. A label broad enough to include both King's organization and groups expecting armed confrontation had already done much of the work of deciding who was dangerous.

There were genuine crimes among the activities that interested the FBI. The Ku Klux Klan practiced racist terror. Some people in other targeted movements committed bombings, shootings, and other violence. Investigating such acts was part of the Bureau's work. COINTELPRO authorized interventions against a much wider field of political activity: recruiting members, raising money, forming coalitions, publishing arguments, and gaining a hearing.

An organization could attract intervention because its influence was growing, without agents having to establish that the growth involved a crime. Its declining membership could then count as success even if no crime had been prevented or prosecuted.

The Klan program complicates any account in which the FBI simply opposed every kind of political change in exactly the same way. Its campaign addressed organizations responsible for racist violence and, according to the Senate report, generally remained closer to its original list of targets than the expanding campaigns against the left. Bureau officials credited it with important successes. Even there, however, a useful intelligence lead, a lawful prosecution, and an anonymous attempt to undermine an organization were different exercises of power. Success against the Klan could not confer a general authority to sabotage political associations.

## From a foreign adversary to a domestic opponent

The first program grew out of the Cold War and the FBI's work against Soviet espionage. Bureau officials believed techniques used against foreign intelligence could also weaken the American Communist Party. Informants could intensify disagreements; anonymous material could make members doubt their leaders; disclosure to an employer or another organization could isolate a person politically.

The Communist Party had a real history of connections to Soviet intelligence. That background helped officials treat a domestic party as an adversary against which covert action was available. But the campaign soon reached people whose conduct was much further removed from espionage.

A 1960 directive expanded efforts against supposed Communist influence in other organizations. An accusation could damage both the alleged Communist and the civil rights or peace organization with which that person worked. A newspaper story about Communist participation in a march could be intended to discredit the march itself. The question shifted from whether someone was working for a foreign service to whether their presence could be used against a cause.

The Socialist Workers Party made the change particularly plain. It was Trotskyist and hostile to the Communist Party. The Bureau had no evidence of its involvement in espionage and later conceded that the organization had not engaged in organizational violence or criminal steps to overthrow the government. Nevertheless, its political program, electoral activity, and participation in civil rights and antiwar causes made it a target.

Opposition to one targeted organization offered no protection from becoming a target in turn. The FBI had taken authority to intervene in the political field, and could apply that authority to rival groups for different reasons.

Offices with informants, membership lists, and information about a group's disputes could propose further uses for what they knew. A disagreement between organizers became an opportunity. A sympathetic donor became someone to alarm. A family relationship offered another route of pressure. Information collected in the name of security acquired a second life as material for interference.

## Approval was part of the problem

Senior officials approved many of these activities. Field offices proposed actions; headquarters reviewed them. According to the Senate report, approval rarely came below the assistant director responsible for the division, and often came from one of the Bureau's three most senior men.

The report counted 2,370 approved actions across the five principal programs. An action might be a mailing, a planted allegation, or a more elaborate attempt to disrupt a group. That count is neither a count of all people harmed nor a complete inventory of domestic surveillance. It describes the recorded proposals that passed through these particular programs.

Review could include an explicit assessment of harm. Officials knew that an accusation of informing could expose someone to violence. They considered whether an anonymous letter might break up a marriage. Their question could then be whether that damage would produce enough disruption to justify the operation.

Internal supervision therefore offered little protection when supervisors accepted the objective. A proposal could be carefully reviewed, approved at a high level, and still amount to deliberate abuse of the person receiving it. The surviving paperwork identifies who proposed an action, who approved it, and what they expected it to achieve.

The record of outside knowledge is less complete. Hoover sent officials in the Eisenhower administration a description of the Communist Party campaign in 1958. Later communications gave incoming Kennedy officials some information about that effort, and Johnson administration officials received information about disruption of the Klan. The Church investigation found no equivalent evidence of disclosure for all the other programs.

The surviving notices do not show that presidents directed every campaign. They do show that some officials outside the Bureau received information about disruption, alongside the extensive record of decisions taken at headquarters.

## Whose account of a protest counted

The New Left program reached a loosely defined collection of campus, antiwar, and other protest activity. Headquarters wanted information about activists' sexual conduct and living arrangements that could be used to embarrass them. It also wanted examples of administrators taking a firm line against demonstrators. Reports could confirm an institutional preference about how campuses should be governed as readily as identify a security threat.

The instructions after the 1968 Democratic National Convention in Chicago are particularly revealing. Amid allegations of police brutality, headquarters asked the Chicago office to gather evidence that would disprove the charges and consider using cooperative news outlets to counter them. A later instruction sought informant accounts of demonstrators staging incidents or provoking the authorities.

The request had decided the answer before the evidence arrived. An agency able to investigate police misconduct was asking its personnel to help refute the allegation instead. Attorney General Ramsey Clark later told the Senate he had not known about those instructions and considered them improper. The Bureau's relationship with local police had made independent work by the Justice Department's Civil Rights Division necessary in misconduct cases.

Files and informants could supply an apparently authoritative account of a public confrontation while headquarters selected the account it wanted. A person reading the resulting coverage might never know that the same agency gathering information had a stake in defending one of the participants.

## A directive against political growth

On March 4, 1968, headquarters expanded the Black nationalist program to more field offices and set out five long-term goals. Preventing violence was one. The others included obstructing coalitions, preventing the emergence of a unifying leader, denying groups public respectability, and curbing recruitment, especially among young people.

The memo discussed a possible Black political “messiah” and named King among the figures of concern. His commitment to nonviolence did not put him outside the campaign. His ability to reach people made him important to it.

Read together, the goals turned ordinary measures of political effectiveness into warning signs. A coalition could join people who had previously acted separately. Public credibility could bring new supporters. Recruitment could sustain a movement beyond its current leadership. Those were precisely the capacities an organization needed to influence public life, and the Bureau intended to impede them.

This also created a poor way to judge the program's results. Officials could count a split or a failed alliance as a security success without showing what violence the alliance would have produced. A group that grew more effective appeared more threatening; one that weakened appeared to vindicate the intervention. The assessment depended heavily on the Bureau's original view of the group.

For organizers, the damage could remain private and difficult to explain. A supporter withdrew. A meeting fell through. A rumor reached another chapter. Without access to the files, participants had to interpret those events through the relationships they already knew. The concealed intervention was designed to make them blame one another.

## The campaign against King

The FBI's effort against King began before the 1967 program heading and extended across several kinds of activity. Treating every episode as an operation under the same administrative caption obscures how the campaign developed.

Concern about Communist influence among King's advisers supplied an investigative rationale. Attorney General Robert Kennedy authorized telephone surveillance in October 1963. The Bureau also placed microphones in hotel rooms. Approval of telephone taps did not supply an unlimited authorization for every method subsequently used, much less for a campaign to destroy King's standing.

The information gathered became material for personal attacks. In November 1964, the Bureau sent King an anonymous package containing a recording and a letter that threatened exposure of his private life. The letter pretended to come from outside the Bureau and used language understood as urging suicide. It imposed a deadline for action and threatened public disgrace. The House investigators later found a copy in Assistant Director William Sullivan’s files but could not fix final personal responsibility for the letter.

The [House Select Committee's later examination](https://www.archives.gov/research/jfk/select-committee-report/part-2e.html) traced the campaign through Bureau records and sworn testimony. It described a security investigation becoming an effort to discredit King, with Hoover's hostility helping to sustain it. The files also contained professional assessments that Communist influence on the civil rights movement was weak. Those assessments did not bring the campaign to an end.

King's political leadership and his private life had become joined in an operation whose intended audience included supporters, journalists, and institutions capable of conferring public legitimacy. The Bureau could not defeat his arguments by exposing a crime, so it sought material that might make others refuse to hear him.

King's family had ample reason to distrust the FBI when it investigated his murder. The question of responsibility for the 1968 assassination has a different evidentiary record, examined in [[mlk-assassination]]. The officials who tried to destroy his leadership were responsible for that campaign whether or not they had any part in his death.

## Letters, breakfasts, and a television appearance

The [Senate's separate study of the Black Panther campaign](https://www.aarclibrary.org/publib/church/reports/book3/pdf/ChurchB3_3_BlackPanthers.pdf) shows how different kinds of interference could work together. The Panthers were an armed political organization, and there were real conflicts involving members, rivals, and police. The Bureau also attacked activities through which the party acquired support outside those confrontations.

In San Diego, a church supported the Panthers' breakfast program for children. The FBI sent an anonymous letter to the bishop and obtained approval for three telephone calls pretending to come from parishioners. One caller would threaten to withhold financial support. Another would seek the priest's removal. An apparent dispute between a church and its own congregation would conceal a federal intervention against a community program.

The field office later reported that the priest was transferred and that the breakfast arrangement was not renewed. Those reports give the Bureau's assessment of its success; they do not independently establish that the calls alone produced the outcome. They nevertheless show what agents were trying to accomplish. The meal program's usefulness to families did not shield it from attack when that usefulness also helped the Panthers.

The coloring book episode followed a similar approach. The FBI circulated a violent Panther-associated coloring book to potential supporters, including businesses, to damage the party's reputation. Panther leadership had disavowed the book and ordered its destruction, according to testimony the Senate obtained. The record supports the Bureau's exploitation and distribution of the material. It does not establish the familiar claim that FBI artists originally created it.

A revealing Chicago operation used an outstanding arrest warrant. On January 24, 1969, Hampton was due at a local television studio to record an interview for broadcast the next day. The FBI passed information so that local police could arrest him at the studio that evening. Its subsequent reporting emphasized the embarrassment caused to the Panthers, and headquarters congratulated the office.

A warrant can have a lawful basis while its timing serves a separate political purpose. Here, the Bureau's own account identified interruption of a public appearance as an achievement. The arrest disrupted the planned interview and supplied a different story about Hampton.

These operations reached beyond party members. They recruited the authority of a bishop, the concerns of donors, a business's fear of association, and the spectacle of an arrest. Institutions that appeared to be making independent decisions could be responding to information or pressure whose origin they did not know.

## The danger of engineering suspicion

The proposed letter to Jeff Fort sought to turn this interference toward retaliation. The Bureau knew that the Panthers and Rangers were armed and that relations were already strained. It proposed exploiting that situation with an invented threat against Fort's life. The internal argument anticipated retaliation; the anonymous voice supplied a plausible reason for it.

Elsewhere, agents encouraged hostility between the Panthers and Ron Karenga's US organization. Members of those groups were involved in deadly violence, including the January 1969 killings of Panthers Bunchy Carter and John Huggins at UCLA. The Senate investigation documented the Bureau's efforts to aggravate the conflict while acknowledging that it could not trace particular killings directly to those interventions.

That limit matters to assigning responsibility for a death. It does not erase the choice to add accusations to an already violent rivalry. Officials were taking risks with other people's lives while preserving their own ability to deny involvement.

Accusing someone of being an informant worked on the same vulnerability. In an infiltrated organization, the accusation was believable because real informants existed. A fabricated allegation could isolate a person, force them out, or expose them to attack. Even an innocent member had little way to settle it: denying a secret relationship rarely disproves it.

The December 4, 1969, Chicago police raid that killed Hampton and Mark Clark brought the FBI's involvement into a different setting. The Senate report reproduced Bureau records crediting information from its informant, including a floor plan, with helping the police prepare for the raid. That is a specific, documented contribution to an operation in which people died.

The ensuing civil litigation also needs its procedural history. In [1979, the federal appeals court](https://law.justia.com/cases/federal/appellate-courts/F2/600/600/231409/) reversed major portions of judgments that had prevented the plaintiffs' claims from going to the jury. It found sufficient evidence to require further proceedings, assessing that evidence under the standard favorable to the plaintiffs at that stage. That ruling was consequential, but was not a final jury verdict establishing every allegation about the raid.

The word “neutralize” appears in many of these records. What it meant in practice varied from embarrassment to the disruption of an organization, with some operations risking physical violence. In the Hampton case, the floor plan and exchanges with police tell us more about federal participation than the shared vocabulary alone.

## When the targets could obtain the records

The program's exposure began before Watergate. Documents taken in the March 1971 burglary of the FBI office in Media, Pennsylvania, reached the press. Some bore the previously unfamiliar COINTELPRO designation. That discovery gave journalists something specific to pursue rather than another general allegation of political surveillance.

NBC reporter Carl Stern sought the directives establishing, changing, and ending the New Left program. The Justice Department refused disclosure. In [September 1973, a federal judge ruled for Stern](https://law.justia.com/cases/federal/district-courts/FSupp/367/1316/1425510/) after inspecting the documents privately. The government had invoked protections for internal practices, agency deliberations, and law-enforcement files. The judge found that those claims did not justify withholding the requested directives.

The decision opened an official record of purpose. Victims could describe strange letters or failed meetings, but the directives showed that political disruption was an assigned task. The Church investigation subsequently brought proposals, authorizations, reports, and testimony together on a much larger scale.

The Socialist Workers Party's lawsuit offered another route to the files. In [a 1986 judgment](https://law.justia.com/cases/federal/district-courts/FSupp/642/1357/2398821/), Judge Thomas Griesa examined disruption, burglaries, and informant activity. He identified seventeen disruption operations for which the record showed some intended harm had occurred, rather than awarding damages merely because agents had proposed doing harm.

The resulting award for those operations was $42,500. Separate awards covered 193 surreptitious entries into party and youth-organization offices in New York, and a period of informant intrusion that remained legally recoverable. The total was $264,000. Other claims failed on procedural grounds or for lack of proof; an individual's claim about losing his university job failed because he had not established that the FBI caused the dismissal.

The government's own records connected some interventions to a loss of members, damaged alliances, or disrupted events. The party had been made to spend years proving harms that the Bureau had once recorded as accomplishments.

Money offered only a rough remedy. A missed alliance cannot be rerun under the original conditions, and a discredited organizer cannot recover the exact audience they might have had. The litigation did make the source of some injuries public. For an organization deliberately encouraged to suspect its own members, that knowledge had value beyond the award.

## An ending, and an unfinished obligation

The FBI formally discontinued the programs in April 1971. The termination instruction nevertheless allowed exceptional proposals under individual case headings. The Church investigation found examples of disruptive action after the formal ending and explained why it could not determine their full extent by searching a central COINTELPRO file. The record warrants concern about continuation, without supporting the claim that every later domestic investigation belonged to an unchanged secret program.

The exposure contributed to real institutional changes. Attorney General Edward Levi issued domestic security guidelines in 1976. Congress created permanent intelligence oversight committees, and the Foreign Intelligence Surveillance Act of 1978 introduced a special judicial process for covered foreign-intelligence surveillance. These measures addressed different parts of the broader record of intelligence abuse; FISA was not a general court-approval system for every activity described here.

Rules also require accurate accounts from the people applying them. A [2010 Justice Department inspector general review](https://oig.justice.gov/archives/semiannual/1011/fbi.htm) of investigations involving domestic advocacy groups did not find that the groups had been targeted solely for exercising First Amendment rights. It did find weak bases for some inquiries, investigations continued without adequate justification, and improper retention of information. In the case of a Pittsburgh antiwar event, inaccurate explanations reached Congress through the FBI director. Investigators considered efforts to justify the surveillance more likely than extraordinary carelessness.

The later review investigated different cases under different rules. Yet its account of a false explanation reaching Congress shows how oversight can fail when it depends on the institution being overseen to supply the facts. A requirement to report can accomplish little if the report substitutes a defensible story for the decision actually made.

COINTELPRO's files show officials rewarding themselves for weakening other people's capacity to organize. The operations reached a donor's check, a church breakfast, a proposed coalition, a television booking. These were places where people acquired the means to act together. The Bureau treated their success as something it was entitled to prevent. Effective oversight has to reach that decision, before an approved technique becomes another privately recorded accomplishment.
