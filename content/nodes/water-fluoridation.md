The early case for water fluoridation began with damaged teeth. Dentists investigating the brown, mottled enamel of children in Colorado found an unexpected companion to the staining: resistance to decay. The eventual proposal was to use less of the substance responsible, seeking protection without the conspicuous damage.

That history makes dose central to the subject. Fluoride can help prevent cavities, and excessive exposure during tooth development can alter enamel. The present dispute adds harder questions about brain development, the additional benefit of fluoridated water where toothpaste is widely used, and who should decide what a shared supply contains.

## The water and the toothpaste

Frederick McKay began investigating stained teeth in Colorado Springs in 1901. Water analyses eventually identified high natural fluoride concentrations. Public Health Service researcher H. Trendley Dean then studied the relationship between water concentrations, enamel changes and decay. The [National Institute of Dental and Craniofacial Research's history](https://www.nidcr.nih.gov/health-info/fluoride/the-story-of-fluoridation) describes the reasoning that led from those observations to deliberate adjustment of a water supply.

Grand Rapids, Michigan, began fluoridating in 1945. Researchers examined schoolchildren over time; the institute's historical account reports a decline of more than 60 percent in the decay rate among children born after fluoridation began. Those children grew up before fluoride toothpaste became widely available. Their experience leaves the additional benefit in a population already using toothpaste to be measured.

Fluoride's preventive action is largely at the tooth surface. As the [2015 U.S. Public Health Service recommendation](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/FR-2015-05-01/pdf/2015-10201.pdf) explains, fluoride in saliva and dental plaque helps restore mineral to enamel. Water and food or beverages prepared with it can maintain low concentrations in the mouth. Toothpaste supplies fluoride by another route.

Water’s additional benefit has to be measured against the prevention people already receive. Widespread use of fluoride toothpaste changes the comparison. A contemporary water study needs to account for prevention that people obtain from other sources.

Other fluoride sources also changed the exposure picture. In 2015, the Public Health Service replaced its previous range of 0.7–1.2 milligrams per liter with a single target of 0.7 mg/L. It considered other fluoride sources, fluorosis and updated evidence about water consumption. This was a recommendation for systems adding fluoride, rather than an instruction to bring every naturally fluoridated supply down to that level.

## What a quarter of a tooth means

The [2024 Cochrane review](https://doi.org/10.1002/14651858.CD010856.pub3) separated older studies from those conducted after 1975, when fluoride toothpaste was widely available. For the contemporary comparison of changes in baby teeth that were decayed, missing because of decay or filled, it combined two nonrandomized studies involving 2,908 children.

The estimated additional reduction associated with starting fluoridation was 0.24 teeth per child. The 95 percent confidence interval ran from −0.03 to 0.52, and the reviewers rated the evidence low certainty. The estimate included the possibility of no additional benefit. It described changes over study follow-up, not a quarter of a tooth saved every year.

The comparison uses changes in both groups. Decay can decline where fluoridation is introduced and where it is not, because other prevention and living conditions change too. Counting the full decline in the fluoridated area as the intervention’s benefit would assign it improvements that might have occurred anyway. The estimate tries to isolate the additional difference.

The unit can sound trivial because nobody has a quarter of a cavity. It is an average across children, some with no affected teeth and others with several. To judge its importance, a community would need to know where the prevented decay falls, how serious it would otherwise become and what the program costs. Multiplying the average by a population is not enough to discover who benefits.

The reviewers judged the estimated contemporary benefit smaller and its size uncertain. The review also lacked eligible adult caries studies and could not determine whether fluoridation changed socioeconomic disparities or what happened to decay after cessation.

A broad delivery system could help close an access gap, but its reach alone does not measure whether it does. The distribution of benefit matters as much as the average when inequality is the justification for a program.

A proposal to stop adding fluoride needs to explain how replacement prevention will reach families. If dental visits depend on payment or transport that a family cannot arrange, the promised service may remain out of reach. Funding, uptake and the demands placed on households belong in the comparison of policies.

## Concentration is only part of exposure

A water concentration is a quantity per volume. At 0.7 mg/L, one liter contains 0.7 milligrams of fluoride; two liters contain 1.4 milligrams. This is arithmetic, not a recommended intake or a measurement of a particular person. The same concentration can contribute different amounts to different people's daily intake.

Other sources and body size also matter when describing exposure. Water concentration, total milligrams ingested, dose relative to body weight and a concentration measured in urine answer different questions. Two results using “mg/L” are not necessarily measurements of the same thing: a liter of tap water and a liter of urine are different materials.

Dental fluorosis illustrates why timing matters too. It arises from fluoride ingestion while enamel is developing. The 2015 Public Health Service document describes changes ranging from faint white markings to pitting in severe cases. A mild visible change is not interchangeable with severe enamel damage, and neither is a direct measurement of cognitive development.

A mottled tooth records a disturbance during enamel formation. It does not measure a change in intelligence. For that question, researchers need cognitive assessments and exposure histories, rather than an inference drawn from the appearance of teeth.

## The evidence about children's intelligence

The [National Toxicology Program's 2024 review](https://ntp.niehs.nih.gov/research/assessments/noncancer/completed/fluoride) concluded with moderate confidence that higher fluoride exposures, such as drinking water above 1.5 mg/L, were associated with lower IQ in children. It evaluated total exposure from different sources rather than the effects of a fluoridation program alone. It found insufficient data to determine whether the 0.7 mg/L level affected children's IQ.

The number 1.5 is easy to misread as a biological boundary. The review's description of where the association was supported does not demonstrate that an effect begins exactly there or that everything below it is harmless. Nor does uncertainty below that concentration establish harm at 0.7. These are different conclusions, and neither follows automatically from the review.

A [2019 Canadian prospective cohort study](https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jamapediatrics/fullarticle/2748634) brought measurements from a different exposure setting into the debate. Researchers measured maternal urinary fluoride during pregnancy and later tested children's IQ at ages three to four. Complete urinary-fluoride analyses involved 512 mother–child pairs; a separate estimate of intake from beverages involved 400.

Higher maternal urinary fluoride was associated with lower IQ in boys, with no statistically significant association in girls. The beverage-intake estimate showed an inverse association across boys and girls. These were different exposure measures with different results, not a single uniform effect across all children. A sex difference in one analysis is a finding to test in further cohorts, rather than a sufficient basis for saying boys alone are susceptible.

The investigators averaged urine samples across pregnancy to improve exposure assessment. They also acknowledged that spot samples could not precisely represent fetal exposure throughout pregnancy, that the beverage estimate relied on recall, and that maternal IQ had not been measured. Adjustment for recorded characteristics cannot guarantee that all relevant differences between families have been removed.

Following exposure before testing the children improves the sequence of observation. It does not randomize where families live or what they consume. The study contributes evidence of an association at exposures relevant to North American communities; it does not calculate an IQ change for every child drinking a specified tap-water concentration.

The [2025 meta-analysis by Kyla Taylor and colleagues](https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jamapediatrics/fullarticle/2828425) combined 74 studies, most of them cross-sectional. Its search ran through October 2023; its publication was a new synthesis, rather than a new cohort followed independently of the earlier literature. It found inverse associations overall. Its lower-exposure analyses depended on both the exposure measure and study selection. For drinking-water studies combined, the association was null below 1.5 mg/L; restricting that analysis to studies rated lower risk of bias produced an inverse association. Urinary-fluoride analyses also showed inverse associations at lower concentrations.

Much turns on which observations enter a lower-exposure analysis. The authors described limited data and uncertainty when exposure was estimated from drinking water alone below 1.5 mg/L. A urinary association cannot simply be substituted for a tap-water concentration, and a pooled observational estimate does not remove limitations shared by the studies supplying it. Pooling makes the available evidence easier to examine together. Its value still depends on whether exposures and outcomes were measured well and whether competing explanations were addressed.

## What the industrial origin establishes

One widely used additive, fluorosilicic acid, can be obtained as a byproduct of phosphate-fertilizer manufacture. The [Australian drinking-water guidelines](https://guidelines.nhmrc.gov.au/australian-drinking-water-guidelines/part-5/treatment-chemicals/hydrofluorosilicic-acid) describe that production route and identify possible contaminants, including arsenic and lead, whose concentrations depend on the raw materials. A utility should be able to say which product it buys, what impurities it tests for and what reaches the finished water.

The stock chemical and the treated water are different mixtures. Concentration, dissolution and impurities matter. [NSF's account of its fluoridation-product standard](https://www.nsf.org/knowledge-library/standards-regulations-related-fluoride-drinking-water) describes the release of fluoride ions in water and evaluation of the treatment chemicals and their impurities. The relevant records would include the product specification and its contribution to the finished water at the intended use level.

A certificate is not a completed answer to every question about lifetime exposure or neurodevelopment. Conversely, identifying a fertilizer byproduct does not establish the concentration of a contaminant in someone's glass. That requires information about the product and the finished water. The label “industrial waste” cannot do the work of an analysis.

Measurements inside the body raise another problem of interpretation. Jennifer Luke's [2001 study](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11275672/) examined pineal glands from 11 aged cadavers and found fluoride associated with calcium. It measured deposition, not changes in human melatonin, cognition or a capacity for spiritual experience. Its comparisons also used different tissue preparations, including wet tissue and bone ash. Turning those measurements into a claim about a disabled “third eye” adds a consequence the study did not test. The [[pineal-gland]] question requires evidence about function as well as composition.

## A risk ruling and its later reversal

In September 2024, federal district judge Edward Chen found that fluoridation at 0.7 mg/L presented an unreasonable risk under the Toxic Substances Control Act. His [decision](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USCOURTS-cand-3_17-cv-02162/pdf/USCOURTS-cand-3_17-cv-02162-15.pdf) required a regulatory response, while explicitly distinguishing a risk finding from certainty that fluoridated water injured public health. It left the form of a response to EPA.

On [21 May 2026](https://cdn.ca9.uscourts.gov/datastore/memoranda/2026/05/21/25-384.pdf), the Ninth Circuit vacated the decision and remanded. It held that the district court had improperly taken control of the evidentiary presentation, including delaying a ruling to await additional material the parties had agreed not to present at the first trial. It directed a decision based on the first trial record. The disposition did not decide the underlying scientific risk question.

The 2024 ruling therefore cannot be presented as an undisturbed order proving injury, and the appellate ruling cannot be presented as a scientific finding of safety. The courts were deciding legal questions with consequences for which evidence could be considered and what the agency had to do.

EPA’s [August 2026 announcement](https://www.epa.gov/sdwa/fluoride-drinking-water) described a protocol for developing a new human-health toxicity assessment. The page still listed the enforceable drinking-water maximum at 4.0 mg/L. That ceiling and the 2015 target for adding fluoride serve different purposes; the ceiling is not a recommendation to add that amount. A protocol sets out how an assessment will be done. It is not the completed assessment or a new measured threshold.

## Choosing for a shared supply

The consent objection survives these scientific distinctions. A municipal program changes something residents ingest through ordinary use of the supply. People can disagree about that decision even if they agree about the expected dental benefit. Avoiding the added exposure may require alternatives that cost money or effort; having a theoretical choice is not the same as having an affordable one.

The case for a common program also deserves its full statement. Its reach does not depend on a family buying a product, keeping an appointment or making a separate preventive decision each day. A community might judge that reach valuable, particularly where other prevention is hard to obtain. The judgment still needs evidence about the additional benefit and the distribution of exposure and harm.

A vote cannot replace that evidence, but evidence alone does not decide what burdens a community may impose. In [Utah's 2025 law](https://le.utah.gov/xcode/Title19/Chapter4/C19-4-S111_2025050720250507.html), the state prohibited adding fluoride to public water systems and prevented local governments from requiring or permitting it. The decision illustrates that the choice concerns who governs the supply as well as its chemistry. Moving a decision to the state does not give each resident an individual choice either.

A community deciding today needs a contemporary estimate of additional dental benefit, an assessment of total exposure and a credible account of the alternatives. Keeping a program commits residents to an exposure; ending it commits officials to the consequences of their replacement plan. Either decision should be open to review against what actually happens to the people served.
