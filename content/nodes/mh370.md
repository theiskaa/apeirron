Malaysia Airlines Flight 370 left Kuala Lumpur for Beijing shortly after midnight on March 8, 2014. There were 239 people aboard. At 1:19 a.m., Malaysian time, a controller instructed the crew to contact air traffic control in Vietnam. The crew’s acknowledgment was the last voice transmission: “Good night Malaysian Three Seven Zero.” The aircraft’s identifying signal disappeared from the controllers’ display soon afterward. It did not make contact with the next controller.

The search eventually moved thousands of kilometres from the place where the crew stopped speaking. A radar return showed the aircraft going back across Malaysia. Automatic exchanges with a satellite network showed that equipment aboard the aircraft continued to respond for hours. More than a year later, part of a wing washed ashore on Réunion, on the other side of the Indian Ocean. The signals shifted the search to the southern Indian Ocean, and the pieces that washed ashore supported that broad conclusion. None gave investigators an exact place to look or explained why the flight ended there.

## Losing a flight at the handover

Air traffic controllers had been following a signal that identified the aircraft. Once that signal was gone, an aircraft could still be detected by radar that uses reflected signals rather than waiting for an identifying reply. In the [2018 Malaysian safety report](https://www.mot.gov.my/en/MH370%20Investigation%20Report/01-Report/MH370SafetyInvestigationReport.pdf), civilian and military radar records put the aircraft westbound across the Malay Peninsula and later heading northwest. The last recorded military contact with that track was at approximately 2:22 a.m. The investigators had no direct position fixes beyond it.

The return across Malaysia was not immediately acted on as a sign that a civilian flight was in distress. The safety report says the military treated the radar target as friendly and not a threat to airspace security. Air traffic controllers had a different problem: they had lost contact during a transfer of responsibility. Under the agreement between the two centres, Ho Chi Minh should have alerted Kuala Lumpur within five minutes if it had not established contact at the expected transfer point. Ho Chi Minh first asked Kuala Lumpur about the flight at about 1:39 a.m. Later, the Kuala Lumpur rescue coordination centre was activated at 5:30 a.m.; its formal distress message was issued about an hour after that.

These are specific failures of coordination. The controller expecting a radio call, the military operator assessing a threat, and the people responsible for starting a search were not answering the same question. Earlier coordination might have produced a better account of where to look while the aircraft was still airborne. The record cannot tell us whether it would have led to a rescue. It does show why detecting an aircraft on a screen is not the same thing as recognizing an emergency and acting on it.

## A distance without a position

The satellite exchanges had not been designed to report the aircraft’s location. They were routine messages between an aircraft terminal and the communications network. The ground station recorded timing and frequency information for the signals. The timing told engineers something about the distance between the satellite and the aircraft. At a given instant, many points on Earth are the same distance from a satellite. Plot those points and then discard places the aircraft could not plausibly have reached, and the result is a long arc, not a pin on a map.

The frequency measurements helped distinguish paths that would otherwise fit the same timing data. Motion changes the frequency at which a signal is received, much as motion changes the pitch of a sound as it passes. Engineers had to account for the satellite’s motion and for the behaviour of the equipment before comparing expected measurements with the ones that had actually been recorded. Inmarsat’s engineers used transmissions from MH370 when its position was known to check the timing method. They also tested their frequency model against signals from other aircraft with known positions. Those comparisons showed how well the method worked when the answer was known. After the last radar contact the engineers no longer had that check for this flight.

A series of arcs constrains a route: an aircraft can reach only so much of the next arc in the time available. Among candidate paths, the measurements fitted a southern track much better than a northern one. They did not identify one exact heading or speed through every interval. Inmarsat’s paper presents a worked reconstruction and stresses that its particular route is not uniquely established. The final exchange, at about 8:19 a.m. Malaysian time, gave investigators the last distance constraint, widely called the seventh arc. It was not a recording of impact. The Malaysian safety team judged that the final exchange most likely followed a power interruption caused by fuel exhaustion. Its timing was consistent with how long the aircraft could have flown on its remaining fuel; no recovered recorder confirmed the sequence.

## The pieces the sea returned

In July 2015 a flaperon, a movable part of a wing, was found on Réunion. French authorities confirmed that it belonged to MH370. The Malaysian report identified two other wing pieces as confirmed, and classified some other finds with less certainty. A part that can be traced to the individual aircraft carries more weight than one that merely looks like something from the same type of jet. The distribution of finds in the western Indian Ocean was consistent with debris drifting from a loss in the southern Indian Ocean. But a piece reaching a beach more than a year later has travelled through changing currents and winds. A plausible drift history is not a line that can be followed backward to a single point.

The first large underwater search had to turn a long set of possible locations into a bounded area. About 710,000 square kilometres of seabed were mapped to establish its shape and depth. The more detailed sonar survey, meant to distinguish possible wreckage from the seabed, covered more than 120,000 square kilometres by January 2017. Mapping a region did not mean that every part of it had been searched at that resolution. In 2018 Ocean Infinity surveyed more than 112,000 square kilometres in an area extending farther north along the arc. Neither located the main wreckage. In March 2026 Ocean Infinity reported that it had left the area on January 23 after another search effort. It reported more than 140,000 square kilometres surveyed across its own efforts since 2018, not that much new coverage in the last operation. The company did not report a discovery.

A negative search changes the case for looking in the same place again. How much it changes that case depends on what could be detected in the surveyed area. It does not rule out every part of the seventh arc or every place the aircraft might have reached after the last exchange. The evidence for the broad region and the evidence for any exact position on the seabed are not equally strong.

## What the evidence can say about cause

The safety team used a Boeing 777 simulator to test whether the turn back across Malaysia could have occurred under autopilot in the way suggested by the available radar data. It concluded that the turn was more likely made under manual control. It did not reach the same conclusion for every later turn, and it could not establish who was at the controls or why the transponder signal ceased. Moving the controls would be consistent with an intentional diversion. It could also be a response to a problem. Identifying how a turn was made does not identify the purpose of it.

The captain’s home simulator was a separate piece of evidence, not the equipment used for those tests. Among thousands of coordinates recovered by police were seven manually entered points which, joined on a map, described a route toward the southern Indian Ocean. The files did not establish that all the points came from a single saved flight. There is a difference between recovering points that can be made into a route and finding a record of someone rehearsing the flight that disappeared. The safety report did not draw the latter conclusion. Nor did it find enough evidence to attribute the disappearance to a particular system failure or to rule out interference by another person.

Accounts in which the aircraft landed secretly somewhere else appeal in part to the belief that a modern surveillance system must have seen the whole flight. No such track has been produced. More importantly, a proposed landing would need to explain why the recorded signals fit a southern route and how confirmed pieces of the aircraft later reached shore. A gap in public access to a radar file would not answer either question. It is possible to ask for records without assuming that the record needed to settle the case was ever made.

The Malaysian team could identify changes that would make an aircraft in distress easier to locate without claiming to know what happened in the cockpit. It called for faster recognition of missing contact, better coordination, and more reliable position reporting. Those recommendations follow from the first hours, when different agencies held different clues. Finding the main wreckage would settle where the aircraft came down. Whether it could still tell investigators what happened aboard is a further question, and the one the families have had to live with since that last call.
