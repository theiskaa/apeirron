On February 28, 1966, one of the men on trial for murdering Malcolm X returned to the witness stand and changed his testimony. Talmadge Hayer, later known as Mujahid Abdul Halim, admitted that he had taken part in the shooting. He said his two co-defendants had not.

He knew the other participants, he told the court, but refused to identify them. Muhammad Abdul Aziz and Khalil Islam were strangers to him before their arrests. Both were innocent of this crime.

The jury convicted all three men. Halim had been apprehended at the scene, and physical evidence connected him to the attack. The cases against Aziz and Islam depended on eyewitness identifications, some of them conflicting. They went to prison while information that might have changed the verdict remained inside FBI and police files.

Their convictions were vacated in November 2021. By then, Islam had been dead for twelve years. Neither man could recover the decades spent under a murder conviction; Islam had not lived to see it lifted. It also raised a separate question: what had the original investigation failed to establish about the people who killed Malcolm X?

## The speaker they had come to hear

Malcolm was thirty-nine when he was killed on February 21, 1965, at the Audubon Ballroom in upper Manhattan. His political work had changed considerably during the preceding year. He was building a political organization after leaving the religious movement that had made him a public figure.

Born Malcolm Little in 1925, he encountered the Nation of Islam while imprisoned in Massachusetts. After his release in 1952 he became one of its most effective ministers and its best-known spokesman. The organization offered a demanding account of Black dignity, discipline, and independence in a society structured by white supremacy. Malcolm's defense of it was forceful, and his loyalty to its leader, Elijah Muhammad, had been deeply personal.

That relationship broke down. Conflicts over authority, political engagement, and Muhammad's private conduct became increasingly difficult to contain. Malcolm announced his departure from the Nation of Islam in March 1964. He established Muslim Mosque, Inc., and then the Organization of Afro-American Unity, or OAAU. His pilgrimage to Mecca and travels in Africa helped broaden both his religious outlook and his political relationships.

At the OAAU's [founding rally in June 1964](https://web.mst.edu/lib-circ/files/Special%20Collections/mxebook.pdf), he described an organization that could bring people together across religious and factional differences. He drew an example from African states cooperating despite disagreements among their leaders. The proposed program included education, organization, and self-defense, and appealed to human rights as well as American constitutional promises. His audience was larger than the group of Muslims who had followed him out of the Nation.

The program also grew out of his dissatisfaction with waiting for institutions that had repeatedly failed Black Americans. International alliances offered a way to challenge the treatment of American racism as a matter outsiders had no standing to question. This gave his final year a direction beyond the quarrel with Muhammad, even as that quarrel threatened his safety.

His relationship with the civil rights movement was changing too. He met Martin Luther King Jr. in Washington in March 1964 and visited Selma early in 1965, while King was in jail. There he told Coretta Scott King that he hoped the existence of a more militant alternative could make white people more willing to hear her husband. The encounter suggests possibilities for cooperation; it does not mean that the two leaders had resolved their substantial differences.

He was killed while those alliances were still being tested. The OAAU was less than a year old and lost its principal organizer before it had much time to establish itself.

## The attack and the first arrests

A disturbance interrupted the OAAU meeting as Malcolm began to speak. Someone shouted about a pickpocket, and a smoke device added to the confusion. A man with a shotgun fired at Malcolm near the stage. Halim and another gunman fired pistols.

A member of Malcolm's security team shot Halim in the leg as he fled. Members of the crowd apprehended him, and police arrested him outside the ballroom. He had a magazine containing ammunition for a .45-caliber pistol. Investigators also recovered a pistol that witnesses linked to him, and firearms examination connected it with bullets taken from Malcolm's body. A fingerprint belonging to Halim was found on material in the smoke device.

The case against Halim therefore had physical support that the cases against the other two men lacked. His admission was part of that evidence, alongside the gun, ammunition, and fingerprint. The later review preserved the distinction when it questioned the identification of his accomplices.

Aziz was arrested on February 26; Islam on March 3. Both were associated with the Nation of Islam's Harlem mosque. Neither was arrested at the ballroom, and no forensic evidence tied either man to the murder. The prosecution did not establish that they knew Halim or had worked with him.

At trial, however, several witnesses identified them. Some said they knew them from earlier encounters at mosques. Those claims of prior acquaintance gave the identifications an apparent strength. Yet the accounts differed substantially, and the procedures used to obtain the identifications created further problems.

## A case carried by identification

The [2021 joint motion to vacate the convictions](https://www.manhattanda.org/wp-content/uploads/2021/11/FINAL-Motion-to-Vacate-Convictions-11.16.2021.pdf) reconstructs the trial from its surviving record. Its details show how much depended on accounts that did not fit together.

Witnesses placed the diversion in different parts of the room. Cary Thomas had described Islam with a pistol before the grand jury, then identified him as the shotgun man at trial. Ronald Timberlake said the crowd had captured Aziz as well as Halim, although other accounts described only Halim's capture. The jury heard accusations presented as direct observations, but those observations conflicted on people, weapons, and movements.

The lineup procedures also raised questions. Jasper Davis had described a suspect wearing a gray coat. In a lineup, only two men wore gray coats; Davis said the other man did not fit his description. Such an arrangement can give a witness a route to a selection that depends partly on how police present the choices. The 2021 inquiry could not recover a complete set of lineup photographs or records showing what witnesses had been told.

Aziz and Islam presented alibis. Aziz said he was at home resting an injured leg after visiting Jacobi Hospital that morning. A doctor corroborated the visit. The doctor did not claim that running would have been impossible, though it would have been painful. That qualification matters because the accusation included flight from the scene; the medical evidence supported part of the alibi without mechanically proving the whole of it.

Islam also testified that he was home with his family. Relatives and acquaintances gave testimony about seeing or speaking with the men near the time of the murder. Their lawyers thus had more than denials to present. The prosecution's identifications had to be weighed against alternative accounts of where the defendants were.

Halim complicated that comparison. He initially denied participating and offered an innocent explanation for the ammunition found on him. When he returned to the stand, he admitted that the first account had been a lie. A jury had reason to question his reliability. But his change of testimony also included an admission against himself and an explanation of why the defendants beside him were the wrong men.

His description placed the pistol shooters near the front and a diversion farther back. He would not supply names. The defense could use his account to challenge the prosecution's reconstruction, but it could not bring the people he meant into court merely by demanding that the jury believe him.

Information in government files could have helped the defense pursue that problem. Much of it never reached the lawyers.

## What the defense did not receive

An FBI report from the day after the assassination described a suspected shotgun attacker whose complexion and build differed from Islam's. That description also resembled one given by a defense witness at trial. Had the lawyers received the report, they might have located its source and developed testimony supporting their challenge to the identification.

Other documents recorded failures to identify Islam, or named possible participants outside the group on trial. With those reports, lawyers could have interviewed their sources and compared the alternatives with the prosecution's account while witnesses were still alive.

The FBI also held a file on William 25X Bradley, a former lieutenant in the Newark mosque with military firearms experience. Information about his background and appearance potentially mattered to descriptions of the shotgun attacker. The file existed before the trial. Its usefulness did not depend on the defense already knowing the name it had not been given.

There was a further complication: some witnesses who identified Aziz or Islam had relationships with the FBI. One testified at trial; two others who supplied identifications did not. Their source relationships were concealed. Those relationships could have affected how the defense investigated the witnesses or questioned their independence and possible motives.

A February 25, 1965 FBI report recorded an instruction not to disclose informants' status to the NYPD. Other material indicated that sources were to describe what they had seen while concealing their connection to the bureau. This is evidence of deliberate protection of source identities. It is different from a documented instruction to invent testimony, but it still deprived the defense of information relevant to evaluating the identifications.

The material did not all point one way. Some informants accused the men who were convicted; other reports undermined those accusations. Keeping the relationships secret prevented the jury from seeing the circumstances under which important claims reached investigators.

The 2021 motion found that most of the newly discovered exculpatory material had also been withheld from the original prosecutors. It identified indications of deliberate withholding, while acknowledging exceptions and uncertainty about the history of some documents. Responsibility cannot be assigned accurately by assuming every office possessed the same complete set of facts. The defendants nevertheless bore the cost of the gaps between those offices.

For a lawyer, the difference was practical. A failed identification could lead to a witness who knew the accused and would say he had not seen him. A competing description could support the defense witness whose account the prosecution disputed. Knowledge of an informant relationship could change the questions asked on cross-examination. The withheld material offered ways to investigate and challenge the case, not merely extra pages to place in a file.

## The police officers in the room

Eugene “Gene” Roberts had infiltrated Malcolm's organization as an undercover NYPD officer. He witnessed the assassination and later described his actions during it. His presence gave the defense a potential source of evidence: a trained observer inside Malcolm's security arrangements might explain the positions and movements of people at the scene.

Roberts's identity became public during the Panther 21 proceedings in 1970, years after the murder trial. In later accounts, he placed the shooters near the front of the audience, in a way that supported parts of Halim's reconstruction and differed from prosecution testimony. His testimony also included a confrontation with Halim during the escape.

Other police records indicated undercover officers observing the ballroom through a peephole. A note in the prosecution file referred to three undercovers, only one of whom supposedly saw the shooting and identified Aziz. That note did not supply the person's name, the account of what he saw, or the identification procedure. The surviving records could not settle exactly which officers had been present or what all of them observed.

The officers' observations and their basis could not be tested openly. An unnamed assertion transmitted through another officer was allowed to sit beside an investigation in which the accused were fighting eyewitness claims without the full record.

The absence of visible protection also demands care. The 2021 filing noted that the NYPD had offered Malcolm protection, including shortly before his death, and that he declined. That does not explain every security decision. It does mean that a categorical account of police deliberately withdrawing requested protection cannot be treated as a finding of that inquiry.

## The warning that preceded the murder

One week before the assassination, Malcolm's Queens home was firebombed. He, Betty Shabazz, and their four children escaped. Whatever the remaining questions about the assailants, the danger surrounding the family was real.

An [FBI report dated February 16](https://www.columbia.edu/cu/ccbh/mxp/pdf/firebombedhouse.pdf) assembled information from police, fire investigators, and sources about the attack. It also reported a meeting of Malcolm's organizations afterward, including the decision against reprisals. Alongside the physical investigation, the bureau was receiving accounts of what his followers discussed and decided.

Earlier records show the same reach into private organizational life. A [January 23, 1964 memorandum](https://www.columbia.edu/cu/ccbh/mxp/pdf/012364pho.pdf) conveyed detailed accounts of conversations involving Elijah Muhammad, including disagreements concerning Malcolm and the consequences of his public statements. The bureau could follow arguments about authority and family matters from inside the organization, well beyond what Malcolm and Muhammad said to the press.

The history of political surveillance and disruption examined in [[cointelpro]] supplies substantial reason to investigate how agencies treated Malcolm. Surveillance was not automatically a protective service, and an agency could value access to an organization more highly than the interests of people being watched. The specific evidence of concealment after the assassination makes that concern more than a general suspicion about government.

Several consequential reports examined in 2021 were written after the shooting. They concerned suspected attackers and witness descriptions, rather than an advance warning setting out the plan. The 2021 motion did not find evidence establishing that the FBI or NYPD orchestrated the murder. Its documented findings about withheld evidence should not be expanded into a conclusion its investigators expressly did not reach.

An investigation of the source reports about Malcolm's safety would have to establish what an official learned, when it reached that official, and what action followed. A general assertion that an agency knew everything can conceal the decision or failure that a particular record might establish.

## Why the correction took so long

Halim eventually named alleged accomplices in affidavits dated 1977 and 1978: Leon Davis, Benjamin Thomas, William “X,” and a man described as “Wilbur or Kinly.” He identified New Jersey associates and assigned them roles in the attack. Those later identifications were not names he had given the jury in 1966.

Aziz and Islam used the affidavits, information about Roberts, and FBI documents in efforts to overturn their convictions. The court denied relief in 1978; a later federal petition also failed. Their appeals had put the competing account before courts decades before the renewed public attention.

The 2021 filing compared the records with what officials had told the court in 1978. In 1978, prosecutors represented that their file held nothing supporting the defense's contentions. The FBI described additional records as largely summaries or parallel accounts offering no useful new information. The later review found those representations inaccurate. It also found police material in the district attorney's file that supported parts of the defense's account.

The court's skepticism also relied on the reputation of the original investigation. A court assessing a late accusation could be skeptical that competent investigators would have missed people genuinely connected to the crime. Yet that skepticism depended partly on an incomplete account of what investigators actually possessed. Information withheld in the first proceeding helped make a later challenge look less credible.

Aziz was released on parole in 1985; Islam in 1987. Parole did not clear their names. Islam died in 2009. Relatives, lawyers, researchers, and journalists continued trying to recover an account that the convictions had made appear settled.

In 2020, Manhattan's Conviction Integrity Program began a joint reinvestigation with the Shanies Law Office and the Innocence Project. The renewed attention surrounding *Who Killed Malcolm X?* belonged to a longer history of challenges. The lawyers now worked with the prosecutor's office to examine thousands of pages and obtain previously unavailable material.

They also interviewed a witness identified as J.M. He recalled taking a call from Aziz shortly after the shooting and then calling him back at his home number. If accurate, that return call supported Aziz's alibi more directly than merely hearing someone say where he was. It remained a recollection gathered more than fifty years later, without telephone records that could now verify the call. The investigators considered it alongside the documentary evidence, rather than making it carry the whole case.

## Exoneration and the unfinished inquiry

The joint filing sought to vacate the convictions and dismiss the indictment. It found that the newly available evidence could probably have produced a more favorable verdict and that the men had been deprived of a fair trial. The motion also considered the problem of information held by police or federal investigators rather than the prosecutor personally. Later decisions had expanded prosecutors' responsibility for material held by investigative partners. The filing argued that the FBI and NYPD had cooperated extensively enough here to count as a joint investigation for that analysis, while acknowledging a legal question about applying later rules retroactively. It ultimately relied on newly discovered evidence, fundamental fairness, and the interests of justice in asking for relief.

On November 18, 2021, Justice Ellen N. Biben granted the requested relief.

The prosecutor's motion was precise about a limit: it made no determination of actual innocence. The eyewitnesses who had identified the defendants were dead, key identification records were missing, much of the physical evidence could not be located, and Halim declined an interview. The convictions could not stand, but the review could not conduct every inquiry that should have been possible in 1965.

That limitation does not reduce the exonerations to a technical favor. The evidence at issue concerned the identity of the attackers. The failure to disclose it took away opportunities to locate witnesses, test identifications, and investigate other suspects while people and material were still available. The passage of time then made those missed opportunities permanent.

The broader allegation of government participation has also entered civil litigation brought by Malcolm's estate and daughters. An [August 19, 2026 federal decision](https://law.justia.com/cases/federal/district-courts/new-york/nysdce/1:2024cv08680/631724/140/) granted the federal defendants' motion to dismiss. Its reasons included jurisdiction, time limits, and insufficiently pleaded claims; it did not decide the remaining claims against the city in that order. The judge expressly distinguished time-barred claims from a judgment on their historical merits. Allegations recited in the decision were being assumed for the procedural analysis, not adopted as findings after a trial.

A historical account should preserve that difference too. A complaint can identify a path for investigation. A procedural dismissal can close a legal route without resolving the past. Neither document, by its existence alone, supplies the missing account of who directed the killers.

What is established is severe enough. A political leader was murdered in front of his supporters and family. Two men were convicted through proceedings deprived of material information held by public agencies. Decades of challenges passed before those convictions were undone. The agencies' responsibility for that failure deserves examination in its own right, with the same insistence on evidence that the original prosecution failed to meet.
