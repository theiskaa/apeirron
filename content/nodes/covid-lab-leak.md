The early COVID-19 cases were in Wuhan, a city with both a market selling live wildlife and a laboratory studying bat coronaviruses. Each fact suggested somewhere to look. Neither, by itself, identified how the first person became infected.

Much of the argument since then has collapsed several questions into one. SARS-CoV-2, the virus that causes COVID-19, could have evolved in animals and reached a person during field sampling. A laboratory can study a naturally occurring virus without engineering it. An accident can occur in civilian research. A deliberately released biological weapon would require a further, very different account of intention and preparation. Calling all of these possibilities “the lab leak” makes the dispute easier to conduct and harder to resolve.

The route into people is the question that needs an answer. An infected animal reaching a trader and an infected researcher reaching the community would leave different histories. The strongest published scientific work builds a case around the market and the wildlife trade. The laboratory inquiry has serious unanswered questions, but the documents considered here do not identify a verified chain from a laboratory to the first human infections.

## The cases around the market

The Huanan Seafood Wholesale Market became associated with the outbreak early. That creates an obvious problem for later research: if doctors looked especially hard for patients connected to a market, the resulting case list might reflect their search as well as the disease's spread.

Michael Worobey and colleagues addressed this in a 2022 *Science* paper. They reconstructed the residential locations of 155 December 2019 cases from maps in the World Health Organization (WHO)–China investigation report. Cases clustered around Huanan. More importantly, the pattern persisted among the 120 patients without a known market link. Their homes were concentrated near the market too. The researchers compared the pattern with Wuhan's population distribution and tested how much removing nearby cases changed the result. [The study](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9348750/) argues that the clustering cannot readily be explained by preferentially finding market customers.

A patient without a recorded visit to Huanan could have caught the virus from a neighbor who worked there. “No known market link” therefore does not mean “unrelated to transmission around the market.” It is one reason the unlinked cases matter: community spread can leave a geographical pattern after the individual contacts have become impossible to reconstruct.

An early concentration can also arise after the first transmission. A person infected elsewhere could introduce a virus into a crowded market, where it spread efficiently. To assess that explanation, the map has to be considered alongside what was sold there and what investigators found inside.

The reconstructed locations also come from the cases that were recognized. People whose infections went unnoticed cannot appear on that map. Its value is in the pattern within the available records; a complete history of the earliest infections would require information those records do not contain.

## What a swab can contain

Chinese investigators began environmental sampling when the market closed on 1 January 2020. Their [published surveillance study](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-023-06043-2) reports 923 environmental samples, with 74 positive by the study's testing criteria. A separate collection of 457 animal samples, taken from 18 January onward, produced no positives. Those animal samples included remaining refrigerated or frozen goods and animals still accessible at the site. They were not a complete set of the live mammals sold before closure.

That distinction changes the meaning of a negative result. Testing what remains in a market weeks later can answer whether those particular specimens contain detectable virus. It cannot establish that every animal previously brought through the market was uninfected. The animals available for those later tests cannot stand in for all the animals previously traded there.

The environmental samples preserve a different kind of information. A swab from a stall may collect human material, animal material and virus left at different times. Sequencing can identify traces from several organisms in that mixture. It cannot automatically assign a virus to the organism that shed it.

In a 2024 *Cell* paper, Alexander Crits-Christoph and colleagues reanalyzed market sequencing data. They found genetic material from susceptible wildlife, including raccoon dogs, in virus-positive samples from a wildlife stall. The analysis places animals capable of infection where viral contamination was found. Its authors explicitly acknowledge that mixed environmental material cannot conclusively identify the species that shed SARS-CoV-2. They also discuss [uneven sampling dates, degradation and the difficulty of comparing relative sequence abundance](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11427129/).

Finding animal DNA and viral RNA together is more informative than learning only that a market sold animals. It remains less informative than obtaining a positive specimen directly from an animal with a documented place in the supply chain. The difference is practical: an infected animal could lead investigators toward a farm, a shipment and earlier infections. A mixed swab leaves those links to be established.

Taken together, the case geography and environmental findings give animal-associated emergence an affirmative evidential basis. The unresolved issue is whether the market was where the virus first crossed into people, where an infected trader introduced it, or where an earlier introduction became conspicuous. Those possibilities do not all imply the same first infection.

## What the laboratory evidence establishes

The Wuhan Institute of Virology's coronavirus research involved collecting bat samples, identifying viral sequences and studying how related viruses interact with host cells. That work gave researchers opportunities for contact with material that deserved careful handling. Its possible role in the outbreak is a question about those contacts, the viruses involved and the safeguards in place.

The [June 2023 US intelligence report](https://archive.dni.gov/files/ODNI/documents/assessments/Report-on-Potential-Links-Between-the-Wuhan-Institute-of-Virology-and-the-Origins-of-COVID-19-20230623.pdf) described research and biosafety concerns at the institute. It also said the intelligence community had no indication that the institute possessed SARS-CoV-2 or a close progenitor before the pandemic, and no direct reporting of a particular research incident causing it. Reports of illness among researchers did not establish COVID-19: the symptoms were not diagnostic, and the report described alternative explanations and gaps in what could be confirmed.

The report describes the information available to the intelligence community at that time. It cannot guarantee the contents of every unpublished collection. It does, however, identify what was missing from an account that connected the institute's known research to this outbreak.

A recorded sequence is also different from a tube containing live virus. A research group may sequence part or all of a virus from a collected specimen without successfully isolating an infectious virus. Treating every sequence in a database as a ready laboratory stock gives an inventory more contents than the record establishes.

The 2023 report considered known relatives such as RaTG13 and BANAL-52 too genetically distant to be immediate progenitors. Calling one of them a close relative describes a comparison with other known viruses. It does not make it the pandemic virus one laboratory step earlier. The intermediate history still has to be found.

The missing link could, in principle, take several forms: a documented close precursor held before the outbreak; a contemporaneous infection in a person who handled it; or an accident record supported by laboratory and medical evidence. None requires a researcher to have designed the virus. An investigation limited to looking for engineering would miss a possible accident involving a collected natural virus.

## A proposal and an audit

One especially consequential document is the 2018 Project DEFUSE funding proposal submitted by EcoHealth Alliance and collaborators to the US Defense Advanced Research Projects Agency (DARPA). Its stated purpose was to reduce the threat from bat-borne coronaviruses. The proposed work included experiments altering features of spike proteins, which help a virus attach to and enter cells. Among those features were cleavage sites, where an enzyme cuts the protein. Those plans make the [proposal](https://usrtk.org/wp-content/uploads/2023/01/defuse-proposal.pdf) relevant to questions about research risks and oversight.

DARPA did not fund the proposal. At a [May 2024 congressional hearing](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/CHRG-118hhrg55548/html/CHRG-118hhrg55548.htm), lawmakers questioned Peter Daszak about both the application and comments in a draft concerning work in Wuhan. Daszak denied knowing that the proposed cleavage-site experiments had been performed at Wuhan or the University of North Carolina. His denial is testimony to examine alongside other records, not independent verification of everything either institution did.

The proposal is evidence of a research intention. Turning it into evidence of the pandemic's origin requires showing what was actually carried out, using which material, and how it reached a person. A rejected application cannot supply those later events merely because its subject resembles a feature of the pandemic virus. Equally, its rejection does not establish that every related activity was impossible under other funding. Actual research records would answer a question that the application leaves open.

Some failures of oversight are better documented. A [2023 US Department of Health and Human Services inspector general audit](https://oig.hhs.gov/reports/all/2023/the-national-institutes-of-health-and-ecohealth-alliance-did-not-effectively-monitor-awards-and-subawards-resulting-in-missed-opportunities-to-oversee-research-and-other-deficiencies/) found that the National Institutes of Health (NIH) and EcoHealth had not effectively monitored grants and subawards. NIH failed to follow up a late progress report until nearly two years after it was due. The audit also found inadequate guidance and difficulties obtaining scientific documentation from Wuhan after the outbreak. It criticized NIH's improper termination of a grant as well as deficiencies involving the recipient.

The auditors examined compliance and administration. They expressly did not assess the scientific results or the accuracy of NIH's and EcoHealth's scientific interpretations. An origin finding was outside the audit's scope.

An agency should be able to determine whether agreed reporting conditions have been met while the work is underway. If it cannot obtain the records necessary to do that, assurances about compliance become difficult to check. That failure matters even if the pandemic ultimately proves to have begun in the wildlife trade.

## Reading a genome, judging a statement

The furin cleavage site became a recurring focus of the dispute. It is a feature of the spike protein at which a host enzyme can cut, helping activate the protein during infection. Its presence raises a biological question about how it arose. It does not, on its own, name a laboratory or establish a route into humans.

In March 2020, Kristian Andersen and colleagues published [“The proximal origin of SARS-CoV-2”](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41591-020-0820-9). They argued that features of the genome were consistent with natural processes and against purposeful construction. Their concluding rejection extended to laboratory-based scenarios generally. That broader language deserves scrutiny: even persuasive evidence against deliberate engineering would not exclude a laboratory incident involving a naturally occurring virus. The paper was a genomic argument, not an inspection of Wuhan's inventories or staff health records.

DEFUSE creates the converse problem. It shows that researchers were interested in altering features of coronaviruses. Linking that interest to SARS-CoV-2 requires a history that explains the rest of the virus and fits the chronology. A resemblance between a proposed experiment and one feature of a later virus leaves much of that history unwritten.

There were failures in disclosure too. A February 2020 *Lancet* letter supporting Chinese health professionals declared no competing interests. In a [June 2021 addendum](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8215723/), the journal published Daszak's expanded disclosure, including EcoHealth's US-funded work in China, collaborations and recombinant coronavirus research. Those relationships were relevant context for readers assessing his participation in an origins discussion.

Incomplete disclosure warrants correction and scrutiny of independence. It does not tell us which person first carried the virus. A conflict can give someone a reason to favor an explanation; establishing that reason is different from establishing that the explanation is false. The underlying claim must still be tested.

## The date on a released document

Official accounts have diverged. The 2023 intelligence report described agencies reaching different conclusions, with differing confidence. WHO's Scientific Advisory Group for the Origins of Novel Pathogens, or SAGO, [reported in June 2025](https://cdn.who.int/media/docs/default-source/documents/epp/sago/independent-assessment-of-the-origins-of-sars-cov-2-by-sago.pdf?download=true) that the weight of available scientific evidence favored animal-to-human spillover. It could not establish precisely when, where or how this happened. It also lacked access to the laboratory information needed for a full assessment of a research-associated incident.

On 18 June 2026, the Office of the Director of National Intelligence released documents with a much stronger public claim: that US-funded Wuhan research sparked the pandemic and that Anthony Fauci helped suppress the laboratory-origin account. [The release](https://archive.dni.gov/index.php/newsroom/press-releases/press-releases-2026/4166-pr-11-26) also alleged manipulation of intelligence and misleading congressional testimony. The announcement goes considerably further than the earlier public assessment. The supporting records therefore deserve close attention.

The [first released document bundle](https://www.dni.gov/files/documents/Newsroom/Reports%20and%20Pubs/COVID-19_Release_DNI_Gabbard_6-18_Part-1.pdf) illustrates why that examination requires attention to dates. An included Lawrence Livermore analysis was written on 27 May 2020 and declassified in June 2026. It judged conditions for a laboratory accident to be present, while giving equal weight to laboratory modification, natural emergence and release of a naturally occurring virus from a laboratory. It did not present that judgment as identification of the actual first infection.

Elsewhere in the bundle, a December 2024 email headed “Final Congressional COVID Report, Confirms Lab Leak” summarizes the congressional report. The email identifies its writer as an open-source specialist. Its presence in an intelligence archive does not turn the forwarded public conclusion into a separate intelligence discovery.

Reading the first document as a new 2026 finding would erase its original uncertainty. Reading the second as independent corroboration would count the same public report twice. Neither reading follows from the date stamped on the release.

Outside readers also cannot inspect intelligence that remains classified. An agency's reason for protecting a source may be legitimate, while leaving its conclusion dependent on trust in its handling of that source. The public cannot resolve that difficulty simply by choosing whichever institution it already believes.

## What would move the inquiry forward

Early case geography and evidence from wildlife stalls give animal-associated emergence a substantial scientific case. The failure to identify the immediate infected animal leaves important parts of that history unresolved. Laboratory research, proposed experiments and oversight failures establish reasons for investigation, while leaving the particular chain from research material to human infection unverified in the records examined here.

WHO's 2025 report called for access to laboratory inventories, work and biosafety records, and staff health information, as well as further investigation of the wildlife supply chain. These are specific avenues of inquiry. A sample tied to a dated animal shipment would change the animal-origin case differently from a documented pre-outbreak infection tied to laboratory material. Both would add something that arguments about reputations cannot provide.

An origin inquiry may remain incomplete while responsibility for a particular failure is clear. NIH had reporting requirements and failed to follow them up in time. Enforcing the terms attached to research funding is a task that can be defined and checked while the work is still underway. That responsibility does not depend on reconstructing the first infection.
