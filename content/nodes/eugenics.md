The Supreme Court introduces Carrie Buck as a “feeble minded” woman. Her mother and infant daughter receive the same label in the next sentence. Justice Oliver Wendell Holmes begins his argument with three generations of inherited incapacity already presented as facts.

The sequence in [the 1927 opinion, *Buck v. Bell*](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/274/200/), matters. The Court was considering whether Virginia could authorize an operation to make Buck sterile. Yet the classification that supposedly made the operation necessary entered the opinion with scarcely any uncertainty. A young woman's ability to have another child depended on whether that classification could be challenged. The judgment treated the challenge as settled.

Eugenics promised to improve future generations by influencing who reproduced. Its advocates disagreed about methods, while sharing a confidence that inherited human qualities could be ranked and that society should act on the ranking. Encouraging the supposedly desirable to have children and preventing the supposedly undesirable from doing so belonged to the same project. Compulsory sterilization gave that project an immediate, irreversible power over particular people.

To understand how that power worked, we need to follow who supplied the description, what counted as evidence, and whether the person described had anyone capable of disputing it.

## What a family record could prove

Francis Galton introduced the word *eugenics* in his 1883 book *Inquiries into Human Faculty and Its Development*. His [explanation of the term](https://galton.org/books/human-faculty/SecondEdition/text/html-single/src/human-faculty4.htm) extended beyond arranging marriages. He wanted to consider influences that would help the human groups he judged more suitable prevail over those he judged less suitable.

That judgment came before any particular breeding policy. Someone had to decide what improvement meant and which people embodied it. Galton's language moved readily between individual abilities, families and races. A hierarchy of human worth could therefore appear as a scientific starting point rather than a political decision requiring a defense.

In the United States, the [Eugenics Record Office at Cold Spring Harbor](https://archivesspace.cshl.edu/repositories/2/resources/67), established in 1910, made the collection of family information an organized undertaking. Its surviving holdings include fieldworkers' reports, pedigrees, correspondence and administrative records. Charles Davenport and Harry Laughlin were central figures. Early support came from the Harriman family; the office later became part of the Carnegie Institution's work.

A pedigree could make an argument visible. Put a trait beside relatives across several generations and the repetition begins to look like an explanation. But a family also shares housing, income, access to schooling, exposure to institutions and the judgments of the people recording its history. To establish inheritance, a researcher needed a way to distinguish those influences. A pedigree showing repeated poverty or dependency could leave that work entirely undone.

Some of the categories were judgments about conduct from the outset. In the sterilization campaign, medical conditions appeared alongside judgments about criminality, dependency and sexual conduct. A person could be classified by an official's account of how a family lived, then have that classification offered as the biological reason the family lived that way. The record could absorb an accusation without providing a way to test it. The office’s own institutional history records opposition from geneticists including T. H. Morgan and H. J. Muller. Its authority and funding did not mean its conclusions represented an uncontested scientific consensus.

## The safeguards in Virginia

Virginia's 1924 statute described sterilization as beneficial both to patients and to society. According to the law's account, people who otherwise needed confinement might be released once they could no longer reproduce. Institutions could save money, and their former residents could become self-supporting. The proposal presented the loss of reproductive capacity as a condition of greater freedom.

The promise of release made the proposal particularly coercive. If officials said a resident could safely leave only after sterilization, accepting the operation could appear to be the route out of an institution. That bargain placed control over confinement and control over reproduction in the same hands. Describing it as a benefit to the patient could conceal how few alternatives the institution was prepared to offer.

The law also contained an elaborate procedure. A superintendent had to petition an institutional board. The person concerned and a guardian received notice; a guardian could be appointed if necessary. Evidence was recorded, and an order could be appealed to the circuit court and then to Virginia's highest court. Holmes set out those steps carefully. Their presence helped persuade him that Buck had received due process.

Everything depended on what could actually be contested. The institution supplied the hereditary diagnosis and proposed the operation. A hearing could protect its subject only if the diagnosis, evidence and claimed benefit were genuinely open to dispute. An appeal on paper does little when the people conducting the case agree about the result they want.

Historian [Paul Lombardo's account of the case](https://www.eugenicsarchive.org/html/eugenics/essay8text.html) shows how poor the evidence was. Laughlin submitted a deposition without having met Carrie. Other witnesses also testified; the case did not rest on that deposition alone. Judgments about her mother and brief observations of her baby, Vivian, helped establish the supposed hereditary pattern. Lombardo's research found that Carrie's pregnancy followed rape, rather than the promiscuity attributed to her, and that her lawyer collaborated with supporters of the law. Vivian's later school record included the honor roll.

Lombardo's findings expose how the case was constructed. Her right to control her reproduction should never have depended on a historian eventually finding a favorable report card. A disability would not have made her life available for the state's breeding program. Correcting the classification reveals one wrong; rejecting the authority claimed over her addresses another.

Holmes's reasoning went well beyond accepting the diagnosis. He compared compulsory sterilization with compulsory vaccination and invoked the sacrifices society could demand from citizens. But the asserted danger was the existence of a future child. The Court allowed officials to treat that possibility as an injury preventable by surgery on the parent.

His declaration—“Three generations of imbeciles are enough”—compressed accusation, prediction and sentence into a single declaration. It allowed no space for what a child might become, or for the possibility that the state had misdescribed the family it claimed to understand.

## A criminal classification meets a different Court

Fifteen years later, [*Skinner v. Oklahoma*](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/316/535/) exposed another route from a social category to a supposedly hereditary defect. Oklahoma's law authorized sterilization of certain habitual offenders. Jack Skinner had been convicted of stealing chickens and twice of robbery with firearms. In the proceeding against him, the jury was told that the question left for it was whether a vasectomy could be performed without harming his general health.

The health test defined injury narrowly. A person might recover from surgery without an immediate physical complication and still lose a future he might want: the possibility of having a child. The hearing could approve the operation while leaving that loss outside the issue it was asked to decide.

The proceeding offered no inquiry into whether his conduct was inherited, whether an operation could prevent similar conduct in a future generation, or whether depriving him of the ability to have children was justified. His convictions had already supplied the category; the hearing concerned the physical feasibility of carrying out its consequence.

The statute exempted embezzlement. A person who took money entrusted to him could escape a measure that applied to someone who stole a similar amount without that prior relationship. Ordinary criminal law might distinguish the offenses for its own reasons. Oklahoma had supplied no basis for treating the distinction as a difference in what either person would pass to a child.

Justice William O. Douglas's 1942 opinion held that the discrimination violated equal protection. The distinction between the crimes went to the heart of the state's claim. The state had taken a legal distinction between kinds of theft and given it the consequence of a rule about human inheritance. Douglas also emphasized the fundamental character of marriage and procreation and the irreversible injury sterilization imposed.

The decision nevertheless left much unresolved. It distinguished *Buck* rather than overruling it, and the justices did not agree on every reason for rejecting Oklahoma's law. Chief Justice Harlan Fiske Stone's concurrence stressed the absence of a meaningful individual hearing about inheritance. He still accepted that a state might intervene after an appropriate inquiry. The opinions show a boundary being contested, rather than a Court unanimously abandoning the eugenic premise.

Evidence could defeat Oklahoma's claim that criminality followed the lines drawn by its statute. Protection of bodily autonomy asks a further question about the power to operate even when some inherited characteristic is real. Neither question can safely be delegated to the people who designed the classification.

## Exchange across the Atlantic

American and German eugenicists corresponded, exchanged research and discussed sterilization laws. Historian [Egbert Klautke's study of those relations](https://discovery.ucl.ac.uk/id/eprint/1471934/3/Eugenics%20USA%20Germany%2010-2015%20HARVARD%20REVISED%20FINAL.pdf) traces both the influence of American examples and German initiatives. German proposals predated the Nazi seizure of power. Under the Nazis, compulsory measures became more radical, and American enthusiasts used Germany's program to promote their own. Influence moved in both directions.

Financial relationships also belonged to this history. The Rockefeller Foundation supported German institutions conducting research in heredity and eugenics. The [[rockefeller]] essay examines that funding more closely. A grant can establish support for an institution and its work; responsibility for a particular policy or crime requires tracing what the recipient did and what the funder knew and decided. The grant records give that inquiry a place to begin.

The Nazi [Law for the Prevention of Offspring with Hereditary Diseases](https://encyclopedia.ushmm.org/content/en/article/forced-sterilization-a-form-of-nazi-persecution), enacted in July 1933, named nine conditions assumed to be hereditary. Special courts, composed of a judge and two physicians, decided cases. Police could enforce an order when a person refused surgery. The US Holocaust Memorial Museum estimates that some 400,000 Germans were forcibly sterilized under the law.

The machinery combined medical selection with legal authority and police force. A court's involvement did not make the person's agreement necessary. The procedures themselves authorized the abuse.

## What a consent form leaves unanswered

Coercion can also occur where the formal rule requires consent. That is why later sterilizations need their own records, rather than being folded into an unchanged program extending from the 1920s to the present.

A [2014 California state audit](https://information.auditor.ca.gov/pdfs/reports/2013-120.pdf) examined female prisoner sterilizations during fiscal years 2005–06 through 2012–13. It identified 144 women who had undergone bilateral tubal ligation, a procedure generally performed to prevent future pregnancy. The last of those operations occurred in 2011.

The auditors found deficiencies in the informed-consent process for 39 women. In 27 cases, they found no evidence of the required physician's signature. In 18, they identified potential violations of the waiting period. Six cases fell into both groups. These figures describe different failures within the same group of 39, rather than two groups that can simply be added together.

The missing signature mattered because the physician was supposed to certify that the patient understood the lasting effects and appeared competent to consent. The waiting period was intended to give her time to reconsider. Each safeguard concerned a different stage of making and maintaining a decision. Treating either as paperwork would miss the protection it was supposed to provide.

Outside hospitals performed the operations; prison staff did not carry out the surgery. Prison health staff nevertheless had responsibilities where they obtained consent; the report identified at least nineteen such cases within the group of 39. Dividing care between organizations created a dispute about responsibility precisely where a patient needed someone to take it.

The auditors also found that medical notes were too poor to establish the quality and content of counseling. They could not determine whether staff had answered questions or supplied the information needed for the decision. The report therefore supports a finding of serious consent and oversight failures. It does not establish that every one of the 144 women was forced to undergo surgery.

To establish what happened to a particular woman, an investigation would need to ask who explained the procedure, what the woman understood, whether she could refuse without penalty, and whether she had time to change her mind. A signed form is one piece of evidence in that inquiry. It cannot answer all of those questions on its own.

## Who gets to decide

Calling every attempt to influence human biology eugenics can make the decision itself disappear. A person seeking information about an inherited condition, a family making a reproductive decision and a state preventing a classified group from reproducing involve different people exercising different powers. An account of each decision needs to identify whose purpose it serves and whose agreement it requires.

The ambitions discussed in [[transhumanism]] raise further questions about which abilities are valued and who can afford an intervention. Those questions deserve attention without assuming that every voluntary use of genetic knowledge repeats a compulsory sterilization law. The questions include whether a person can refuse, and what pressure the institution or market puts on that refusal.

The same care applies to population arguments. Counting resource use or proposing a population target does not by itself establish a policy of hereditary selection. The question becomes concrete when a proposal specifies who must change their lives, who decides and what happens to someone who refuses.

Buck's case shows what was lost when those questions were answered entirely by officials. The institution defined her, experts supplied the hereditary account, and the Court accepted the account as grounds for an operation. Later research could correct the file. It could not return the choice that the judgment had taken from her.
