In his [farewell address on 17 January 1961](https://www.archives.gov/milestone-documents/president-dwight-d-eisenhowers-farewell-address), Dwight Eisenhower defended military readiness as a means of keeping the peace. He also warned that the permanent establishment supplying that readiness could endanger democratic government. The same capacity he considered necessary had created a political problem he expected to persist.

A country could need a weapon without needing every weapon its institutions proposed. It could value military expertise while allowing the people who supplied it too much authority over public choices. Eisenhower’s phrase “whether sought or unsought” left room for influence that grew without a secret agreement to seize power.

The military-industrial complex names the relationships among the armed services, suppliers, political decision-makers and the people whose livelihoods depend on defense work. Its influence can enter at several points: defining what the country needs, choosing who supplies it, deciding what earns a payment and making it difficult to change course. Those decisions can be examined more closely than a claim that all the participants want war.

## From a military requirement to a continuing obligation

A weapons program brings people with different responsibilities to the same decision. A service wants equipment it can use. An elected government makes strategic commitments. Legislators decide what to fund and answer to constituents. A contractor wants profitable work, while its engineers and employees may also believe strongly in the mission. Oversight bodies examine costs, performance and compliance.

Their interests can converge around a program without becoming identical. A contractor may want an additional production run; a commander may prefer improvements to equipment already in service; a legislator may want to preserve the factory while questioning the proposed mission. Their shared support can conceal disagreement over what should happen next.

That is why an announced price is only the start of the obligation. A fleet also needs trained operators, maintenance, parts, software, facilities and eventual replacement. Buying the equipment creates later needs that other organizations must meet. If only a narrow group can supply them, the government’s bargaining position may change after the initial competition.

The requirement itself deserves attention before anyone bids. Imagine that two ways of meeting a mission are available: modifying existing equipment or buying a new platform. A specification written around the new platform’s features can make the modification appear unsuitable before its costs and limits have been compared. Later competition among suppliers would not reopen that earlier choice. The specification has already narrowed the decision, before the prices arrive.

The question is not simply whether the country should spend more or less. A larger budget can buy useful capacity, absorb an avoidable overrun or sustain equipment that cannot meet its intended mission. A smaller budget can cut waste or leave a genuine requirement unmet. Spending needs to be connected to what is delivered, at what cost and under whose control.

## What a contract rewards

“Cost-plus” is sometimes used as though the contractor receives a fixed percentage of whatever it spends, making every overrun an automatic increase in profit. That description gets an important contract type wrong.

Under [FAR 16.306](https://www.acquisition.gov/far/16.306), a cost-plus-fixed-fee contract pays a negotiated fee fixed at the beginning. The fee does not vary with actual cost, although changes to the work may lead to an adjustment. The regulation recognizes a weak incentive to control costs. It does not say the fee automatically grows with each extra dollar spent.

A [firm-fixed-price contract](https://www.acquisition.gov/far/16.202-1), by contrast, generally leaves the contractor responsible for its costs and resulting profit or loss. [FAR 16.102](https://www.acquisition.gov/far/16.102) prohibits the cost-plus-a-percentage-of-cost system. The terms determine which costs the seller has agreed to bear.

Consider a deliberately simplified comparison. Expected allowable costs are $100 million. One contract provides reimbursement plus a $5 million fixed fee; the other sets a $105 million firm price. Assume unchanged work, no additional incentives or price adjustments, and sufficient authorization for the reimbursed costs.

| Outcome | Cost reimbursement plus fixed fee | Firm fixed price |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Costs remain $100 million | $105 million paid; $5 million fee | $105 million paid; $5 million margin |
| Costs rise to $120 million | $125 million paid; $5 million fee | $105 million paid; $15 million loss |

The first contractor’s fee remains $5 million; the government bears the extra $20 million. Under the firm price, that increase turns the anticipated margin into a loss. Both contracts can go over their original cost estimate, with very different consequences for the seller.

Retaining risk can make sense for the government. Research may involve work whose scope cannot yet be estimated reliably. Demanding a firm price can produce a large allowance for uncertainty, discourage bids or create later disputes about what the original promise included. Cost reimbursement still requires scrutiny of whether expenses are allowable and the work is being performed.

Nor does a fixed price settle every incentive. A poorly specified requirement can produce an argument over changes. A contractor can meet the stated terms while delivering something less useful than officials expected. The contract has to reward the result the military needs, with measures that track more than the easiest activity to count.

## The F-35: paying for one target, needing another

A [June 2026 GAO review of F-35 sustainment](https://files.gao.gov/reports/GAO-26-108113/index.html) provides a concrete example. Across fiscal years 2021–2025, the fleet’s mission-capable rate fell from 67 to 44 percent. Its full-mission-capable rate fell from 38 to 25 percent. The first measures the share of time an aircraft can perform at least one assigned mission; the second concerns all its assigned missions.

GAO also found that incentive arrangements had not aligned with the services’ readiness requirements. The air-vehicle contractor, Lockheed Martin, could qualify for fees at targets below those requirements. Program officials described the difficulty of negotiating attainable targets, reconciling different participants’ requirements and awarding contracts on time. The review examined arrangements from 2020 onward; its detailed comparison of full-mission-capable incentives covered 2020–2023.

Consider how a maintenance target can miss the intended result. Imagine a contract that rewards a repair organization for quickly dispatching parts. That can be worth measuring. But a part dispatched is not necessarily an aircraft repaired: it may be the wrong part, arrive without another needed component or wait for an available technician. Paying for dispatch alone can reward an activity that improves while the desired result does not.

In that hypothetical case, the dispatch target leaves out work needed to make the aircraft usable. Officials have to decide what the contractor controls, how improvement will be measured and what work other organizations must complete. A contract that penalizes a supplier for everything can be as badly designed as one that rewards it for very little.

The F-35 findings are serious because readiness is the purpose for which the support is bought. A payment can be permitted by a contract and still expose a weakness in the contract itself. Accountability then involves the officials who negotiated and approved the target as well as the contractor paid to meet it.

## Experience for sale, and a demonstrated conflict

Former military personnel can bring valuable knowledge to industry. They may understand how equipment is used, what fails in practice and how requirements are written. Their experience can help a supplier make equipment more useful.

A [2021 GAO review](https://www.gao.gov/products/gao-21-104311) found 1,718 former senior or acquisition officials who left the department during 2014–2019 and worked in 2019 at the 14 contractors it examined. GAO was measuring employment within that selected group of contractors, not establishing individual violations. The report examined restrictions and compliance practices and identified an opportunity to strengthen contractors’ responsibility for employees’ compliance.

The prospect of that later job can affect the official’s present position. An official who expects to seek work from a supplier may have a private interest in preserving that supplier’s goodwill. The official need not consciously decide to exchange a contract for a job for the situation to require disclosure and careful handling. When there is evidence of an exchange influencing a decision, the allegation can become much more specific.

Darleen Druyun’s case crossed that line. In its [2006 announcement of the Boeing settlement](https://www.justice.gov/archive/opa/pr/2006/June/06_civ_412.html), the Justice Department described Boeing’s hiring of her daughter and future son-in-law and its recruitment of Druyun while she held responsibility for Air Force contracts. Druyun and Boeing executive Michael Sears pleaded guilty to conflict-of-interest violations. According to the announcement, she admitted that the employment favors influenced her contracting decisions.

The $615 million settlement covered the hiring matter and separate disputes involving competitor information and launch contracts. The total spans those matters; it cannot be assigned solely to one tanker deal. The admission about employment favors establishes the conflict more directly than the size of the combined resolution.

The case gives the concern about employment influence a specific form. There is a responsible official, an employment interest and an admission that favors affected decisions. That is much stronger evidence than a list of people who changed employers.

## Dependence changes the cost of saying no

Procurement is not an ordinary consumer market in which an unhappy buyer can always choose another product. Changing a complex system can require retraining, new facilities, a different supply chain and years of development. Some of the dependence reflects technical work already done; some can be worsened by procurement choices that leave the buyer without usable alternatives.

A [2023 GAO review of defense mergers and acquisitions](https://www.gao.gov/products/gao-24-106129) found gaps in how the department assessed and monitored risks beyond competition, including national-security and innovation concerns. The issue was not only whether officials approved a merger, but whether they followed what happened afterward. The report’s later updates continued to describe resource constraints on that work.

A merger can remove a competing source or combine capabilities that had been difficult to sustain separately. The buyer needs to know what will remain: two viable production lines, one line under a new owner, or a supplier able to withdraw an alternative from the market. The number of company names alone cannot answer that.

Suppose the government buys equipment but leaves the repair manuals, specialist tools and training with the original supplier. Years later, it invites other companies to compete for maintenance. Those companies may have little chance of submitting a credible bid without access to the missing knowledge. In this hypothetical purchase, the first competition did not preserve the possibility of a second. Access for another maintainer would have had to be arranged and paid for.

The factory’s payroll can make the purchase difficult to reconsider, too. Consider a town whose main specialized employer supplies a weapons program. Employees and local businesses have immediate reasons to resist cancellation. Their interest is real, even if national analysis favors another use of the money. The gains from a change may be spread across many people, while the threatened losses have an address, a payroll and elected representatives.

Workers cannot move specialized skills as easily as a budget line can move money. If a program ends, the government has to decide whether it still needs the production capacity and how it will handle the disruption to the workforce. Leaving those decisions until cancellation is proposed gives supporters of the existing purchase a ready argument for another year.

## An audit disclaimer is a serious finding, not a missing-money total

The Inspector General’s [account of the fiscal-year 2025 financial-statement audit](https://www.dodig.mil/Reports/Audits-and-Evaluations/Article/4484312/the-fy-2025-dow-financial-statement-audit-explained-report-no-dowig-2026-081/) reports another agency-wide disclaimer of opinion. Auditors could not obtain sufficient reliable evidence to conclude that the statements were fairly presented. The explanation identified 26 material weaknesses.

The audit took place; the evidence was insufficient to support an opinion. The value of assets covered by it is not a theft total. In a simple inventory example, a record might list 100 components while checks reconcile 90. The other 10 require investigation; the unreconciled record does not establish that all 100 were lost. Real financial statements involve many more records and tests, but their total value is no substitute for tracing the discrepancy.

The weakness has operational consequences. If an organization cannot establish which parts it owns, their condition and where they are held, it has less reliable information for deciding what to buy or repair. Financial accountability and military readiness meet in the inventory.

A [GAO report published on 9 November 2026](https://files.gao.gov/reports/GAO-27-108713/index.html) examined that problem in the F-35 program. It described approximately 26 million parts, valued at about $31 billion, and incomplete records across contractor- and government-managed facilities. GAO found weaknesses in the property system, data validation and physical inventory procedures. The report also described ongoing work toward including parts in the financial statements.

Those estimates describe the parts population as of March 2026. The $31 billion is not a finding that the whole population disappeared. The finding identifies a control failure: the government needs reliable evidence about assets it owns and depends on, including assets held by contractors. Replacing a control failure with an unsupported theft total makes it harder to identify what must be repaired.

## A powerful interest can still lose

Defense programs can end, even after substantial work has been done. The Army’s [fiscal-year 2026 budget justification](https://www.asafm.army.mil/Portals/72/Documents/BudgetMaterial/2026/Discretionary%20Budget/rdte/RDTE%20-%20Vol%202%20-%20Budget%20Activity%204A.pdf) records its February 2024 decision to discontinue funding for the Future Attack Reconnaissance Aircraft beyond fiscal year 2024. It describes closing contracts, winding down the program and handling equipment and technology investments.

That decision brought closeout work and remaining obligations. Ending future development did not make the existing contracts and equipment vanish. It shows why the ability to stop a program has to be planned as carefully as the ability to begin one.

Stopping is not automatically wise, either. Money already spent cannot be recovered simply by continuing, but there may be useful work left to complete. The choice concerns future costs, future capability and feasible alternatives. A cancellation that saves a procurement line but leaves a costly capability gap has not demonstrated a net saving merely by ending the contract.

Suppose, in another simplified example, $10 billion has already been spent. Finishing the program would cost $20 billion more; a feasible alternative would cost $12 billion, including transition. The earlier $10 billion is gone under either choice. It cannot by itself justify spending the additional $8 billion. But if the alternative delivers materially less capability or arrives too late, the comparison must include that loss. The difficult work is estimating the remaining options honestly, not preserving the original plan to make an earlier estimate look defensible.

The benefit of readiness can also be difficult to measure. A force held ready may contribute to deterring an attack; its usefulness is not measured only by equipment fired in combat. But the absence of a war cannot identify which particular acquisition prevented it. Officials still have to explain how a proposed capability addresses a plausible threat, what else could do the job and what risks follow from buying less.

Contractor revenue does not answer those questions. Neither does the existence of a danger. Both can be real while a particular purchase is poorly designed or unnecessary.

## Keeping the option to change course

The pressure Eisenhower described does not end when one conflict or contract ends. Building a permanent capacity to fight also creates organizations that can make a strong case for their own continuation. Expertise, employment and dependence accumulate around decisions whose original justification may no longer be enough.

Scrutiny has to reach the places where a choice can still change: the requirement before it hardens into a design, the performance target before it becomes a payment rule, the employment negotiation before it compromises an official and the maintenance arrangement before alternatives disappear.

The test is whether officials can reconsider a commitment with reliable information about what is delivered and what changing course would cost. A military establishment serves the public more securely when the public can demand that account—and sometimes refuse the next purchase.
