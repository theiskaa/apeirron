The fatal bullet entered Robert Kennedy’s head just behind his right ear. Powder deposits showed that the muzzle had been very close. Yet the man seized with a gun in the Ambassador Hotel pantry had approached Kennedy from the front.

That is the difficulty at the center of the RFK assassination. Sirhan Sirhan’s presence as a shooter is exceptionally well established: people watched him fire, struggled with him, and took his revolver. Reconstructing the shot that killed Kennedy is harder. It requires fitting a medical examination to several seconds of movement that no witness recorded in full.

The case also carries the consequences of a poorly preserved investigation. Ceiling panels were destroyed. The original firearms examiner’s work did not withstand later scrutiny in all its particulars. A witness describing a possible accomplice underwent questioning that helped produce a recantation but left reasons to distrust it. Following these failures helps explain why a case with a gunman caught at the scene became an argument lasting decades.

## A candidate in a hotel pantry

Kennedy had won the California Democratic primary on June 4, 1968. He addressed supporters at the Ambassador Hotel in Los Angeles, then headed toward a press room through the pantry, stopping to greet hotel workers. Shortly after midnight, Sirhan began shooting. Kennedy and five other people were wounded. Kennedy died the following day, June 6.

The California result kept a serious presidential campaign alive. It had not secured the nomination. Hubert Humphrey remained a formidable opponent in a party whose delegates were selected through much more than primary elections. Even Kennedy’s associates later differed about his chances: campaign aide Peter Edelman thought he would have won; Harris Wofford put his chances of nomination at about even. The [recollections collected by PBS](https://www.pbs.org/wgbh/americanexperience/features/rfk-what-if/) preserve that disagreement. The death closed an uncertain political future, not an election whose result was already known.

Sirhan was twenty-four, born in Jerusalem and living in the Pasadena area. He carried a .22-caliber Iver Johnson revolver with an eight-round cylinder. Hotel employee Karl Uecker grabbed his gun hand; others joined the struggle. People continued to be shot while the effort to restrain him was under way. The weapon was recovered with its ammunition spent.

The five surviving victims were Paul Schrade, William Weisel, Ira Goldstein, Elizabeth Evans, and Irwin Stroll. Their wounds became part of the effort to trace the bullets through the pantry. Schrade, a labor official and Kennedy supporter, later helped press for a new examination of the evidence. His involvement is a reminder that criticism of the investigation did not begin with people indifferent to what Sirhan had done. One of the people he wounded became a persistent critic of the case against him.

Kennedy’s murder followed his brother’s assassination by less than five years and Martin Luther King Jr.’s by only two months. That succession helped make a young man with a revolver seem an inadequate explanation. For people mourning a political future as well as a person, the case against Sirhan had to answer a much larger suspicion about who could exercise power in the country.

## What the jury was asked to decide

At the 1969 trial, Sirhan’s lawyers did not build their defense around the claim that somebody else had fired the fatal bullet. They argued diminished capacity: that his mental condition prevented the deliberation required for first-degree murder. The defense called psychiatrists and psychologists, including Bernard Diamond, who described severe mental illness and a dissociated state. The prosecution’s psychiatrist, Seymour Pollack, disputed the defense’s conclusions about Sirhan’s capacity to plan and understand his act.

Pollack did not present Sirhan as an entirely healthy man. He described substantial psychological disturbance but concluded that Sirhan retained the relevant capacity. The disagreement concerned how his condition affected his responsibility. Calling the trial a choice between a sane assassin and a mind-controlled innocent erases the question the competing clinicians actually addressed.

There was evidence of preparation. According to the trial record summarized in the [California Supreme Court’s 1972 opinion](https://openjurist.org/7/cal3d/710/people-v-sirhan), Sirhan practiced with his revolver at a gun range for several hours on June 4, including rapid firing. He had been at the Ambassador two days earlier. Before the shooting, he asked hotel employees whether Kennedy would pass through the pantry. They said they did not know.

Police recovered notebooks containing repeated statements about killing Kennedy, including a deadline before June 5. Sirhan acknowledged the handwriting while disputing his memory of producing the entries. The writings were evidence of a fixation that preceded the shooting, although their repetition also entered the psychiatric debate. Pollack interpreted them as an effort to strengthen Sirhan’s resolve. Later advocates would interpret them as automatic writing under suggestion.

Some testimony deserved particular caution. A trash collector said Sirhan had spoken of shooting Kennedy before the assassination. On cross-examination, however, he acknowledged such hatred of Sirhan that he would do anything to obtain a conviction. That admission was part of the trial record too. The case should not acquire extra strength by repeating his accusation without the reason to distrust him.

Sirhan’s hostility to Kennedy’s support for Israel also appeared in the trial evidence. A newspaper article found on him discussed Kennedy’s willingness to provide arms. He described his views about the Arab-Israeli conflict in testimony. His statements and writings give the motive a basis beyond an inference from his Palestinian background. Nor would the absence of membership in a political organization make an individual political grievance implausible.

The jury convicted him of first-degree murder and five assaults and imposed death. In 1972 the California Supreme Court changed the murder sentence to life imprisonment after its broader ruling against the death penalty under the state constitution, while affirming the conviction. The murder conviction remained in force.

## The distance between a wound and a witness

The [medical investigation led by Thomas Noguchi](https://www.archives.gov/files/research/rfk/releases/2025/0612/1536518_grf_us_presidents_commission_on_cia_activities_within_the_united_states_rockefeller_commission_temporary_parallel_file_box_2.pdf) documented three gunshot wounds. The fatal bullet entered the right mastoid region, behind the ear, and penetrated the brain. The other two entered the right armpit area: one passed through soft tissue and emerged toward the front of the upper chest; the other traveled into the upper back and was recovered near the sixth cervical vertebra. There was also a bullet path through the clothing that did not enter Kennedy’s body.

The report follows each path through tissue and records its entrance and, where there was one, its exit. That lets an examiner distinguish the two holes made by a bullet passing through the body from wounds caused by separate rounds.

Noguchi’s team also investigated firing distance. In test firings, they compared powder patterns produced at different distances and angles. One arrangement used fabric to simulate the area behind the ear and an animal ear to approximate its shape. At a muzzle distance of about an inch from the edge of the simulated ear, the test pattern most closely resembled the deposits observed on Kennedy. A two-inch test retained similarities in the distribution of individual grains.

Those experiments support a very close shot. They also show how the conclusion was reached: through comparison under an approximate reconstruction, rather than a measurement of the original gun-to-head distance. Trial testimony summarized in the 1972 opinion put the fatal shot at an inch and a half or less. A reconstruction placing every shot several feet from Kennedy would have to explain the powder evidence.

Uecker and other witnesses initially placed Sirhan in front of Kennedy. Critics have reasonably asked how that position fits the rear entrance and short distance. Uecker was especially important because he was close to both men and took hold of the gun arm. His recollection cannot be brushed aside simply because the medical evidence is inconvenient.

But the witnesses did not supply a continuous set of positions for every shot. In her [2015 federal order](https://www.williampepper.com/pdf/Judge_BRO_order_accepting_Magistrate_AJW_report_-_1-5-2015.pdf), Judge Beverly Reid O’Connell reviewed testimony about Kennedy turning, Sirhan advancing or lunging, raised arms, and the struggle. Uecker’s trial testimony included Kennedy beginning to turn as the shooting and fight developed. Other witnesses differed about distance and about how many shots preceded the attempt to restrain Sirhan.

A wound described as coming from behind refers to the victim’s body at the instant of impact. A witness saying that a man approached from the front may be describing his position before the shooting or at its beginning. Kennedy’s head, torso, and the gun arm could move separately. Their starting positions alone cannot resolve the later geometry.

The court found that the witnesses did not identify the instant of the fatal shot. It reasoned that a jury could accept their evidence of Sirhan shooting while declining to treat their position estimates as exact. The witnesses’ accounts of movement give the court’s reasoning a basis. Even so, the surviving record lacks a sequence showing precisely how the muzzle reached the position Noguchi’s findings require.

## Eight rounds and the later examination

The familiar arithmetic for a second gun starts with four bullet paths involving Kennedy or his clothing and five other wounded people. That makes nine, against an eight-round revolver. But the LAPD reconstruction assigned a single bullet to the passage through Kennedy’s shoulder pad and then to Schrade’s head wound. Its total was eight.

Whether that path fits the positions of Schrade and Kennedy is a question about the reconstruction. The count of wounded people cannot answer it on its own. Ceiling defects create a related counting problem: a bullet can enter and leave a panel, producing more than one hole.

The more consequential challenge concerned the marks on recovered bullets. A rifled barrel leaves grooves and finer scratches as a bullet passes through it. Examiners compare those marks with one another and with bullets fired under controlled conditions from the suspected weapon. Broad compatibility is easier to establish than identification of an individual gun, especially when impact has damaged the bullet.

At trial, LAPD criminalist DeWayne Wolfer identified recovered bullets as having been fired from Sirhan’s weapon. Critics, including William Harper, challenged the comparisons. In 1975 Judge Robert Wenke appointed seven firearms examiners to study the evidence and test-fire the revolver. The examination included bullets recovered from the victims and test bullets from the original investigation as well as new firings.

The results were more limited than Wolfer’s original certainty. The panel could not identify the three principal comparison bullets—from Kennedy, Goldstein, and Weisel—as having been fired from Sirhan’s particular revolver. There were insufficient corresponding individual marks. Possible reasons included barrel fouling, damage on impact, cylinder alignment, and loss of fine detail over time.

The Kennedy bullet in that comparison was the one recovered near his neck, not an intact fatal bullet taken from his brain. The fatal projectile had fragmented. This limits what the comparison could answer even in ideal conditions: identifying the neck bullet would connect a gun to one wound, while the inference about the fatal wound would still depend on the rest of the shooting evidence. A reference to “the Kennedy bullet” can conceal that difference.

The examiners also found no significant difference in the rifling angles that had been offered as evidence of separate weapons. Their overall conclusion was that the examined bullets did not supply substantive evidence of more than one gun. These were two findings: the panel could not reproduce the individual identification, and it did not establish a second weapon.

Special counsel Thomas Kranz’s [review of the examination and hearings](https://www.archives.gov/files/research/rfk/releases/2025/0507/62-hq-587_ebf_1336_sec_001_ser_1336_only-part_2_of_6.pdf) also records problems with Wolfer’s documentation and his identification of a photograph. A picture he described as showing an evidence bullet beside a test bullet was identified by the later examiners as showing two evidence bullets. The mistake concerns the very comparison the photograph was supposed to document.

The defense’s decision to contest mental capacity rather than the identity of the fatal shooter meant that the trial had not subjected the bullet identification to the kind of independent examination it later received. This helps explain why the dispute lasted. A subsequent inquiry could undermine the original examiner’s confidence without producing either an alternative weapon or a new trial.

By 1975, handling and the passage of time had complicated the attempt to check Wolfer’s work. The panel left a less decisive identification than the jury originally heard, and no finding of a second weapon.

## What the investigation threw away

Some of the material needed for independent examination was no longer available. In 1975, Assistant Chief Darryl Gates acknowledged that the LAPD had destroyed pantry ceiling panels, their X-rays, and related records in 1969. The justification was that the tests had already been made and the material had no further value. Kranz’s account records that explanation alongside the ensuing dispute.

That was a poor standard for preserving evidence in an assassination. Independent examiners might ask different questions or find that the original records did not adequately answer the old ones. Once the panels were gone, an assurance that their examination had settled everything became harder to test.

When the files opened in 1988, an [Associated Press report from the release](https://newspaper.library.tamu.edu/lccn/sn86088544/1988-04-20/ed-1/seq-14.pdf) described destroyed door jambs and 2,410 photographs recorded as burned in August 1968. Archivist John Burns said the subjects of the burned photographs were unknown. Calling all of them photographs of the shooting would go beyond that record.

Door-frame holes generated another argument. Investigators and photographers recorded marks described as possible or reported bullet holes. Wolfer said the examined wood contained no bullets or bullet fragments. A marked photograph and a witness’s description cannot now substitute for laboratory examination of the material. The competing claims concern whether those marks were made by bullets at all, as well as how many rounds were fired.

Retaining the material would have allowed later examiners to look for metal, inspect the edges of a hole, and compare its path with the rest of the scene. In its absence, neither the police dismissal nor an additional bullet count can be checked in the same way.

The handling of Sandra Serrano presents a different kind of loss. She reported that a woman in a polka-dot dress had passed her on an outside stairway after the shooting and claimed participation. The [California State Archives’ tape inventory](https://archives.cdn.sos.ca.gov/collections/rfk/appendix-e.pdf) lists her June 20 questioning in three stages: an initial interview, a polygraph examination during which she maintained the account, and a subsequent interview in which she recanted.

The [recorded-interview transcript](https://www.isgp-studies.com/misc/rfk/testimonies/polka-dot-dress/sandra-serrano-1968-06-20-recant.pdf) shows Sergeant Enrique Hernandez repeatedly supplying the preferred explanation: information had been mixed up, the dress had been embellished, the account magnified. He praised her as an honest woman when she agreed. Serrano also complained that questioning and exposure to other witnesses’ accounts had confused her. The document records a recantation, but it gives reasons to question the independence of the account produced in that room.

The interview leaves us with two conflicting accounts and a questioning process that helped shape the second. It also leaves the reported woman unidentified. The later description of her as Sirhan’s handler adds a relationship and a history of conditioning that Serrano’s report did not describe.

## The guard and the recording

Thane Eugene Cesar, a security guard near Kennedy, became the most frequently named alternative gunman. He acknowledged drawing a weapon, a .38-caliber revolver, but denied firing. The caliber differs from the .22 bullets recovered in the case. Proponents of his involvement therefore also invoke his ownership of a separate .22, making the allegation depend on which weapon he carried and used.

Donald Schulman’s accounts have often been offered as direct evidence against a guard. His immediate recorded description included a claim that a security guard had shot Kennedy. In other interviews he said he had seen a drawn gun but had not seen a guard fire. A later documentary included a stronger accusation again. Kranz’s report sets out these conflicting versions and the questions about where Schulman had been standing. They cannot fairly be presented as one consistent identification maintained unchanged from the night of the shooting.

Kranz also reported that Cesar’s .38 was not test-fired or retained. Cesar said officers had examined it, a claim the LAPD orally supported without documentation. Preserving and documenting that examination would have been preferable to leaving researchers to argue over an account of an inspection. The file consequently leaves an avoidable gap in the examination of a man close to Kennedy who acknowledged drawing a gun.

A recording made by reporter Stanislaw Pruszynski later offered another route to the bullet count. Philip Van Praag identified more than eight apparent shots, including closely spaced impulses that he thought a single revolver could not have produced. If those sounds are separate gunshots, the analysis would support another weapon. The issue is their classification, not the subtraction of eight from thirteen.

In the federal proceedings, an opposing analysis by Phillip Harrison identified eight shots and disputed that the extra impulses had the characteristics of gunfire. The sides also disputed assumptions about the microphone and the struggle over Sirhan’s hand. A sharp impulse on an old recording has to be distinguished from other abrupt sounds and the recording’s response to them before it can be counted as another discharge.

O’Connell’s 2015 order considered the competing analyses but did not purport to settle every acoustic disagreement. It concluded that Van Praag’s work did not meet the demanding test for reopening Sirhan’s otherwise time-barred petition through a claim of actual innocence. The order leaves the acoustic disagreement unresolved while rejecting the legal claim made with it.

## The claim of a programmed shooter

Sirhan’s accounts of lost memory and the evidence of his responsiveness to hypnosis helped give the case its most elaborate explanation: that he had been conditioned to fire as a distraction while another gunman killed Kennedy. Later psychologist Daniel Brown argued that Sirhan had undergone coercive persuasion involving hypnosis and other methods. His opinion became part of the effort to reopen the conviction.

The theory offers a single cause for the notebooks, amnesia, possible accomplice, and disputed shot positions. The history of [[mkultra]] also gives government interest in manipulating behavior a real documentary basis. Intelligence agencies did investigate hypnosis, drugs, interrogation, and memory, often through abusive experiments.

In the 2015 order, the court described his interpretation of Sirhan’s personality and later recollections, including suggestions allegedly conveyed by an unidentified man and through a radio. It noted the lack of further evidence identifying the drugs or sensory-deprivation procedures Brown proposed. The diagnosis and recollections were doing most of the work of establishing the supposed operation.

The earlier clinicians had disagreed with one another. Diamond’s defense testimony described a dissociated state; Pollack found evidence of reasoning and planning and rejected the claimed loss of capacity. Later experts’ disagreement with Diamond does not remove Pollack’s observations or resolve the conflict. Nor can a subject’s ability to respond to hypnosis in an examination establish who influenced him before the crime.

The court also cited material that complicated a uniform account of total amnesia: detailed notes supplied to defense investigator Michael McCowan and his account of a conversation in which Sirhan described Kennedy turning his head. Such evidence needs its own scrutiny, particularly where a conversation was later reported by another person. But a narrative of complete, consistently demonstrated memory loss cannot simply leave it out.

A later recollection can suggest a lead to investigate. When it appears decades after an event and after extensive discussion of a proposed explanation, its agreement with that explanation is not independent confirmation of it. In this case, a record of contact with an identified programmer, a contemporary instruction, or corroborated preparation would carry a different weight from an expert’s interpretation of hypnotic responsiveness. The material considered in 2015 did not supply that connection.

## What a later decision can answer

The federal ruling concerned a specific legal obstacle. Sirhan’s petition was untimely, and he sought to pass through an exception requiring new, reliable evidence sufficient to show that, more likely than not, no reasonable juror would convict. The court found the showing inadequate and denied an evidentiary hearing. That is a more demanding task than pointing to a questionable police decision or an unresolved detail in a reconstruction.

Parole answers another question again. In August 2021 a panel found Sirhan suitable for release. Governor Gavin Newsom reversed that decision on January 13, 2022, arguing that Sirhan remained a public-safety risk, particularly because of his account of the crime and lack of insight. The [written decision](https://www.gov.ca.gov/wp-content/uploads/2022/01/1.13.22-Sirhan-Reverse-Decision.pdf) concerns suitability for release, not a fresh trial of the pantry evidence. A parole grant would not establish innocence; its reversal does not repair the original investigation.

More records became accessible in federal releases in April, May, and June 2025. The [National Archives’ release page](https://www.archives.gov/research/rfk) distinguishes the newly published files, earlier Freedom of Information Act copies, and evidentiary material. Documents can include allegations collected by investigators as well as their findings. Publication gives researchers material to check; the presence of a claim in an agency’s archive does not mean the agency verified it.

The comparison with [[jfk]] is strongest in the long argument over what the official evidence can bear. It is weakest when family tragedy is made to establish a single perpetrator behind both murders. The two shootings have different witnesses, weapons, medical findings, and investigative histories.

In the RFK case, the evidence places Sirhan in the pantry firing a revolver after preparations and writings directed at Kennedy. It has not established a second gunman or a conditioning operation. The close-range fatal wound remains the most important demand on any reconstruction, and the destruction and weak documentation of evidence have made checking that reconstruction harder. We can hold the shooter responsible while also holding the investigation responsible for the questions it left unnecessarily difficult to answer.
