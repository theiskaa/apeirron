The American flag on USS Liberty was shot away during the air attack. Before the torpedo boats closed in, the crew raised a larger one, seven feet by thirteen. The ship was already burning. Her captain, William McGonagle, had been wounded; several officers around him were dead or dying. The replacement flag was one of the few ways left to tell an approaching force whose ship it was attacking.

On June 8, 1967, Israeli aircraft and torpedo boats attacked Liberty in international waters off the Sinai Peninsula. Thirty-four people were killed and 171 wounded. The torpedo tore open the ship’s intelligence spaces below decks. Liberty survived through the work of a crew that had lost much of its ability to defend itself in the first air passes.

Israel said its forces had mistaken the ship for an Egyptian vessel. The Navy inquiry and American intelligence assessments attributed the attack to mistaken identity, but their judgments about responsibility could be scathing. Secretary of State Dean Rusk demanded disciplinary action. Clark Clifford, reviewing the incident for the White House, found gross failures of command and identification. Decades later, survivors and the Navy inquiry’s former counsel argued that the attack had been deliberate and the investigation constrained to protect Israel.

An identifiable American ship had come under attack by an ally’s air and naval forces. The investigation established much about what happened to her crew and much less about the decisions of the men who fired. The gap between those two inquiries is where the dispute has persisted.

## A listening ship near a war

Liberty was a converted cargo ship equipped to collect signals intelligence: radio transmissions and other electronic communications that could help American analysts understand foreign military activity. Her antennas and technical spaces served an intelligence mission; the official designation “technical research ship” gave little indication of the work inside. Four .50-calibre machine guns provided limited protection. They did not equip her to fight aircraft and fast torpedo boats.

The Six-Day War had begun on June 5. Israel was fighting Egypt, Jordan and Syria, and the United States wanted information about a conflict in which it was trying to avoid becoming a belligerent. A ship close to the coast could receive transmissions unavailable to a collector farther away. That advantage also brought Liberty within reach of forces conducting an active war.

The intelligence mission was obscured in the public explanation prepared after the attack. Officials described Liberty as helping maintain American government communications and relay information about evacuating civilians. Protecting the listening operation meant giving the public an account that concealed why the ship was there.

The NSA’s later operational history describes the ship’s principal collection task as Egyptian communications. It records no specific assignment to intercept Israeli communications, partly because the ship lacked Hebrew linguists. Some routine Israeli air traffic was recorded incidentally. Later theories that the attack suppressed an intercepted Israeli atrocity or secret order depend on a recording or report that these records do not contain.

Meanwhile, American commanders were reconsidering the ship’s position. On the evening before the attack, the Joint Chiefs ordered greater separation from the coast, then decided that Liberty should remain at least 100 nautical miles from Israel, Syria and Egypt. The orders passed through a communications system that repeatedly failed to deliver them to their destination.

One message went toward the Pacific rather than the Mediterranean. An oral instruction outside the usual command route prompted a request for written confirmation. Further delays occurred at headquarters and in retransmission. A message intended for Liberty went through a station she was not using; another was misrouted between communications facilities. The ship did not receive the withdrawal instructions before she was attacked.

The original Navy inquiry heard a communications officer explain the result: Liberty was operating under orders that headquarters had superseded, but that the ship did not know had changed. Headquarters had decided to remove the ship from danger hours before the attack. On Liberty, the captain was still acting on the orders he had received.

## The attack as the crew experienced it

Aircraft had passed near Liberty during the morning. In his inquiry testimony, McGonagle described jets whose markings he could not identify and a transport-type aircraft returning at intervals. The overflights did not appear threatening. Men aboard could see the fighting on the coast; they had no warning that their own ship would be attacked.

Early that afternoon the crew conducted a drill. McGonagle pointed to smoke ashore to impress on the men the need to close the ship’s watertight fittings quickly. Afterward he stayed on the bridge. At about two o’clock, while watching an aircraft through binoculars from one side of the bridge, he heard an explosion on the other.

The first air passes struck the gun positions and bridge area. Lloyd Painter, the officer of the deck, testified that he was trying to telephone the forward gun crews when their positions were hit. He described seeing the area break up in smoke and metal. Fire, damaged communications and casualties made organizing a response difficult almost immediately. McGonagle ordered more speed; men below worked to bring the ship’s machinery into action while others fought fires and treated the wounded.

The inquiry recorded apparent radio jamming during the air attack, described as a continuous signal interfering with voice communications. The NSA history also describes interference as operators changed frequencies. From the ship, the effect was an urgent practical problem: calling for help was difficult when help was most needed. The reports do not, by themselves, identify the operator of the interference or establish what that operator knew about the ship’s nationality.

After the air attack, three torpedo boats approached. The crew raised the larger flag. McGonagle initially ordered a forward machine gun to fire, then recognized an Israeli flag on a boat and ordered firing withheld. A short burst had already been fired; ammunition in an unattended gun also began discharging in the heat of a fire. By then the air assault had already inflicted extensive damage and knocked out most of the ship’s defensive capacity.

A torpedo struck at about 2:35 p.m. It opened a tear roughly thirty-nine feet long in the starboard side and flooded the research spaces across two deck levels. Twenty-five men died in that part of the attack. The ship listed and lost steerage. A hatch had to be closed to contain the flooding after an unsuccessful attempt to reach men below.

The main air and torpedo attacks occupied roughly half an hour. The danger lasted much longer. Boats remained nearby or returned; helicopters and reconnaissance aircraft appeared; the crew feared another torpedo would finish the ship. Later, power and manual steering allowed Liberty to make slow progress away.

The usual treatment area in the wardroom was badly damaged, so the mess decks became a casualty station. Liberty’s doctor and two corpsmen relied on volunteers to carry men, dress wounds and give assistance. Keeping machinery running also meant keeping the lights and pumps available to people working elsewhere in the ship. McGonagle remained on the bridge despite his wounds. Painter later remembered the night on the mess decks as men continued dying around them.

The liferafts became a particularly bitter dispute. In the preserved inquiry testimony, Painter said the rafts had been strafed and burned and that damaged ones were thrown overboard. The court’s damage findings described rafts holed or burned in their stowages. Later survivor accounts alleged that boats deliberately fired on rafts in the water, an allegation repeated by a private commission led by retired Admiral Thomas Moorer. Ward Boston, the inquiry’s former counsel, said in 2004 that testimony about such firing had been removed from the record. He specifically identified Painter’s testimony as incomplete in the released record.

## A ship identified in the morning

Israel’s account acknowledged that Liberty had been identified earlier in the day. As summarized in the NSA history, Israeli naval headquarters first plotted an unidentified ship, then marked it as American, and eventually recorded the name Liberty. Her position was later removed from the plot because it was considered out of date. That created the setting for a ship known to headquarters in the morning to be treated as an unknown target in the afternoon.

Reports of shelling near El Arish contributed to the alarm. Torpedo boats went out, and a radar report assigned the distant vessel a speed of about thirty knots. Such a speed suggested a warship; it was far beyond Liberty’s actual movement. Aircraft were sent to investigate and attacked. Doubts about identification emerged, but the boats subsequently attacked a ship they said they had identified as the Egyptian El Quseir.

There were serious problems with this explanation even for American officials who concluded that the attack had been a mistake. A June 21 CIA assessment called the reported speed bizarre for a ship of Liberty’s appearance. It questioned the torpedo boats’ identification and noted that doubts had already arisen among the airmen. The assessment also corrected an earlier American account that had missed the morning identification. It concluded that mistaken identity remained the likely explanation while describing the Israeli account as self-serving and pointing to gross negligence.

Headquarters had information that should have prevented the attack. The plot had contained the ship’s nationality and name. Removing the marker lost that protection, according to the Israeli account. The American assessments found serious failures in passing the earlier identification to the attacking forces, while leaving unresolved how far down the command system accurate information had travelled.

The Israeli inquiry also examined an order to the boats not to attack because the target’s identity was uncertain. In the NSA history’s summary of that inquiry, the division commander said he never received it. His deputy said he had received and passed it on. The examining judge accepted that the message could have escaped the commander’s attention in the heat of battle. Even within Israel’s account, a caution prompted by doubts about the target had failed to stop the torpedoes.

The pilots and boat crews also had a ship in front of them. Liberty had English hull markings, an American flag and a silhouette that differed substantially from El Quseir. Rusk’s diplomatic note insisted that the vessel should have been identified and that the boats had time to inspect her before using torpedoes. The note demanded disciplinary measures and compensation. Its language expressed anger at the conduct of an ally, rather than acceptance that an unavoidable battlefield confusion had occurred.

[Clifford’s July review](https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1964-68v19/d373) reached a similarly severe judgment. He found no available evidence that the Israeli high command had ordered a premeditated attack on a ship it knew was American. He also found the proposed confusion with an Egyptian vessel unbelievable and the failures of command inexcusable. His conclusion against premeditation did not excuse the decisions to fire without establishing identity. He held the Israeli government responsible and called for punishment of the military personnel concerned.

The Israeli examining judge reached a much more forgiving conclusion in July 1967. He found the officers’ conduct insufficiently unreasonable to warrant trial. He accepted reliance on the reported speed and the claimed resemblance between the ships. Even the Israeli military prosecutor had challenged that reasoning: El Quseir could not plausibly be both the slow auxiliary identified by the boats and the fast vessel supposedly implicated in coastal shelling. The judge nevertheless regarded the identification error as possible. The NSA history later rejected that reasoning sharply. Its account records deputy director Louis Tordella’s description of the preliminary inquiry as a whitewash.

For the men aboard Liberty, the repeated attacks and the large replacement flag made an innocent identification failure difficult to accept. They had watched weapons hit their ship at close range. The official explanation asked them to believe that the force doing the shooting had failed to recognize what seemed unmistakable from the deck. For the survivors, that explanation meant accepting a succession of identification failures as the cause of their shipmates’ deaths.

## What the intercepts recorded

The surviving intelligence recordings are often asked to settle the question of intent. Those discussed in the NSA history cover the helicopters sent out after the attack.

An American airborne collection aircraft recorded those helicopters’ communications with ground control. Ground control initially described the ship as Egyptian. Later exchanges sought its identity; a helicopter reported an American flag and the hull marking GTR-5. The traffic shows confusion in that rescue and reconnaissance exchange, followed by recognition of an American vessel.

It was not a recording of the first attacking pilots receiving their orders, or of the torpedo boats deciding to fire. The NSA history says communications from those attackers were not available to its account. A helicopter’s surprise can be evidence of what that crew and its controller believed at that moment. It cannot settle what other officers knew earlier.

Other American collection continued after Liberty was hit. The airborne recording survived because she was one of several listening platforms in the region. Sinking Liberty would have destroyed a source of intelligence, while leaving others in operation.

## American help and American restraint

The response unfolded across a large distance. The carriers America and Saratoga were roughly 450 miles west of Liberty. The NSA chronology records orders to launch aircraft during the afternoon, with instructions to protect the ship and drive off or destroy forces clearly attacking her while avoiding unnecessary involvement in the war.

Before those aircraft arrived, Israel notified the United States that its forces had attacked an American ship by mistake. Washington was also communicating with Moscow. Johnson used the hotline to explain that American aircraft were being sent to investigate and that the United States wanted to avoid further hostilities. The Soviet leadership passed word to Egypt. The aircraft were recalled.

Later critics argued that American leaders had subordinated the crew’s safety to the relationship with Israel. The Moorer commission’s findings included the charge that rescue aircraft were recalled while Liberty was still under attack. The published NSA chronology records aircraft being called back after the Israeli acknowledgment and Washington’s exchanges with Moscow. It does not resolve the commission’s allegation of a recall during the attack. Establishing that earlier sequence would require the launch and recall orders to be traced separately, rather than treating every reference to a recall as the same event.

Contact that evening showed how uncertain communication remained. An Israeli helicopter brought the American naval attaché from Tel Aviv, but he could not board. A package weighted with an orange was dropped onto the ship. Inside was his calling card, with a question about casualties written on it. Liberty replied by signal lamp. The attaché understood there were casualties, but could not tell whether the number being flashed was four or forty. American destroyers reached the ship the next morning.

## What kind of inquiry was conducted?

The Navy court convened two days after the attack and worked on a compressed schedule. Rear Admiral Isaac Kidd presided; Captain Ward Boston served as counsel. Its appointing order directed it to examine all pertinent circumstances of the attack, the damage and the casualties. The record contains testimony from injured crewmen, communications officers and technical personnel, together with logs, messages and photographs.

Its findings said the available evidence indicated mistaken identity. They also recorded the coordinated and destructive character of the attack, the replacement flag, apparent jamming and the failure of withdrawal orders to reach the ship. Its principal strength was reconstructing what happened aboard Liberty and within American communications. It heard no Israeli witnesses. A finding about the cause of another country’s attack consequently rested on a much thinner examination than its account of the American ship’s damage and conduct.

The administration understood that limit. A June 26 White House memorandum discussing the public release said the court believed the attack resulted from an Israeli communications failure, but that the public summary should avoid a flat judgment about intent. It explained that the inquiry had not been responsible for determining the attackers’ culpability and had heard no Israeli witnesses. The administration thus published a summary more guarded about intent than the court’s own findings. Rusk’s demands for accountability and Clifford’s criticism existed alongside the finding of mistake.

Other reviews followed. A Joint Chiefs team examined command and communications failures and proposed changes. The House Appropriations Committee used the missed messages as the starting point for an investigation of military communications. Neither review questioned the Israeli officers who ordered and carried out the attack. An investigation of messages lost on the way to Liberty could establish why the ship remained near the coast; it could not establish what the attackers had been told.

Boston’s later challenge came from inside the original proceeding. In a [signed declaration dated January 8, 2004](https://ifamericansknew.org/download/BostonDeclaration.pdf), he said that he and Kidd had regarded the attack as deliberate, that the inquiry had been given far too little time, and that Kidd told him the finding of mistake had been dictated from above. He also alleged alterations to the certified record, including the loss of testimony about liferafts.

Boston had helped take the testimony and prepare the record he was challenging. His account of presidential instructions came through what Kidd allegedly told him; he did not claim to have heard Johnson issue the order. The alleged changes to the transcript would require a comparison with the certified record Boston said he had prepared. Kidd had died before Boston’s declaration, leaving that part of the account without a reply from its central intermediary.

The private commission chaired by Moorer reached much broader conclusions in October 2003, calling the attack deliberate and demanding a new investigation. Representative John Conyers later inserted its findings and a shorter Boston affidavit into the Congressional Record. That gave the challenge a public congressional platform. It did not turn a private commission’s conclusions into findings adopted by Congress.

## The settlement and what it left unsettled

Israel paid compensation for the dead in 1968 and the injured in 1969. A separate settlement for damage to the ship followed in 1980. McGonagle received the Medal of Honor; Liberty received a unit citation. The vessel was decommissioned in June 1968 and eventually sold for scrap.

Payments and decorations addressed recognizable obligations. They could compensate families, acknowledge courage and close a claim for a damaged ship. They could not answer a survivor asking why another force had repeatedly attacked a clearly marked American vessel, or why the people who ordered the attack were never questioned by the American court.

The strongest contemporary American assessments leave little basis for describing the attack as a blameless mishap. They identify an American ship, information in Israeli hands that should have protected it, failures to identify before firing, and responsibility for the resulting deaths. Their conclusion against premeditation was a judgment on the evidence available to them, not the product of a full examination of the attackers.

The ship survived because people stayed at damaged machinery, fought fires, treated one another and kept working after the torpedo. Whether the next boat was coming to help or to fire again was a question they had to answer from the deck, long before officials agreed on words for what had happened.
