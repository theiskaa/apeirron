An investor can appear to own a bank while borrowing the purchase money from someone else. That arrangement becomes more troubling when the lender holds the shares, guarantees the investor against loss, and treats the acquired institution as part of its own business. The name on the ownership papers then tells only part of the story.

This was central to the Bank of Credit and Commerce International's concealed acquisition of American banks. BCCI used nominees and complicated financing arrangements to obtain interests it could not openly secure on the same terms. Its relationships with politicians, intelligence figures, accountants, and lawyers helped the arrangement survive scrutiny.

Nugan Hand, the smaller Australian financial group that collapsed in 1980, also attracted money through impressive connections and unreliable representations of its financial condition. Former American military and intelligence figures appeared around its business. Its partner Michael Hand disappeared, leaving investigators without the person who might answer many of their questions.

Their records show different ways to separate the appearance of a transaction from the people and obligations behind it. They also show investigators reaching different conclusions about intelligence involvement. BCCI’s documented relationships cannot fill the gaps in Nugan Hand’s history.

## The bank across jurisdictions

BCCI was established in 1972 under Pakistani banker Agha Hasan Abedi. It grew into an international group with banking entities incorporated in Luxembourg and the Cayman Islands and extensive operations elsewhere. Its cross-border structure divided oversight among authorities that did not possess a complete view of the business.

The [Kerry–Brown Senate investigation](https://irp.fas.org/congress/1992_rpt/bcci/) treated this fragmentation as part of the bank's design. Accounts, affiliates, nominees, and jurisdictions could be separated when someone sought responsibility, then treated as a common pool when management wanted to move money. A transaction could appear one way in a local set of books while serving another purpose within the group.

A hypothetical loan shows the problem. On one balance sheet, a bank records an asset: money a borrower owes it. If an undisclosed agreement excuses that borrower from the risk, the apparent asset is less secure than the accounts suggest. If the borrower uses the money to purchase shares on the bank's behalf, the transaction may also conceal ownership. The paperwork must be read as a set. A loan agreement alone will not show the side arrangement that changes its meaning.

BCCI's [criminal history](https://irp.fas.org/congress/1992_rpt/bcci/04crime.htm) included fraudulent records, sham transactions, laundering drug proceeds, and concealed ownership through nominees. The bank's liquidators agreed to a broad criminal plea in December 1991 that admitted institutional conduct involving deception and illicit finance.

An ordinary depositor could have savings at risk without knowing anything about those arrangements. The presence of a famous arms dealer in the account files may attract attention, but it is the concealed condition of the institution that connects the scandal to everyone who trusted it with money.

## Buying a bank without appearing to buy it

According to the Senate's [account of BCCI's entry into the United States](https://irp.fas.org/congress/1992_rpt/bcci/06early.htm), the group wanted access to domestic banking opportunities, including the advantages offered by federally insured institutions. Regulatory obstacles stood in the way. It used investors presented as independent owners rather than disclosing BCCI's actual position.

The relationship continued after the acquisitions. In the report's [account of First American's management](https://irp.fas.org/congress/1992_rpt/bcci/07later.htm), BCCI officials discussed the American institutions alongside their own offices as parts of one business. They placed personnel, helped arrange capital, and sought to coordinate expansion. The nominal separation that mattered to regulators did not describe how BCCI understood its interests.

A genuine outside shareholder risks losing the money invested. A nominee protected by undisclosed arrangements can face a different bargain. The apparent owner’s willingness to buy is then less reassuring: the person making the decision may not bear the loss the regulator assumes. Examining the loan, collateral, and guarantees together reveals who has an incentive to act and who has merely supplied a name.

Concealment created a second problem inside the accounts. A holding carried as a loan still needed to look like a performing loan. If interest was not paid, adding it to the balance could enlarge the asset on paper without bringing money into the bank. BCCI's ownership arrangements therefore contributed to the very financial weakness they helped hide.

The bank was borrowing credibility as well as moving funds. Clark Clifford and Robert Altman served in prominent roles at First American and as BCCI's lawyers. Clifford had been U.S. defense secretary and a senior presidential adviser. His reputation made the arrangement appear less likely to conceal a fundamentally different owner.

Their individual responsibility was examined in separate proceedings. A New York jury acquitted Altman in August 1993, and the state subsequently dismissed the charges against Clifford. A [later federal court opinion records those outcomes](https://law.justia.com/cases/federal/district-courts/FSupp2/2/58/2486543/). In [February 1998, the Federal Reserve announced a settlement](https://www.federalreserve.gov/boarddocs/press/enforcement/1998/19980203/default.htm): without admitting its allegations, Clifford and Altman agreed to transfer shares worth approximately $5 million for the benefit of BCCI's innocent creditors and depositors. Altman accepted a restriction on participation in banking without the Board's approval. The Board dismissed its prohibition action against Clifford because of age and ill health.

The administrative settlement and the criminal dispositions are different outcomes; the payment cannot be substituted for a finding of criminal guilt.

Prestige can obstruct inquiry without every person lending it being part of a common plan. A regulator faced with reputable advisers may demand less verification. An adviser may accept assurances from a client. A client may arrange information so that separate people each see an innocuous part. The remedy is to establish who bore the risk, who controlled the shares, and what assurances were actually checked.

## A case made too small

BCCI's drug-money business was exposed through Operation C-Chase, an undercover Customs investigation involving agent Robert Mazur. The resulting 1988 case led to a January 1990 plea agreement under which the bank paid $14 million in forfeiture and undertook to cooperate. BCCI remained open.

The Senate's [law-enforcement chapter](https://irp.fas.org/congress/1992_rpt/bcci/08just.htm) explains why prosecutors accepted the agreement and why the investigators later criticized it. Tampa prosecutors weighed the charges they believed they could prove, a possible adverse ruling, and the value of a corporate plea and cooperation. The Senate argued that the approach sacrificed the chance to develop the wider case, including the concealed ownership of First American.

The negotiated resolution became part of the problem. Treating the bank as a cooperating institution could preserve access to other suspects while reducing pressure to investigate its management and wider business.

The choice also affected incentives for employees. If the corporation settled and lower-level officers remained exposed, investigators could lose an opportunity to obtain information in return for cooperation against the organization. A quick and defensible result in one case could narrow the route to a larger one.

The report was especially critical of efforts by Justice officials to help keep BCCI open after the plea. It traced those efforts to requests by the bank's lawyers, who were former federal prosecutors. The former prosecutors had access to serving officials who could make the bank’s continued operation easier.

An official can sincerely believe that preserving a suspect institution will help catch other criminals. The practical question is what limits that accommodation: which investigators retain access, what further offenses remain open to inquiry, and who is responsible if cooperation becomes an excuse for leaving the institution intact. The bank benefits immediately from continued operation. Any promised public benefit lies in investigations that may or may not follow.

## Information that did not arrive where it mattered

The intelligence record creates another route into the scandal. The CIA acknowledged using BCCI for intelligence gathering and using First American for banking purposes. Acting director Richard Kerr described the BCCI activity as limited and routine. The Senate report also challenged gaps in the agency's account and the difficulty of obtaining its records.

The more troubling example concerns knowledge of ownership. The report's [intelligence chapter](https://irp.fas.org/congress/1992_rpt/bcci/11intel.htm) describes information supplied by the CIA to Treasury and the Office of the Comptroller of the Currency in 1985 about BCCI's concealed control of First American. The original memorandum could not be found; the agency reconstructed its contents from underlying information. The Senate found no adequate explanation for the failure to alert the Federal Reserve, the authority responsible for First American's supervision.

Producing intelligence is not the same as getting a regulator to act on it. A report can be classified, sent to an official whose responsibilities lie elsewhere, or treated as someone else's problem. A recipient can remember a briefing without remembering its crucial point. Each office may describe its own handling as sufficient while the fact fails to reach the place where it could change a decision.

The responsibility lies along the route the warning was supposed to follow. Transmission, receipt, and a decision to act are different events. Without someone checking that the warning reached the relevant regulator, an office could mark its own task complete while the concealed ownership continued.

BCCI also served intermediaries involved in the arms transactions of [[iran-contra]]. The Senate described financing and accounts associated with Adnan Khashoggi and Manucher Ghorbanifar. Those relationships are part of the record of covert finance. They should not be expanded into the proposition that BCCI controlled every account, supplied every dollar, or financed every related war.

The label *intelligence bank* can obscure this range. A bank can be an intelligence target, a place where an agency holds an account, a provider of credit to an intermediary, or an institution knowingly carrying out an operation. Those relationships have different implications for its management and for the agency using it. Establishing one helps formulate the next question; it does not answer it.

## The audit that finally forced action

Auditors faced the same difficulty of assembling the whole, along with the obligation to question what they were shown. The Senate's [account of BCCI's accountants](https://irp.fas.org/congress/1992_rpt/bcci/10accounta.htm) records opposing explanations. Price Waterhouse described deception and collusion that defeated its audit work. BCCI financial officer Masihur Rahman argued that the auditors had access to underlying records and should have recognized the problems much earlier.

The report attributed significance to warnings that persisted while annual accounts continued to receive approval. It also recognized that the auditors' later work helped force the bank's closure. The June 1991 report known as Sandstorm brought the fraud to a point where continued operation could no longer be defended. BCCI was shut down on July 5, 1991.

A recommendation to correct a deficiency allowed the business to continue. A finding that the accounts could not be trusted made continuation harder to justify. During the years between those judgments, new deposits could still arrive.

The British inquiry led by Thomas Bingham examined supervision. In the [Commons debate following its publication](https://api.parliament.uk/historic-hansard/commons/1992/nov/06/bcci-bingham-report), ministers accepted serious criticisms while defending decisions intended to protect depositors. Other members pressed the point that earlier insistence on an intelligible group structure could have exposed or limited the fraud. They were disputing identifiable choices about authorization, supervision, restructuring, and closure.

Waiting can look prudent because immediate closure produces visible losses. The damage from continued operation is less visible until later. A regulator must weigh both, but cannot let the difficulty of closing a bank become a reason to accept accounts it cannot understand. A credible demand for structural change needs a consequence if the change does not happen.

## Nugan Hand's different record

Nugan Hand's collapse began a decade before BCCI's closure. The group had been founded in Sydney in 1973 by Frank Nugan and Michael Hand. Nugan's death in January 1980 was followed by investigations, the collapse of the business, and Hand's departure from Australia. American military and intelligence connections helped make its history a persistent subject of suspicion.

In March 1983, the Australian government [expanded Justice Donald Stewart's inquiry](https://pmtranscripts.pmc.gov.au/release/transcript-6069) to cover the group's activities, including alleged drug and arms offenses, exchange-control breaches, and interference with investigations. The terms were broad enough to examine more than the mere presence of former intelligence personnel.

The commission’s referral of exchange transactions to the Reserve Bank survives among [Australian government papers](https://archive.org/details/Nugan-Hand). It describes the group’s *Yorkville contra* facility. A client deposited money with a group company in one country and obtained the right to an equivalent sum from another company elsewhere. In the transactions described, one country was Australia, and the arrangement could work without transmitting the client's money across the border.

Money could become available abroad without the original cash physically leaving Australia. The group instead adjusted obligations between its companies. Here, the contra was the offset between accounts. Searching only for an international transfer would therefore miss the arrangement that gave the client access to overseas funds.

One example in the accompanying schedule follows A$100,000 handed over in cash in Australia in 1977. The group issued an offshore certificate of deposit, and a 1979 instruction directed principal and interest to Hawaii. The cash deposit and overseas payment were different events connected through the group’s books.

The commission compared several sets of records and reconciled exchange-rate differences before totaling transactions. A different dollar figure did not necessarily mean a different payment.

This is a financial mechanism that can be described and examined. It does not require a hidden intelligence purpose to matter. Exchange controls and tax obligations could be avoided through the group's arrangements, while outsiders confronted records that separated the cash deposit, the liability, and the later payment.

## What the Australian inquiry concluded

Special Minister of State Michael Young presented the final report to parliament on November 27, 1985. His [statement, reproduced in the preserved papers](https://archive.org/stream/Nugan-Hand/Inquiry%20into%20the%20Activities%20of%20the%20Nugan%20Hand%20Group%20Part%202_djvu.txt), described companies sustained by false accounts and circular transactions. It acknowledged that destroyed or concealed documents, Nugan's death, and the inability to obtain Hand's evidence had hampered the work.

The account also rejected important allegations. Young said the inquiry had found no objective evidence that the CIA used the group or was used by it. It had not established that the Chiang Mai office was created to attract deposits from Golden Triangle drug producers. Those conclusions differ sharply from the common description of the commission as having confirmed an agency-run heroin laundry.

The government did acknowledge financial services to people connected with drugs, including a transfer collected by a member of the Mr Asia syndicate. According to Young, the inquiry did not establish that those handling it knew it would purchase narcotics. A suspicious customer, a financial service, knowledge of its intended use, and operational direction were separate findings.

That distinction leaves legitimate grounds for criticism. A business willing to accept funds without asking their origin can make itself useful to criminals. Its officers cannot establish that the business was responsible merely by insisting they did not know what the customer would do next. But an account of the inquiry must retain what it actually found about their knowledge and the proposed CIA relationship.

The absence of Hand remained a serious limitation. In 2015, Peter Butt [reported finding him in Idaho under the surname Fuller](https://www.propublica.org/article/after-disappearing-from-australia-a-cia-linked-fugitive-found-in-idaho). Locating a missing participant was a significant development; it did not itself provide his explanation of the bank's transactions.

The surviving record supports a comparison with BCCI in the use of reputation, misleading financial appearances, and complicated company relationships. It supports different conclusions about demonstrated intelligence use. Combining the banks into a single organization, or treating the earlier collapse as a designed prototype for the later one, would require a history of people, transfers, and decisions that the resemblance alone cannot supply.

## The people waiting for their money

BCCI's closure did not end its financial history. Recovering assets, pursuing claims, and distributing funds continued for years. In [December 2011, a British minister told parliament](https://hansard.parliament.uk/Commons/2011-12-08/debates/11120860000022/BankOfCreditAndCommerceInternational) that creditors had received more than 85 percent of what they were owed at the beginning of liquidation, with another distribution expected. The statement is incompatible with a claim that every depositor permanently lost everything.

It is also a poor reason to minimize the harm. A substantial repayment after years of waiting does not restore the use of savings during that interval. A business may have needed the money for payroll, a family for a house, or a saver for retirement. Recovering a nominal amount and recovering the life that money was meant to support are different outcomes.

The two bank failures expose ways to conceal responsibility through the ordinary materials of finance: shares, loans, company boundaries, accounting entries, and professional assurances. BCCI's intelligence customers belonged within that history, alongside management fraud and decisions by prosecutors, auditors, and regulators. At Nugan Hand, the covert-operation allegations remained disputed while the financial arrangements supplied their own concrete evidence of abuse.

The bank's name is the beginning of the inquiry. The obligation behind an account, the person controlling an apparent owner, and the official who receives a warning are where responsibility becomes visible. The records were difficult to assemble, but they were not all beyond recovery. People paid a high price while the institutions responsible for assembling them delayed.
