Porter Garnett's 1908 account of the Bohemian Club's forest entertainments includes a practical problem. Each play had to supply a defeated figure called Care, whose body could then be burned. Some members thought the requirement cramped the writers. Others considered it a challenge to their ingenuity.

The writers were negotiating a tradition they had inherited. A ceremony often presented as the disclosure of a hidden religion was also a production that people wrote, revised, scored and lit. Garnett describes electrical effects and musicians, disagreements about artistic form and an audience joining a procession after a play. Members were making their own entertainment.

People who control resources can enjoy a performance and become more familiar with one another in the process. The question is what those relationships make possible when the holiday ends. Bohemian Grove is worth examining as a setting for that process, with its own rules about who is welcome, who serves and what the people present are permitted to ask of one another.

## The people making the spectacle

The Bohemian Club began in San Francisco in 1872. Its outdoor entertainments developed into the retreat in the redwoods near Monte Rio. Art and performance were important to the institution's identity. The later presence of prominent business and political figures did not eliminate the need for people who could write music, make scenery, perform and organize a show.

Garnett wrote with the club's permission and admired what it produced. His book therefore needs to be read as an appreciative account, rather than an independent investigation of its power. It describes how members created the effects they admired.

He describes lighting stations placed behind trees, with colored fire timed to the music. The hillside's final illumination was a result that people had worked to improve. The forest supplied a setting; technical skill made it appear to participate in the drama. An audience might experience wonder without knowing precisely how an effect had been accomplished.

A film can show the robes, darkness, music and burning figure while leaving the production and its purpose out of view. The image records the appearance of the performance. It cannot, by itself, identify every participant's beliefs or turn the figure being burned into a real victim.

Garnett dates the first Cremation of Care to 1881. In the period he describes, it followed the main play, with a procession and a funeral pyre elsewhere in the grounds. He notes changes in the ceremony's literary, musical and scenic treatment in 1906 and 1907. The writers and musicians had room to alter what later audiences would experience as tradition.

The 1906 production gives the symbolism a particular setting. Garnett reports that the San Francisco disaster had destroyed the clubhouse and scattered members. Charles K. Field wrote *The Owl and Care* for an encampment held three months later. Three Bohemians arrive from a ruined city seeking rest; Care appears as a monster declaring himself disease and death. The action moves between a dead tree and a living one before a figure called Love helps light the final pyre.

The ruined city belonged to the audience’s recent experience. The opposition between dead and living trees gave grief and recovery a visible form. In this production, relief was set against losses the club and its members had recently suffered.

Programs, scores and accounts of production preserve those choices. They let us follow an institution’s traditions through the people who made and changed them.

## Which kind of care is being burned?

The figure called Care personifies burdens that the gathering promises to suspend. That is different from care in the sense of concern for another person's welfare. Treating the two meanings as identical makes a performance about escape from anxiety into an explicit pledge to abandon conscience.

In the later ceremony described by the sociologist G. William Domhoff, the promised escape has a limit. Care interrupts the celebration to remind the men that he will be waiting when they return to the marketplace. The priest acknowledges the reprieve is temporary. Fellowship, represented by a lamp's flame, supplies the means to complete the burning.

The men stage a period of relief together, knowing that Care will return. Even if some take the occasion more seriously than others, that story is intelligible without a doctrine of human sacrifice.

Relief from work is not equally available to everyone. The ability to leave ordinary work, enter a protected setting and spend time with useful acquaintances is itself an advantage. A theatrical assertion of fellowship does not distribute that advantage beyond the people invited.

An owl-shaped structure cannot establish which deity, if any, its audience worships. Questions about access and relationships remain after that supposed proof is set aside. A comparison with an ancient ritual would require a history of transmission and evidence of intended meaning. Similar-looking fire cannot supply those on its own.

## Friendship and agreement are different results

Domhoff studied the club through overlapping membership lists, historical records, interviews and observation. He wanted to understand whether wealthy people in different sectors formed an actual social circle. His answer emphasized social cohesion: familiarity and trust that could make later cooperation easier. He explicitly distinguished that argument from the claim that the Grove secretly decides policy.

His methods help show what the proposed social circle consisted of. Finding that members share clubs or corporate connections establishes opportunities for contact. Observing repeated social interaction adds something about how those opportunities are used. Neither observation alone shows that they agree about a disputed policy, still less that a public official has accepted instructions from the group.

Imagine two businessmen competing over a government decision. Knowing each other socially could make discussion less hostile. It could also make their rivalry more personal. A retreat does not remove the interests that bring them into conflict. To establish a common policy, someone would need to follow the discussion into a proposal, an agreement and an attempt to secure it.

Trust can matter before that stage. An unfamiliar person has to persuade someone to listen. A familiar person may get the conversation without making the same effort. The benefit can be the chance to present a view, learn what worries another participant or ask for an introduction. Getting a hearing can be valuable before anyone agrees to help.

The club's motto, “Weaving spiders come not here,” offers members an image of refuge from business. An instruction to refrain from business does not end the effects of acquaintance. A man could refrain from soliciting a contract at dinner and still leave better acquainted with someone who influences contracts. A conversation need not contain a deal to have an effect on a later decision.

That is also why an explicit ban on business cannot prove that private relationships have no public consequences. The important question is what happened later. Did an acquaintance merely become a friend? Did he provide an introduction? Did he receive special treatment from someone acting for an institution whose resources were not his to give away?

## An audience a politician wanted

The Lakeside Talks provide a setting where the recreational and political purposes meet. Speakers address members and guests informally. Domhoff reproduces Richard Nixon's recollection of his 1967 address: Nixon considered it an important step in his return to presidential contention.

Nixon had an opportunity to appear before people whose opinion he valued. He could present ideas and demonstrate a manner of handling them. An informal audience could take his measure beyond the brief encounters of an ordinary campaign.

For a politician, a favorable impression could be valuable even if nobody made a binding commitment that afternoon. The result might be a later introduction, a contribution or a willingness to defend him in another conversation. Each would be a possible effect to investigate, rather than a result established by the appearance itself.

Nixon's account is also retrospective. A successful politician chooses episodes that help explain his success. Calling a speech a milestone does not tell us what would have happened without it, and it does not show that everyone present supported him. The recollection establishes the value he assigned to the occasion.

That value is enough to raise a question of unequal opportunity. A candidate invited into a setting where influential people will listen has an advantage over one who cannot enter. The existence of voters elsewhere does not cancel that advantage. Equally, the invitation does not dispose of those voters or make their eventual decision a formality.

## What the atomic-project meeting actually shows

There was a consequential atomic-project meeting at the property in September 1942. It is often retold as if Ernest Lawrence revealed the bomb project in a Lakeside Talk before ordinary members. The [official history by Richard Hewlett and Oscar Anderson](https://www.energy.gov/sites/prod/files/2013/08/f2/HewlettandAndersonNewWorldNoBookmarks.pdf) describes a different event: the S-1 Executive Committee's visit to inspect work at Berkeley and discuss progress.

The technical issue was whether an experimental method could be enlarged into production. Lawrence's electromagnetic process separated uranium isotopes—forms of the element with different atomic masses—using beams of ions, electrically charged atoms. Under controlled conditions, a magnetic field bent their paths differently according to mass, allowing the isotopes to be collected separately. The difficulty was not merely whether the physical principle worked. Multiple beams interfered with one another, and the material had to be collected in a useful form. An experiment that established separation was not yet a factory that could deliver enough material.

The history records the committee watching Berkeley experiments on September 13. At the Grove, Lawrence argued that experimental results justified a pilot plant. The committee recommended accelerating a five-tank pilot and part of a full-scale installation at the Tennessee site. Domhoff identifies the gathering as an off-season meeting of project planners, rather than a session of the ordinary encampment.

These details locate both the decision-makers and the decision. A committee already responsible for the work considered technical evidence and recommended accelerating development. The use of club property does not make the committee identical with the club. It also does not make the event irrelevant to the history of the site: a member's access could supply a place for a restricted meeting.

The meeting does not establish that a summer audience of bankers and entertainers received a Lakeside briefing on the bomb project. Nor does it establish that the entire project originated in the redwoods. Those claims erase the program, experiments and administrative responsibilities that made the meeting intelligible.

A particular place can host more than one kind of gathering. An official committee using private premises, a politician cultivating an audience and friends relaxing in a camp have different participants and purposes. Giving them all the same label makes the Grove appear to possess a single authority exercised through every event on its land.

## The people who were working

The picture of men temporarily putting aside rank has another limit: someone still has to prepare food, serve it and clean up. In the club’s [1986 employment case](https://law.justia.com/cases/california/court-of-appeal/3d/187/1.html), the court described a rule against employees fraternizing with members and guests. A worker could be present at the gathering without sharing the social access that made it valuable to an invitee.

For an employee, privacy could mean being excluded from the conversation. A private party can be a place of relaxation for one person and a workplace for another. The host's wish to forget ordinary responsibilities cannot determine the employee's rights. Indeed, the ease of the first person's experience may depend on the second person's discipline.

The California Court of Appeal considered the club's exclusion of women from employment under the Fair Employment and Housing Act. The club invoked association, privacy and an asserted occupational qualification. The court rejected the idea that being male was necessary for the jobs at issue. It noted both the absence of close member–employee association and the lack of a factual basis for equating sex with job performance.

The opinion specifically separated employment from the question of admitting women as members. It did not turn a right to work into an invitation to the club. That boundary prevents the legal result from being mistaken for a general opening of the encampment.

The advertised fellowship had a boundary. The ease of members together depended on maintaining a different relationship with the people serving them. Being on the same grounds did not mean belonging to the same circle. A rule could be informal among friends and exacting toward workers without any contradiction in the institution's daily operation.

The employer had to defend its practices outside its own terms. The relevant evidence concerned work, hiring and the actual relationship between people, rather than the symbolic meaning of the owl.

## What survives the holiday

The strongest reason to study Bohemian Grove is the unequal distribution of relationships that may matter elsewhere. It supplies a setting in which some people can become familiar, appear before an audience or offer hospitality. How those opportunities are used remains a question about particular people and decisions.

The invitation can have consequences for people who never receive one. A worker may have to accept the boundary between service and fellowship. A citizen may need to know whether an official returned a favor. Neither question is answered by a promise of recreation or a claim that every guest serves one hidden purpose.

Garnett's account restores the musicians, lighting arrangements and arguments about how to write a play. The atomic history restores a committee and a technical problem. The employment judgment restores people for whom the retreat was work. Those accounts describe the Grove more clearly than an interpretation that requires every activity to express the same concealed purpose.

The ceremony promises a temporary release from Care. It cannot promise the same release to everyone who makes the gathering possible, and it cannot settle what its guests owe to people outside it. The holiday ends. The friendships, invitations and obligations do not necessarily end with it.
