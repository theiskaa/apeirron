On 4 March 2001, *The Lone Gunmen* premiered with a story in which a passenger plane threatened to hit the World Trade Center. Six months later, two airliners struck the towers. A television story had put a recognizable part of a real catastrophe on screen before it happened.

To understand that resemblance, we have to ask how the writers arrived at the idea, whether anyone supplied it to them and what effect seeing it had on viewers. The theory called **predictive programming** attempts to connect them: someone who knows what is coming places it in fiction so that the public will be more willing to accept it.

That explanation draws some of its force from things that do happen. Governments work with entertainment producers. Fiction supplies people with ideas about the world. Institutions rehearse disasters through imagined scenarios. The difficulty is establishing the additional connection between a particular story, a planned event and the response of an audience.

## What the theory adds

In a transcript dated 23 November 2006, the broadcaster Alan Watt described dramatic fiction as a means of introducing ideas into people’s minds while they believed they were being entertained. He argued that familiarity could make a later development feel normal or inevitable. He also claimed that some producers received information about the direction in which society would be taken. His [account](https://www.cuttingthroughthematrix.com/transcripts/Alan_Watt_Blurb_PredictiveProgramming_PART1_Nov232006.html) states a deliberate process, rather than a resemblance discovered afterward.

The claim contains several steps. A sponsor knows about, or intends, a future development. That sponsor gets a relevant scenario into a production. An audience encounters it. The encounter changes how people respond when the development arrives. Evidence for the last step cannot establish the first, and evidence that someone influenced a script cannot establish what viewers later did.

Imagine a manufacturer paying to show a new device in a popular drama. The device appears useful and desirable, and the manufacturer hopes viewers will want it. That would be deliberate promotion through fiction. If the device later went on sale, the resemblance between screen and shop would have a documented explanation.

A claim that the same drama prepared people for a secretly planned disaster would require a different record. We would need to trace the relevant scenario to the people who knew of the plan and establish why it was placed there. The existence of product placement would not supply that record for us.

The theory becomes harder to assess when all the steps are compressed into the word *programming*. It can then mean showing an idea, making it memorable, persuading someone, arranging an event or revealing a hidden plan. An argument can move between those meanings without ever demonstrating the full process.

## The plane and the towers

The Television Academy’s 2 March 2026 interview with Vince Gilligan and Frank Spotnitz confirms the pilot’s March 2001 premiere and directly addresses the World Trade Center storyline. Spotnitz recalled that his first thought on seeing the attacks was the show; he feared that the attackers might have taken the idea from it. Gilligan described shock among people working at Fox that morning. The co-creators were looking back nearly twenty-five years after the premiere. [The interview](https://www.televisionacademy.com/features/online-originals/x-files-lone-gunmen-spinoff-vince-gilligan-frank-spotnitz) also shows that the resemblance has been discussed publicly.

Spotnitz initially feared that the influence might have gone from fiction into action. His fear does not establish that either. It shows how a resemblance can prompt several explanations even for someone who helped make the work.

The attacks themselves cannot be described by importing the rest of a fictional plot into history. The [9/11 Commission’s account](https://9-11commission.gov/report/911Report_Exec.htm) identifies nineteen hijackers taking over four flights as part of al Qaeda’s operation. A story about a plane approaching the towers is not independent evidence that government officials secretly arranged the actual attacks. That allegation would need evidence about the actual people and operation.

There were also reasons for the towers to appear in stories about danger. The Commission’s summary records the 1993 truck bombing of the World Trade Center. A writer imagining an attack on a prominent, already attacked landmark would not be selecting a building from an undifferentiated list of all buildings on earth. The historical setting helps explain why some scenarios are easier to imagine than others.

## How a collection of hits can mislead

When a montage begins with a real event and searches earlier films for matching images, the viewer sees the results of a selection process. Missing from the sequence may be the many scenes examined and discarded, the changes in the criteria during the search, and equally alarming stories that resemble nothing that followed.

Consider a deliberately simplified calculation. Suppose each comparison had a one percent chance of meeting an agreed definition of a match. Across one hundred independent comparisons, the chance of at least one match would be about 63 percent. That number is an illustration, not an estimate of how often films anticipate events. It shows why the number of opportunities matters alongside the apparent rarity of one hit.

Stories borrow from one another. Cities, weapons, diseases and political fears recur because writers inhabit the same world. Their themes are neither equally likely nor independent. A catalogue of repeated images can reveal a shared cultural concern without revealing an operation behind it.

The definition of a hit can also expand as the search proceeds. One scene supplies the location; another supplies a date; a third supplies a similar shape. A reversal, a number visible only after enlarging a frame or a very broad interpretation of a disaster may be accepted when a close narrative match is unavailable. Each added freedom creates more opportunities to find something that fits.

There is also a gap between a detail being present and an audience encountering it. A number discovered by enlarging a fleeting background image may never have entered an ordinary viewer’s account of the story. To argue that it prepared people for something, an investigator would need to establish what was perceptible and how it affected them. Calling it subconscious cannot replace that work. A clue intended for discovery after an event would serve a different purpose from a story intended to change attitudes before it.

A useful test would fix the rules before the outcome. Someone could record what a scene predicts, the period in which it is expected to happen and what would count as a miss. A catalogue would retain failures alongside successes. This would not settle whether a successful prediction came from intelligence, perceptive writing or chance, but it would establish more than a collection assembled after events were known.

Research, observation, genre conventions and a writer’s particular interests all shape which scenarios reach the screen. If specialists warn that a threat is plausible and a drama explores it, the warning can be a common source of both the fiction and later preparedness. No one needs to know which version of the threat will occur.

## The leverage that is documented

The U.S. Army openly describes its work with film and television producers. Its entertainment-assistance FAQ says staff can advise writers, review drafts and suggest changes. Productions seeking support submit scripts and resource requests; approved projects require an agreement. The stated criteria include benefits to the Defense Department or the national interest, public understanding of the armed forces and recruiting or retention. Supported productions are subject to official screening before release. [These are the Army’s own published procedures](https://www.army.mil/outreach/ocpa-west-faqs.html).

That arrangement gives the military leverage. Equipment, locations and personnel can make a production possible or change its cost. A producer who wants them has a reason to negotiate over the portrayal. The consequences could extend beyond a corrected uniform or an accurate technical term to the actions audiences see a military institution taking.

Imagine a production that can use an expensive location only if a disputed military sequence is changed. The producer accepts, and a version more acceptable to the supporting institution reaches the screen. A production agreement and successive drafts could establish that intervention. Interviews and correspondence might explain how important the support was to the decision.

The immediate concern would be representation: whose conduct was softened, whose account was omitted and whether the audience knew about the relationship. A documented change of this kind would give an investigator a decision to examine directly, whether or not the film depicted a future disaster.

The relevant agreement and drafts would concern that production. They would not identify the participants in a different film merely because it also depicted a military operation. The [[operation-mockingbird]] question about direct relationships with media has the same requirement: a general history cannot identify who intervened in a specific work.

## Familiarity is not agreement

Fiction can leave information behind. In *Learning Facts from Fiction*, a 2003 study by Elizabeth Marsh, Michelle Meade and Henry Roediger, participants read short stories and later answered general-knowledge questions. The authors report three experiments in which story information affected answers, including when that information was incorrect. Repeated reading increased the effect. The [authors’ abstract, reproduced by Washington University](https://profiles.wustl.edu/en/publications/learning-facts-from-fiction/), describes a route by which material encountered as fiction can enter a person’s account of the world.

That result is narrower than the claim that a disaster film makes an audience accept a later disaster or its political consequences. Recalling a supplied fact on a knowledge test, approving a policy and deciding not to resist it are different outcomes. The reported experiments concern knowledge-test answers, rather than either of those political outcomes.

A defender of the theory might say that viewers need not welcome the disaster. The film could instead present one response as inevitable: for example, making exceptional military power seem the only way to restore safety. That is a more specific account of persuasion, and one that deserves attention to the actual story. Does the work endorse that response, criticize it or leave its consequences unresolved? A catalogue of catastrophes alone does not answer.

Even familiarity could lead in different directions. Imagine a story in which an emergency power is abused. Someone who later recognizes a similar proposal might be reassured that the situation is familiar. Another viewer might recognize the danger more quickly and oppose it. The same remembered story could supply a warning rather than a reason to comply.

An event, the explanation offered for it and the response proposed afterward can meet with different reactions. A viewer might believe a catastrophe occurred, reject a government’s account of responsibility and oppose the policy adopted in its name. Treating these positions as one act of acceptance conceals the political judgment the theory is supposed to explain.

Evidence for a deliberate persuasion campaign would therefore include the intended response and evidence of what the audience actually did. A sponsor’s confidence is not a measurement of success. Nor does a viewer’s recollection of a scene demonstrate that the scene overrode their judgment.

An experiment on the hypothetical emergency-powers story could randomly assign participants to watch different versions of the same crisis. Researchers could measure support for the proposed powers before and after, while holding other features of the story as constant as possible. A difference would support an effect under those conditions. It would still leave open how long the effect lasted, whether it changed behavior and how a later real emergency—with new information and competing arguments—would alter it.

## Rehearsing a pandemic

On 18 October 2019, the Johns Hopkins Center for Health Security, the World Economic Forum and the Bill & Melinda Gates Foundation held a pandemic exercise. Its materials describe fictional news reports, briefings and discussions for participants. The published scenario concerned a novel coronavirus passing from bats to pigs to people, with an outbreak beginning in Brazil and spreading globally. The organizers constructed a problem for participants to consider through its consequences. [The exercise materials](https://centerforhealthsecurity.org/our-work/tabletop-exercises/event-201-pandemic-tabletop-exercise) set out its scenario and purpose.

After questions about its relationship to the outbreak in China, the Center stated on 24 January 2020 that the exercise had not been a prediction. It also said that the inputs used for its fictional virus were not similar to those of the newly identified virus. [That statement](https://centerforhealthsecurity.org/2020/statement-about-ncov-and-our-pandemic-exercise) describes the organizers’ position. A scenario explores consequences under chosen assumptions. A prediction claims something about what will actually happen.

A fire exercise can assume that an exit is blocked so participants must consider another route. The choice of a blocked exit does not show that someone intends to obstruct it during a real fire. Likewise, the resemblance between an exercise and a later emergency is not enough to establish that its organizers arranged the emergency.

An exercise can nevertheless advance an agenda. Which participants are invited, which constraints are treated as fixed and which responses are presented as sensible all influence what people rehearse. In Event 201’s published recommendations, the organizers called for coordinated public communication and for media companies to prioritize authoritative messages and suppress false ones. That is a concrete proposal about communication power, open to examination on its own terms.

The recommendation leaves important decisions to whoever defines authoritative information. An uncertain factual claim needs a way to be corrected, and a legitimate objection to policy needs room to be heard. The procedure for distinguishing an error from disagreement deserves public scrutiny. These concerns arise from the proposal itself; fictional outbreak parameters provide no evidence of a plan to cause COVID-19.

The exercise’s influence would also need to be traced. A recommendation, a subsequent policy and a decision to adopt that policy are different records. Similar wording might suggest a route to investigate; correspondence or an explicit citation could establish a more direct connection. Neither would by itself identify who caused a pandemic.

## What to do with a resemblance

An apparent prediction can be an opening for research. The first task is to recover the original work rather than rely on an edited montage: its release date, the complete scene and the parts that do not resemble the later event. Then comes production history. A technical adviser, a sponsor and an unrelated institution may all know about a plausible risk while having very different relationships to the script.

The claim should stay the same as the evidence is examined. If the claim is advance knowledge of a particular attack, proof that governments sometimes assist filmmakers will not complete it. If the claim is influence over a portrayal, a record of requested changes may provide a strong answer even though it establishes no foreknowledge. If the claim concerns public acquiescence, the audience’s response has to enter the account.

The theory is right to direct attention to entertainment’s political uses. Stories can carry sponsors’ interests and furnish people with expectations. A proposed explanation of a catastrophe has to follow the people and decisions further than that.

A screen image and a later event can look remarkably alike while the history connecting them remains unknown. A dated instruction to place a scenario in a script would tell us something different from a match found in a thousand hours of television. The distinction lets us examine influence without granting its supposed authors the power to write everyone else’s future.
