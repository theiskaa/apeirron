In its 2011 audit of Federal Reserve emergency assistance, the Government Accountability Office added up $16.1 trillion in loan transactions. The table became a source for claims that the Fed had given that sum to banks. Its heading contained a warning about how to read it.

The total adds loan transactions across several emergency programs. A bank renewing an overnight loan can appear in the sum day after day. The amount outstanding at one time was much smaller. That history shows its continued dependence on credit, while the amount at risk at any one time answers another question.

The Federal Reserve can create the settlement balances banks use, influence borrowing costs and provide emergency support on a scale unavailable to an ordinary lender. Its decisions affect people who neither work in finance nor chose its officials. The arguments over that power began long before the crisis, with disagreements about who should be trusted to exercise it.

## A private meeting, then a public argument

In November 1910, Senator Nelson Aldrich and a small group of financial specialists met privately at Jekyll Island, Georgia, to design a banking-reform proposal. They concealed their identities from staff and used the appearance of a hunting trip. The gathering supplied important ideas for the central-banking debate that followed.

It came after a crisis that had exposed the weakness of the existing arrangements. During the [Panic of 1907](https://www.federalreservehistory.org/essays/panic-of-1907), withdrawals from trust companies and distress in short-term lending threatened institutions involved in the payment system. J. P. Morgan helped organize support, but other banks, the clearinghouse and inflows of gold also mattered. Private coordination could supply assistance unevenly; there was no Federal Reserve offering a standing national framework.

The [Jekyll Island plan](https://www.federalreservehistory.org/essays/jekyll-island-conference) proposed a national reserve association with branches and substantial control for bankers. The secrecy let its authors work together before having to defend their preferences in public. It also gave critics a durable reason to question whose interests had shaped the proposal.

But the plan and the eventual law were different arrangements. Democrats opposed Aldrich’s design. Carter Glass initially favored regional institutions, while Woodrow Wilson insisted on a central board under public appointment. The [Federal Reserve Act signed in December 1913](https://www.federalreservehistory.org/essays/federal-reserve-act-signed) emerged from a struggle over the distribution of authority. Retaining financial ideas from the earlier proposal did not make the changes in governance imaginary.

The seven-member Board of Governors familiar today dates to the restructuring provided by the Banking Act of 1935. Congress has repeatedly amended the system’s organization and responsibilities.

The history also changes the meaning of “designed by bankers.” The participants could develop a proposal, organize support and argue from experience their opponents lacked. They still needed legislation granting authority, and the struggle over public appointments affected who would use it. Financial expertise and political authority entered the process at different stages.

The central bank’s powers came from legislation, while an influential early proposal reflected a narrow gathering’s preferences. Both facts help explain the continuing argument about its authority.

## Who gets to decide

The [Board of Governors](https://www.federalreserve.gov/aboutthefed/section10.htm) is a federal agency. Its seven positions are filled through presidential appointment and Senate confirmation, with staggered fourteen-year terms. The chair’s designation is a separate four-year appointment. Long terms are intended to separate monetary decisions from the immediate demands of an election cycle; they also make it harder for an electorate to change the institution’s course quickly.

The system also supervises financial institutions and provides payment and settlement services. Monetary policy is its most prominent function, but a Reserve Bank is more than a committee meeting about rates.

There are twelve regional Reserve Banks. Commercial banks that belong to the system must hold stock in their district’s Reserve Bank. This is the source of the familiar claim that private banks “own the Fed.” That description can make the regional stock sound like ownership of a company whose shareholders direct national policy.

Reserve Bank stock is not an ordinary share that an investor can buy, sell or accumulate to take over a company. [The statute prohibits its transfer or use as collateral](https://www.federalreserve.gov/aboutthefed/section5.htm). Dividends are governed by law. The often-repeated claim of a universal six-percent payment is also incomplete: [larger member banks face a statutory formula](https://www.federalreserve.gov/aboutthefed/section7.htm) that uses the lower of six percent and a specified Treasury-auction yield, with an inflation-adjusted asset threshold.

Banks nevertheless have a real role in regional governance. Each Reserve Bank has nine directors in three classes. Member banks elect three Class A directors to represent banks and three Class B directors to represent the public. The Board of Governors appoints three Class C public representatives. Class B directors cannot be bank officers, directors or employees; Class C directors face an additional restriction on bank shareholdings.

The Reserve Bank’s president is selected by the eligible Class B and C directors, subject to approval by the Board of Governors. The bank representatives in Class A do not make that appointment. The [rules](https://www.federalreserve.gov/aboutthefed/section4.htm) give banks a role while limiting which decisions their direct representatives can make. They leave room for a serious objection about whose experience is represented. A legally designated public representative is not the same thing as a representative directly elected by the public.

Monetary-policy decisions belong to another body, the [Federal Open Market Committee](https://www.federalreserve.gov/monetarypolicy/fomc.htm). It combines the seven governors with five Reserve Bank presidents: New York’s president has a permanent vote, and four other voting places rotate. All the other Reserve Bank presidents participate in discussions without voting at those meetings.

This gives the governors a majority of the twelve voting positions when every seat is filled. It also gives regional presidents a substantial role, including the permanent place for New York. An elector can influence appointments through the presidency and Senate, but regional selection and long terms put other decisions at a distance from an election.

Congress sets the objectives. [Section 2A](https://www.federalreserve.gov/aboutthefed/section2a.htm) names maximum employment, stable prices and moderate long-term interest rates. The first two are usually described as the dual mandate. The committee has discretion over how to pursue them; it does not require a new congressional vote before each rate decision.

## Two kinds of account balance

An account balance can mean more than one kind of obligation. The balance in a household’s bank account is a commercial bank’s liability to that household. A reserve balance is a bank’s asset held at the central bank. Both are monetary claims, but they sit on different balance sheets and serve different users.

Suppose a bank grants a business a $100,000 loan and credits its account. The bank acquires a $100,000 asset—the borrower’s promise to repay—and a matching liability, the new deposit it owes the customer. The business acquires a deposit and a debt. The bank has created spendable deposit money, but the customer has not acquired $100,000 of net wealth merely by borrowing it.

The [Bank of England’s 2014 explanation](https://www.bankofengland.co.uk/quarterly-bulletin/2014/q1/money-creation-in-the-modern-economy) describes this process and its constraints. It is an account of modern bank lending using the British system, not a statement that American and British banking law are identical. Lending creates deposits; it does not give each bank unlimited freedom to issue obligations it cannot honor.

If the business pays a supplier at another bank, the payment has to settle. The originating bank needs settlement resources and a viable way to fund its position. It also faces credit risk, capital and liquidity requirements, costs and competition. A promise to repay is valuable only to the extent that repayment can reasonably be expected.

The business spends a customer deposit; its bank settles through a different account. Banks can settle payments through balances at Reserve Banks. The Fed can create those balances when it lends or buys an eligible asset. A central-bank loan creates a reserve balance and an obligation to repay the central bank. Buying a security instead gives the Fed an asset it has purchased. Neither operation is identical to a household receiving an unconditional payment.

These distinctions are visible in the Fed’s [published balance sheet](https://www.federalreserve.gov/monetarypolicy/bst_frliabilities.htm). Currency and reserve balances are liabilities of the central bank. Securities and loans are assets. The balance sheet’s size records the scale of those operations. The assets and their terms show more about the risks accepted and the institutions supported.

## How a committee influences borrowing

The Fed does not announce the interest rate on every mortgage or instruct each bank how many loans to make. Its principal rate decision concerns a target range for the federal funds rate, an overnight money-market rate. It then uses tools to encourage market rates to trade near that range.

Under the ample-reserves approach, the important tools include interest paid on reserve balances and the rate available through overnight reverse repurchase transactions. The [Fed’s implementation explanation](https://www.federalreserve.gov/econres/notes/feds-notes/implementing-monetary-policy-in-an-ample-reserves-regime-the-basics-note-1-of-3-20200701.html) describes the logic: an institution able to earn a particular return with the central bank has less reason to accept a worse comparable return elsewhere. Different institutions have access to different facilities, so one administered rate alone does not put a firm floor under every overnight transaction.

Lenders and investors carry the change into other markets by comparing alternatives. A borrower considering a project faces the price a bank offers; the bank considers its funding costs, credit risk and other uses for its balance sheet. Changing short-term conditions works through those comparisons, expectations and spending decisions. A firm’s response depends on whether its project still looks worthwhile at the price of credit available to it.

Some borrowers feel the change sooner than others. For a borrower with an existing fixed-rate loan, a change in the Fed’s target does not rewrite the contracted rate. A person seeking a new loan or refinancing may face different conditions, while a saver may receive a different return. The consequences depend on the contracts and positions people already hold.

The [Fed’s account of monetary policy](https://www.federalreserve.gov/aboutthefed/fedexplained/monetary-policy.htm) describes easing as a means to support spending and employment and tightening as a means to restrain inflationary pressure. Neither has a painless setting that serves every interest. Slower spending can help reduce price pressure while making a firm less willing to hire. Easier credit can support an investment while also encouraging borrowing that later proves unsustainable.

The objectives still leave the committee with a judgment to make. An inflation target does not tell a committee exactly how long to tolerate weakness in employment, how persistent a supply disruption will be or how quickly a previous decision will take effect. Discretion enters where the statutory objectives meet an uncertain economy.

## Buying a bond is a particular transaction

Large-scale asset purchases, commonly called quantitative easing, extend the explanation beyond an overnight rate. The Fed buys securities with newly created reserves. When the seller is a nonbank institution, the payment passes through its bank: the bank receives reserves, and the seller receives a deposit in exchange for the security.

A [Fed research note on pandemic deposit growth](https://www.federalreserve.gov/econres/notes/feds-notes/understanding-bank-deposit-growth-during-the-covid-19-pandemic-20220603.html) explains why reserves and customer deposits should not be treated as the same total. Purchases from nonbanks can increase both. A purchase from a bank can instead change the composition of the bank’s assets. Loan activity, government transfers and decisions about saving also affected deposits over the period. Their combined effects did not reduce to one fixed ratio between deposits and reserves.

The seller has exchanged one asset for another. That transaction can affect prices and incentives even though it is not a gift equal to the security’s purchase price. By purchasing longer-term securities, the Fed aims to influence broader financial conditions, including longer-term borrowing costs. [Its policy explanation](https://www.federalreserve.gov/faqs/about_12844.htm) identifies Treasury and federally issued or guaranteed securities among the assets used in these purchases.

The next effects are uneven. A rise in asset prices helps existing holders differently from people trying to buy those assets. A fall in borrowing costs affects a debt-free saver differently from a business refinancing an obligation. Employment gains would reach a different set of people again. Assessing the policy requires following those different paths. The purchase price alone cannot measure how the gains and costs were distributed.

The acquired assets bring income, and the liabilities can bring costs. Reserve Banks incur expenses, pay statutory dividends and transfer excess earnings to the Treasury under the governing rules. Their 2025 [combined financial statements](https://www.federalreserve.gov/aboutthefed/files/combinedfinstmt2025.pdf) report a net loss before Treasury remittances and explain the deferred-asset treatment when costs exceed earnings. Future net earnings are needed to clear that accumulated shortfall before affected Reserve Banks resume remittances.

Those entries connect monetary operations to public finances. The interest paid on liabilities and the income received from assets need not move together. A central bank has powers an ordinary company lacks, but its transactions still have financial consequences worth showing in the accounts.

## The crisis figure, read with its heading

The $16.1 trillion total appears in table 8 of [GAO’s 2011 emergency-assistance report](https://www.gao.gov/assets/gao-11-696.pdf). Its heading says the transaction amounts are not adjusted for loan term. The report covers programs with different lending periods and lists large American and foreign financial groups. Repeated borrowing is part of what the table measures.

GAO later [explained the arithmetic](https://www.gao.gov/blog/tarp-turns-10-looking-back-government-assistance-banking-sector) with a hypothetical bank renewing a $100 million loan eleven times. The sum becomes $1.2 billion, while the bank has effectively borrowed $100 million for about a year. Its peak outstanding balance remains $100 million. The same transaction history answers different questions depending on which number is used.

The crisis programs’ loans outstanding peaked at more than $1 trillion in late 2008. The lending was a large commitment at a time when other funding had become scarce. Its terms, collateral and repayment record tell us more about that commitment than treating the sum of transactions as a permanent transfer.

Treasury’s TARP, the Fed’s lending and FDIC guarantees were also different interventions. They involved different powers and forms of assistance. Adding face amounts without regard to what each represented can produce an impressive total that is difficult to interpret.

A simplified run shows what emergency lending can do. Suppose an institution owns loans that will repay over several years while many of its creditors demand cash today. Selling the loans immediately may bring a distressed price and force losses. A temporary lender can bridge the mismatch, allowing payments to be made while the assets continue to earn income.

That situation differs from one in which the loans are worth too little ever to cover the institution’s obligations. More short-term borrowing would postpone recognition of a loss. In a crisis, deciding which situation applies is difficult: the prospective repayment depends partly on whether the panic continues. The lender’s judgment about assets and collateral therefore matters alongside the amount lent.

Even a sound loan on protective terms can confer a valuable advantage. A temporary lender offers time when competitors or other creditors are unwilling to provide it. The recipient may repay every dollar and still have benefited greatly from access. The public’s return and the recipient’s advantage need to be examined separately.

A firm excluded from comparable support faces another set of choices. Repayment would not, by itself, settle whether the eligibility rules were fair, whether the price reflected the public risk or whether creditors received protection others were denied.

GAO’s [published findings](https://www.gao.gov/products/gao-11-696) identified weaknesses in conflict policies, procurement guidance and documentation. Many crisis contracts were awarded without competition under emergency conditions. A bank responding urgently may need expertise already available, but that rationale does not explain how long an exceptional arrangement should last or when it should be opened to competition.

The report also describes JPMorgan Chase as both borrower and vendor while its chief executive served as a New York Fed director. GAO did not find evidence of systemic favoritism in the program requirements it reviewed, and it did not identify a vendor conflict that compromised policy goals. Those findings coexist with its recommendations for stronger safeguards.

The market relationships create a further difficulty. A central bank needs information from the markets it is trying to stabilize, and it may hire firms with the ability to manage complicated assets. Those relationships can also leave outsiders wondering whether public power is being exercised on terms insiders helped define. Independence from daily political direction does not resolve a conflict of interest.

Congress changed the emergency framework in 2010. Under [section 13(3), as amended](https://www.federalreserve.gov/aboutthefed/section13.htm), programs require broad-based eligibility and prior approval from the Treasury Secretary. A facility structured to remove assets from one specific company or help it avoid insolvency proceedings does not meet that eligibility standard. The statute also calls for procedures excluding insolvent borrowers, protection against losses and reports to congressional committees.

These restrictions changed the authorization for future interventions. They do not answer every dispute about how a program’s eligibility or collateral terms are chosen. Applying them requires knowing who was eligible, how collateral was valued and what congressional committees were told.

## Audited, independent and still answerable

The Fed’s accounts are audited. [Section 11B of the Federal Reserve Act](https://www.federalreserve.gov/aboutthefed/section11b.htm) requires independent annual financial-statement audits. The [Board’s audit explanation](https://www.federalreserve.gov/faqs/about_12784.htm) also describes Inspector General work, GAO reviews and publication of financial statements and the weekly balance sheet.

Recording a transaction correctly and choosing a sound policy are separate achievements. [The statutory GAO framework](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USCODE-2024-title31/pdf/USCODE-2024-title31-subtitleI-chap7-subchapII-sec714.pdf) excludes specified monetary-policy matters from ordinary review while providing additional authority to examine certain assistance and credit facilities. “Audit the Fed” can therefore mean expanding a particular oversight power. It should not imply that no one has ever inspected the accounts.

Deliberation is private, but decisions do not remain wholly secret. The FOMC issues statements, publishes minutes after about three weeks and releases transcripts with about a five-year delay. Its [description of those records](https://www.federalreserve.gov/faqs/minutes-federal-open-market-committee-fomc.htm) distinguishes the summary available soon after a meeting from the later detailed proceedings.

Whether that is enough transparency is open to criticism. A record arriving years later can inform history without enabling a contemporary challenge. A statement released immediately gives a decision and its stated rationale; it does not show every discarded forecast or disagreement. The later record lets readers compare the public explanation with the alternatives actually discussed. That comparison is part of accountability, especially when the decision’s effects have proved different from what was expected. Immediate disclosure can change the incentives of people discussing uncertain options. The choice is about the timing and scope of scrutiny, rather than an absolute choice between public knowledge and total secrecy.

Operational independence involves another choice about when political control should enter. Giving an elected government direct control of each rate decision would make the line of electoral responsibility easier to trace. It could also let a government postpone restraint for short-term political advantage. Insulating decisions reduces that temptation while placing more weight on appointments, the mandate, explanation and oversight.

A public account of a rescue should show which institutions were protected, why the terms were chosen and what alternatives were considered. An account of monetary policy should explain expected effects on employment and prices, the uncertainty in those expectations and the consequences borne by different groups. Those obligations remain even when the transaction is lawful, the loan is repaid and the audit finds that the numbers were recorded correctly.
