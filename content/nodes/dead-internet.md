A page can be busy without anyone talking. A search crawler retrieves its links, a scraper copies its text, and an automated program tries passwords against its login form. Those requests may outnumber the visits of people who stop to read. Yet the comments underneath could still be a conversation between people.

Now imagine the reverse: a quiet page with a dozen comments, all posted by one operator using different accounts. Its traffic looks modest. Its apparent agreement is manufactured. These are hypothetical cases, but the difference between them matters whenever someone says that most of the internet is now fake.

The Dead Internet Theory gathers several suspicions into one explanation. Much of what seems to be human activity, it proposes, is generated or staged; the remaining people encounter an environment arranged to influence them. Its strongest versions describe a coordinated replacement of ordinary online life. More loosely, the name expresses a recognizable frustration with repetitive posts, implausible engagement and pages that seem written only to occupy a search result.

The difficulty is deciding what each observation establishes. Automated traffic, a deceptive identity, generated prose and a manipulated recommendation are different ways of making an online encounter less than it appears. An account of the internet has to keep them distinguishable long enough to explain who is doing what.

## How a feeling became a theory

In August 2021, journalist Kaitlyn Tiffany traced the theory to a January post on Agora Road’s Macintosh Cafe by a user called IlluminatiPirate. The post placed the internet’s supposed death around 2016–17 and alleged organized manipulation. Tiffany contacted the poster, who affirmed the belief. Her reporting documents a particular formulation and its circulation, rather than the first occasion on which anyone distrusted online activity. ([The Atlantic](https://www.theatlantic.com/technology/archive/2021/08/dead-internet-theory-wrong-but-feels-true/619937/))

The word *dead* does considerable work. It converts differences between earlier and later online experiences into an event: something once alive has been replaced. That can make a scattered set of disappointments feel intelligible. A forum loses its regulars; search results grow less useful; unrelated accounts repeat the same phrase. Under the theory, these cease to be separate problems with potentially different causes.

Online, a reader usually encounters the finished appearance of an exchange. A profile picture stands in for a person; a displayed number stands in for an audience. The labor, ownership and software behind either may be hard to inspect. A theory promising to explain that hidden machinery offers something more satisfying than another afternoon spent wondering whether a reply was sincere.

It also creates an evidential trap. Once absence of convincing human contact becomes evidence of replacement, any unpleasant encounter can confirm the theory. A formulaic response looks automated, an emotional one engineered to provoke. Even disagreement can be dismissed as planted. A useful investigation must allow the possibility that some encounters are ordinary, including encounters with people who are tedious or wrong.

## What the traffic figures count

The 2026 Thales *Bad Bot Report* puts automated activity at about 53 percent of the web traffic observed in 2025. Its categories include benign automation as well as malicious bots. The report combines work by the company’s threat researchers and security analysts; its traffic analysis concerns access to applications and services. It is valuable evidence about activity visible to that security provider. It does not count the proportion of public conversations conducted by machines. ([Report, pp. 3–4, 7](https://irp.cdn-website.com/711a67a7/files/uploaded/2026-Thales-Bad-Bot-Report.pdf))

Consider an imaginary local-history site. Ten volunteers write its articles over several years. In one afternoon, a scraper retrieves the entire archive thousands of times. Most requests that day could be automated, while every article remains human-written. The extra traffic may impose serious costs on the site. Describing those costs requires no claim that the volunteers have disappeared.

A different count would be needed for each further question. To estimate synthetic articles, investigators would need a defined collection of articles and a defensible way of establishing their production. To estimate deceptive accounts, they would need criteria for deception and access to relevant account evidence. To estimate what people actually encounter, they would need exposure data. An item can exist on a server without anyone reading it.

Even a falling human share of requests need not mean fewer human readers in absolute terms. Automated activity could grow faster while human use also grows. The changing share alone cannot show that human use declined.

Sampling matters too. The traffic reaching a provider’s customers can reflect their businesses, locations and security arrangements. A login service under attack and a small invitation-only discussion group present different measurement problems. Neither the size of a vendor’s dataset nor the precision of a percentage makes it a random census of all communication.

None of this makes automation inconsequential. A site that spends its resources serving unwanted requests may become harder to operate. A writer may lose readers if automated summaries satisfy their searches elsewhere. These are questions about infrastructure and distribution, with consequences for the people who still produce material. They can be investigated directly instead of being compressed into a claim that a majority of online voices are fictitious.

## A real operation, and a misleading audience

There are documented attempts to manufacture an apparent public. In August 2023, Meta described its removal of a network associated with the influence operation it called Spamouflage: 7,704 Facebook accounts, 954 Pages, 15 Groups and 15 Instagram accounts. The company linked the operation to individuals associated with Chinese law enforcement. Its account describes operators working from different locations and apparently receiving central directions. ([Meta announcement](https://about.fb.com/news/2023/08/raising-online-defenses/); [Q2 report, pp. 11–14](https://regmedia.co.uk/2023/08/29/meta_quarterly_adversarial_threat_report_q2_2023.pdf))

The report records repeated supposedly personal comments and occasional apparent serial numbers left in posted text. It also gives a reason to distrust the operation’s apparent audience: many followers were inauthentic, including those acquired through previously spammy Pages. Meta reported no evidence of substantial engagement from authentic communities on its own services. Its findings should be read as the platform’s assessment of the network it investigated, rather than an independent measurement of every effect across the internet.

Account creation, distribution, attention and belief are successive achievements. A campaign may be competent at one and ineffective at another. A large stock of profiles can make the first achievement look like all four. Even an unsuccessful persuasion campaign can burden moderators or give a later writer a bogus quotation to cite. Its consequences need to be followed through the particular route by which someone encounters and uses its material.

The coordination described in this case does not require each participant to be a bot. A human operator can run false identities, copy assigned text and follow a posting schedule. Calling the whole operation automated may obscure the organization and labor that make it possible. Conversely, a clearly identified automated account can perform a legitimate task without pretending to be a neighbor.

The historical concerns behind [[operation-mockingbird]] and [[manufacturing-consent]] remain relevant: who can arrange the information available to an audience, and with what accountability? But a documented network does not authenticate every claim about covert participation in a separate controversy. A connection to an agency, a particular account or a particular death requires evidence specific to that claim. General knowledge that influence operations exist cannot supply a missing chain of attribution.

## Authorship is only part of the encounter

Generated prose adds a further difficulty. It allows an operator to produce variations on a message without writing each sentence. That can make a campaign cheaper to expand, while leaving other costs intact: obtaining distribution, maintaining accounts, avoiding enforcement and giving anyone a reason to pay attention. The existence of a generator does not establish success at those tasks.

A text can also have mixed production. Someone may commission, edit and publish generated material under an accountable name. Someone else may write every word of a fabricated testimonial. The first text has machine assistance; the second misrepresents an experience. For a reader deciding whether to rely on either, the publisher’s responsibility and access to the claimed facts matter alongside the method used to form sentences.

Surface style is a poor basis for assigning a hidden operator. Humans imitate each other, repeat slogans and write to templates. An awkward reply may come from a person using a second language. A convincing one may come from someone managing a false persona. A judgment about a disputed account needs more than the feeling that its prose resembles other prose.

That uncertainty does not require settling [[consciousness]]. A reader checking a claimed eyewitness account needs to know whether anyone witnessed the event and whether the publisher can substantiate it. Philosophical disagreement about what a machine could experience does not answer those practical questions. Nor does discovering a human author establish that the account is honest.

What readers see also depends on selection. A recommendation system might present genuine posts that give a distorted impression of a community. Replacing all their authors with machines is unnecessary. If anger repeatedly attracts attention, an environment arranged around that attention can make a varied public appear relentlessly hostile.

That last example is a possible mechanism, not a finding about every feed. To establish it for a service would require evidence about selection and exposure. An investigation of ranking needs to establish how material was selected. An archive of posts may show what was available while leaving unanswered what any particular person was shown.

## When generated material becomes training material

One concern goes beyond deceptive accounts. If models learn from online writing, and online writing increasingly includes model outputs, could later models learn from distortions introduced by earlier ones?

Ilia Shumailov and colleagues investigated recursive learning in a controlled experiment. In the April 2024 version of their preprint, one language-model setup began with an OPT-125m model trained further on WikiText-2, a collection of Wikipedia text. That model produced material for a subsequent model to learn from, and the process continued across generations. Across five runs, the later models became worse at predicting held-out text from the original collection. The evaluation therefore asked whether they retained performance on that material, rather than whether a person found an occasional output convincing. ([Preprint, language-model experiments](https://arxiv.org/html/2305.17493v3))

Each generation learns from a selection produced by the previous one. Features poorly represented in that selection can become harder to recover later. Errors can accumulate when the process repeatedly treats its own outputs as the world to be learned. The authors call the resulting degeneration *model collapse*.

In one setting, later generations trained without any of the original WikiText-2 training examples. In another, the researchers retained a fresh random sample of ten percent of that original data for each generation and used more training passes. Performance deteriorated less in the second setting. Because the settings differed in more than what they kept, the experiment does not isolate the effect of retaining that exact share. It does show why a prediction about a deployed model needs to start with what that model actually learns from.

There is nevertheless a practical reason to care about originals. A document recording an actual measurement or event supplies information that a succession of restatements cannot create by repetition. If later summaries lose the date, qualification or unusual result, reading more of those summaries will not necessarily restore it. Preserving access to the underlying record helps readers as well as model builders.

## Finding someone answerable

The broadest version of the Dead Internet Theory can absorb almost any disappointing experience. Advertising, state influence, ordinary copying, hostile automation and recommendation systems can all produce unsatisfying encounters, but their coexistence does not establish a single authority directing them. In some cases there is a central operator, as Meta assessed in Spamouflage. In others, similar incentives could lead unrelated publishers toward similar material. Attribution has to be earned case by case.

Start with the claim at hand. An account reporting an event should explain how it knows what happened. A supposed public consensus calls for scrutiny of how participation was assembled. When a platform describes its success, an audience count leaves further questions about attention and persuasion. Each claim has a different route back to evidence.

Small communities offer a different kind of evidence: continuity. Participants can return to a disagreement, correct a detail, remember what a project required and accept responsibility for an error. Those practices do not make a group immune to deception. They give its members a history against which a new claim can be judged. A familiar avatar alone does not do that work.

This need not mean requiring a public legal identity from everyone. Someone sharing a vulnerable experience may have good reasons to use a pseudonym. A consistent record, a correction or a document that can be checked can support a claim without exposing every detail of the person behind it. Accountability has to fit the claim: a participant describing their own uncertainty and an institution selling a purported expert consensus owe their readers different explanations.

Deceptive participation makes it harder for readers to find people whose claims they can examine. Treating every encounter as already counterfeit would erase distinctions between a deceptive campaign and the conversations it tries to influence. What deserves protection is the ability to find a source, question the person or institution responsible, and test the answer against a shared record.
