In 1979, the General Accounting Office [described a Treasury investment channel](https://www.gao.gov/assets/emd-79-45.pdf) first offered to Saudi Arabia five years earlier. Its purpose, Treasury officials told the investigators, had been to attract and manage petrodollars. The channel allowed purchases of U.S. securities without entering the regular competitive auction.

The report put a deliberately created investment arrangement into the public record. What remained contentious was disclosure: the investigators objected to the limited information available about individual oil exporters’ holdings. A mechanism for attracting investment and a policy of withholding country-level figures were related matters, but they were not the same secret.

Oil revenue can connect several financial decisions. Oil can be quoted in dollars, paid for through dollar accounts and followed by investment in dollar assets. Each step encourages the next, but no accounting rule makes them inseparable. A producer receiving dollars can buy something else. An importer can obtain dollars without selling goods to America. A Treasury security is a particular investment, not the automatic destination of every oil payment.

Washington wanted the revenues to strengthen financial and political relationships favorable to the United States. The means included services for investors, diplomatic cooperation and the attraction of American assets. Those choices operated inside a dollar system that already extended well beyond oil.

## From the invoice to the investment

Suppose a refiner agrees to buy oil at a price stated in dollars. Dollars are the unit used to calculate its obligation. If the contract requires dollar payment, a refiner earning another currency can arrange an exchange to obtain it. It may buy the dollars, borrow them or use dollar revenue from other business.

The seller then has another decision. It could hold a dollar bank deposit, buy a dollar security, convert the proceeds to another currency or spend them on imports. If it purchases a U.S. Treasury bill, it holds a claim on the American government. If it buys machinery from a European producer, the revenue has followed another route.

| Decision | What it determines |
| --- | --- |
| Invoicing | The currency in which the price and amount due are stated |
| Payment | The accounts and arrangements used to discharge the obligation |
| Investment | Which assets the seller acquires with the proceeds |
| Official reserve management | Which assets a monetary authority chooses to hold |

The owner of the revenue matters too. A private oil company’s income is not automatically part of a central bank’s reserves. An exporting government can also have different institutions responsible for budget revenue, long-term investment and monetary reserves. “The country received dollars” is often too rough a description to tell us who owns a balance or what they will do with it.

Dollar invoicing still matters. When the contract also requires dollar payment, firms need to obtain or finance that payment and may keep working balances or arrange hedges. It also places exchange-rate risk somewhere in the transaction. But the need to make a payment next week does not dictate the entire portfolio an exporter will hold over the next decade.

Exchange-rate exposure can remain serious even where conversion is possible. In a simplified purchase, oil costing $100,000 would require 200,000 units of the buyer’s currency at two units per dollar. At two and a half units per dollar it would require 250,000, with no change in the dollar oil price. Access to an exchange market does not make that difference disappear. The timing of the conversion and the terms of any protection against exchange-rate changes become part of the business decision.

Currency traders were already attending to those separate decisions in the 1970s. In March 1975, the New York Fed’s [foreign-exchange review](https://www.newyorkfed.org/medialibrary/media/research/monthly_review/1975_pdf/03_1_75.pdf) reported that Saudi Arabia had requested future oil payments from the Aramco group exclusively in dollars. The review also described Saudi public assurances that it would continue investing in sterling assets. Dollar receipts and sterling investment could coexist.

That account concerned market reports and official assurances during a particular period. It did not describe a universal rule binding every oil exporter. The traders’ concern was where the revenues would go next. Requesting dollars from a buyer did not settle that question.

## The dollar had a history before 1974

Washington and Riyadh negotiated after the oil shock in a currency already used for international business. The Bretton Woods arrangements had already put it at the center of the postwar fixed-exchange-rate system. The United States promised official gold convertibility at a fixed price, while other countries managed exchange rates around the dollar.

The system came under pressure as foreign dollar claims grew relative to the gold available to honor conversion. On 15 August 1971, Nixon suspended that convertibility. The [Federal Reserve’s institutional history](https://www.federalreservehistory.org/essays/gold-convertibility-ends) follows the earlier attempts to defend the arrangement and its eventual breakdown.

The loss of gold convertibility left a great deal of dollar business in place: contracts, banking relationships, markets and obligations still due. A firm with a dollar loan still owed dollars. A bank outside the United States could also have dollar assets and liabilities; the currency denomination did not turn every such claim into a direct loan to the American government. A trader whose counterparties used a common currency still had a reason to use it. The later oil revenues entered an established network of accounts and contracts.

Calling the dollar “backed by nothing” mixes up the absence of gold redemption with the absence of economic uses. A currency can be wanted because people can spend it, settle debts with it or buy assets in it. Those reasons can weaken, but they do not require a promise to exchange each note for a commodity.

Nor did oil replace gold in the legal sense. A holder of dollars did not acquire a standing right to redeem them at the Treasury for a fixed quantity of Saudi crude. Oil pricing and payment practices helped generate uses for the currency. They did not recreate the conversion promise Nixon had suspended.

## What the cooperation documents actually say

American officials were actively trying to shape where the revenues and commercial opportunities would lead.

In a [6 March 1974 instruction](https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1969-76ve09p2/d104), Henry Kissinger urged the embassy to move toward joint commissions. He wanted to improve relations, offer an alternative to European bilateral arrangements and create a setting in which Washington could press for greater Saudi production and lower oil prices. Suggested topics included industrialization, investment, reserve management, energy projections and military cooperation.

The instruction also attended to appearances. Washington wanted to move quickly without seeming over-eager and to frame its approach as a considered response to Saudi initiatives.

An [April memorandum](https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1969-76ve09p2/d19) proposed two commissions, one for economic cooperation under Treasury leadership and another for security cooperation under Defense leadership. The memorandum recommended a division of responsibilities, with staff still preparing for discussions with the Saudis.

The Joint Commission on Economic Cooperation was established through a public joint statement on 8 June 1974. A technical-cooperation agreement followed in February 1975. [GAO’s 1979 review](https://www.gao.gov/assets/id-79-7.pdf) describes Saudi-funded projects involving American technical and advisory services. Saudi Arabia was to advance the money needed for agreed studies and services into a Treasury trust account.

Oil revenue paid for development and imported expertise, benefiting suppliers and reinforcing the bilateral relationship. GAO described industrialization, trade, manpower training, agriculture, science and technology among the purposes. A dollar trust account funding those projects was not the same arrangement as a portfolio of Treasury securities.

Several relationships were being developed together. Economic assistance, military relationships, oil production discussions and financial investment could support one another while proceeding through different decisions and agreements. Kissinger’s telegram gives production, prices, investment and reserves places on a proposed agenda. Turning the agenda into a single permanent currency obligation would replace the negotiations with a much simpler story.

## An investment channel and withheld figures

The [June 1979 report on OPEC holdings](https://www.gao.gov/assets/emd-79-45.pdf) gives a more direct account of the securities channel. Treasury’s add-on facility was first offered to Saudi Arabia in 1974, extended to other OPEC countries in 1975 and then to official foreign financial institutions more broadly. Purchases used the average auction price. Being outside competitive bidding did not mean being outside the government’s accounts.

Attracting a large investor could benefit American borrowing. Giving official investors a convenient means to buy securities was financial diplomacy as well as debt management. A service built around the needs of an important creditor could help keep its business in American markets.

Disclosure was another question. The report’s cover criticized the limited country-level information available to Congress and the public. Treasury argued that publishing the country totals could reveal a dominant official institution’s holdings. GAO sought greater disclosure and a justification for the treatment.

The dispute was over what could be known about particular holdings. The investment mechanism was publicly described by 1979, while the reporting of country holdings remained restricted. A claim that nobody knew the arrangement existed until decades later would obscure the earlier dispute over who was allowed to inspect it.

The facility gave official investors access without competitive bidding, at the average auction price. That combination deserves attention: it offered convenience to favored customers without, on the report’s account, granting them an undisclosed price. The missing country figures limited scrutiny of who used that access and how heavily.

The withheld detail nevertheless affects what outsiders can judge. A total for several countries can show the scale of their combined investment while concealing concentration in one institution or government. Someone trying to assess dependence on a particular creditor needs the second kind of information. Aggregation can therefore leave a consequential relationship difficult to scrutinize even where the total has been published.

## Where an oil surplus can go

“Recycling” describes the movement of an exporter’s revenues back into expenditure or financial claims elsewhere. If a producer spends less on imports than it earns abroad, it acquires claims on the rest of the world. What form those claims take matters to the countries and institutions receiving the funds.

A purchase of Treasury debt supports the market for American government borrowing. A deposit at a commercial bank gives that bank a funding source, which it may use alongside other liabilities to finance assets. A purchase of goods pays a supplier. These paths can reinforce American influence in different ways; they are not one transaction repeated under different names.

Even a Treasury purchase can involve different recipients of the cash. Buying a newly issued security provides funds to the government. Buying an existing security from an investor pays that investor, who then has another choice about the proceeds. Demand in the secondary market still affects the conditions under which securities can be traded and issued. But “holding American debt” is not a complete account of where a particular oil payment went.

They also distribute risk differently. A bank accepting funds that can be withdrawn soon while lending over a longer period faces a mismatch. A borrower with income in one currency and debt in dollars faces an exchange-rate exposure. A producer saving abroad takes a view about the assets and currencies it will need later.

For a simple example, suppose a company owes $1 million but earns revenue in its domestic currency. If its currency weakens sharply against the dollar, servicing the unchanged dollar debt can require more local revenue. Its need to obtain dollars now comes from the loan contract, whether or not it buys a barrel of oil. Oil is one source of dollar transactions inside a larger system of obligations.

Linda Goldberg’s [2024 account of the international dollar](https://www.newyorkfed.org/newsevents/speeches/2024/gol240303) emphasizes that wider system: trade invoicing, international borrowing, global banking and demand for Treasury assets. She explains how a commonly used currency can become easier to use and finance precisely because others already use it. Her discussion also identifies benefits for American borrowers and producers and the international transmission of financial stress.

The relationships give existing dollar use some staying power. A firm choosing a different invoice currency still needs counterparties, credit and services in that currency. A reserve manager considering an alternative needs assets that can be purchased and sold in adequate volume. Changing a label on an oil contract is easier than replacing all those relationships together.

## Power through accounts, not just oilfields

Financial access can itself become an instrument of state power. On 28 February 2022, the U.S. Treasury [prohibited U.S. persons’ transactions](https://home.treasury.gov/news/press-releases/jy0612/) with Russia’s central bank, National Wealth Fund and Ministry of Finance. Treasury described the action as effectively immobilizing central-bank assets held in the United States or by U.S. persons, in coordination with allied measures.

The action worked through people and institutions responsible for handling the assets. Its scope depended on where the assets were held and who controlled them. Location, currency, custody and applicable law affect which authority can act and what the action prevents.

Messaging and settlement also need to be separated. [Swift describes itself](https://www.swift.com/about-us/legal/compliance-0/fighting-illegal-financial-activities) as a carrier of financial messages. It does not hold the balances or settle the transactions. Losing access to a messaging service can disrupt banking, but the service and the institutions transferring claims are different parts of the payment process.

An alternative payment route has to deal with those other dependencies too. Replacing a messaging route does not automatically replace the banks, funding, custody or legal exposure associated with an asset. Conversely, changing the currency of an invoice need not remove an institution’s dependence on a bank that is subject to restrictions.

Military alliances and control of resources belong to the wider political setting. But the financial power in these examples can be exercised through specified accounts and prohibitions. It does not depend on proving that each war involving an oil producer was fought to enforce an invoicing convention.

## What Iraq’s euro switch establishes

Iraq did change the currency used for its oil sales under the UN Oil-for-Food Programme. The [Secretary-General’s November 2000 report](https://digitallibrary.un.org/record/428603/files/S_2000_1132-EN.pdf) records the request, the committee’s authorization and the opening of a euro sub-account. The sub-account was established on 1 November and oil sales were made in euros from 7 November. The letter-of-credit format was amended to permit payment in that currency.

This was a change inside the UN-administered programme, with its own accounts and approval process. Iraq could choose a different payment currency within that framework; it had not replaced the dollar throughout world oil trade.

The claim that the 2003 invasion punished the euro switch makes a further connection. To establish that motive, the relevant evidence would concern the people deciding on war: their assessment of the currency change, its expected consequences and the role it played among their objectives.

PNAC’s campaign to remove the regime had begun earlier. Its [January 1998 letter](https://noi.org/wp-content/uploads/2016/01/iraqclintonletter1998-01-26-Copy.pdf) urged removing Saddam Hussein’s regime before the euro decision. The record of that advocacy is preserved in the [Clinton Library’s collection](https://www.clintonlibrary.gov/research/archives/finding-aids/project-new-american-century-pnac). A currency concern could conceivably have entered later calculations, but the earlier campaign cannot be explained as a response to something that had not happened.

The euro decision is documented, and the earlier campaign for regime change is documented. The currency explanation still lacks the link between them: a record showing how the switch entered the deliberations of those choosing war. The failure of the WMD case gives good reason to examine those deliberations closely, but supplies no such link itself.

## A large role without a single anchor

The [Fed’s 2025 review](https://www.federalreserve.gov/econres/notes/feds-notes/the-international-role-of-the-u-s-dollar-2025-edition-20250718.html) reports that dollars made up 58 percent of disclosed official foreign-exchange reserves in 2024. That figure concerns reserves, not the share of oil sales or all international payments. The review presents dollar use across several financial functions and explicitly notes the lag in the data: it could not yet show the consequences of policy changes during 2025.

A claim of de-dollarization needs to specify which relationship is changing. A new bilateral payment arrangement, a change in reserve holdings and a reduction in dollar borrowing would be different developments. One may encourage another. None should be treated as all three merely because it uses another currency.

The same applies to an energy transition. Less oil trade would alter one important set of transactions and the revenues available for recycling. It would not cancel dollar debts already contracted, remove the market for Treasury securities or determine the currency used in unrelated supply chains. The dollar’s future is exposed to political and financial choices beyond the demand for petroleum.

The cooperation and investment channels gave oil revenues ways to become longer relationships: a contract for expertise, a continuing bank balance or a government security to be renewed. Following the separate decisions—how oil is priced, how it is paid for, where the proceeds are invested and who can restrict access—shows both the advantages the United States gained and the choices other participants retained.
