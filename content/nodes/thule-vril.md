In Munich, the Thule Society had rooms, a newspaper and people willing to work against the revolutionary government. In a nineteenth-century novel, Vril was a force that could light an underground world, heal its inhabitants and kill those they considered inferior. The two belong to different records. Much of what is now called the Thule–Vril story comes from joining them.

The familiar version runs from occult initiation to Nazi power, then to secret aircraft and a surviving technological elite. It offers an explanation for both the rise of the Third Reich and its supposed continuation after defeat. The historical Thule Society gives the story a firm beginning. The difficulty comes in following the rest of the chain.

Thule's members helped organize a racist political movement. Their meeting place and publishing work let us follow some of that activity closely, before asking how a fictional force entered the account.

## A society with a newspaper

The Thule Society took shape in Munich in August 1918 as a cover organization for a branch of the Germanenorden, a nationalist and antisemitic order. Its organizer, Rudolf von Sebottendorff, gave it a name suggesting a remote northern homeland. The society occupied rooms at the Hotel Vier Jahreszeiten. According to [the Munich Documentation Center for the History of National Socialism](https://www.nsdoku.de/lexikon/artikel/thule-gesellschaft-835), it had more than two hundred members by the end of that year, and its aims included a racist dictatorship.

The esoteric presentation served political aims. The term *völkisch*, often used for its milieu, treated the nation as a community defined by ancestry and racial belonging. A claim to speak for the true people could therefore exclude neighbors who lived in the same city, spoke the same language and held the same citizenship. The idealized homeland gave this exclusion an imagined ancestral authority.

The society's newspaper provides a more concrete route into early Nazi history than a search for hidden initiations. Thule controlled the *Münchener Beobachter*, subsequently renamed the *Völkischer Beobachter*. The Nazi Party acquired the paper in December 1920. That is an identifiable transfer of a publishing platform, with access to readers and a means of repeating political arguments.

There was also a personal connection. Karl Harrer, associated with Thule, helped establish the German Workers' Party, or DAP, in January 1919. The DAP was the party Hitler later joined and which became the NSDAP. [Historian Hermann Gilbhard describes](https://www.historisches-lexikon-bayerns.de/Lexikon/Artikel_44318) a wider effort to coordinate nationalist groups, rather than treating the new party as a simple public name for Thule.

A newspaper can pass between organizations without the seller secretly commanding the buyer. A participant can help found another group without carrying every doctrine of the first into the second. Calling Thule the occult parent of Nazism obscures these different relationships. The newspaper had readers; Harrer had collaborators. Each provided a way for political activity to grow.

Hitler's initiation into Thule does not follow from either connection. Membership and beliefs also varied among people who visited these nationalist circles. Political movements grow through overlapping organizations, rivalries and changes of allegiance. Treating everyone in that network as a member of one concealed order makes the movement look more unified than the evidence warrants.

## Counterrevolution in Munich

Germany's defeat and the fall of the Bavarian monarchy in November 1918 changed what such circles could attempt. In Munich, competing claims to political authority eventually included the Bavarian Soviet Republic of April 1919. Thule's opposition involved practical work against the revolutionary authorities.

Gilbhard's account describes forged seals, infiltration and the movement of information to the government in Bamberg. Couriers used railway officials' identities. Thule also participated in organizing the counterrevolutionary forces associated with Freikorps Oberland. These activities required contacts, access and people prepared to take risks. Secret-society membership could help establish trust, but the work itself was recognizable political and military organizing.

On April 26, revolutionary forces raided the society's premises and arrested a group of people. On April 30, ten prisoners were shot at the Luitpold Gymnasium; seven were Thule members. Schalm's museum account notes that workers' and soldiers' councils publicly distanced themselves from the killings the following day. The deaths subsequently became material for right-wing propaganda against the revolution.

The seven Thule members' politics offered no justification for murdering them. Their deaths also gave survivors a cause around which to rally, with the organization's own aims pushed into the background. The propaganda worked by selecting the part of the event that served its speakers and carrying that part into a broader account of their enemies.

The sequence is easy to lose. The DAP had already been founded before the April killings and the military suppression of the Soviet Republic. It did not simply emerge afterward as the inevitable political expression of Thule's martyrs. A story told backward from Hitler's eventual rule can make each earlier event appear to prepare his arrival. People acting in Munich in 1919 faced a less settled situation, including competition within the nationalist right.

Thule's political importance diminished as other organizations grew. In 1933, Sebottendorff tried to claim credit for the movement's origins in *Bevor Hitler kam*, or *Before Hitler Came*. The claim did not secure his standing with the Nazi regime.

Sebottendorff was making a bid for recognition, with reasons to present his earlier activities as indispensable. Later admirers of an occult explanation have reasons to welcome that presentation. His retrospective claim therefore needs to be weighed alongside the organization's actions and decline, rather than used as its own confirmation.

## The power in the novel

Vril entered print long before these events. Edward Bulwer-Lytton's *The Coming Race*, published in 1871, sends a surface-dwelling narrator into an inhabited world underground. Its people, the Vril-ya, command a force the visitor struggles to explain in the scientific vocabulary available to him.

In chapter VII, he compares it with electricity, then finds that comparison inadequate. Vril also encompasses powers he associates with magnetism and other agencies. It operates across divisions that his own culture treats separately: physical energy, life and mental influence. Within the novel, the explanation makes extraordinary events part of one developed science. The reader receives the explanation through a visitor who cannot reproduce that science himself.

The conversation begins with a problem of translation. His hosts have learned his language while he has been unconscious. Zee, his host's daughter, attributes this exchange of knowledge to Vril. Its power is already involved in his dependence on them before he can ask what it is. He has benefited from something he does not understand, among people who understand him better than he understands them.

The novel also makes fun of the visitor's confidence in his own civilization. He praises American democracy while recalling a senator whose vote his brothers' railway company has bought. He scarcely notices the contradiction. The underground society can expose the failings of the surface world without automatically becoming the author's uncomplicated ideal.

By chapter XXVI, the visitor is miserable. The Vril-ya appear to have solved problems that occupy political reformers above ground: poverty, destructive competition, exhausting labor and disease. Machinery supplies abundance; their lives are long and orderly. Yet his admiration has become inseparable from the knowledge that these courteous people could destroy him without compunction.

This is what disappears when Vril is reduced to “free energy.” Its abundance supports a social order, and that order has boundaries. Comfort within it does not guarantee safety for someone classified outside it. The visitor's status matters more than the kindness he has received from particular members of the household.

Zee's position adds another complication. The novel gives women greater physical strength and an especially effective command of Vril. In chapter XXVI, the narrator connects their rights with their power to enforce them. Authority rests partly on capacities that his own society distributes differently. The imagined civilization lets the book rearrange familiar relations between technology, sex and political authority.

The ending makes the cost personal. Zee helps the narrator escape after a decision that he must die. Her care for him puts her at odds with her community's judgment. Once home, he remembers her with regret, while fearing what would happen if her people reached the surface. Their peaceful life among themselves offers little reassurance to those they might supplant.

The visitor wants to escape the civilization whose achievements he admires. That conflict gives the book something in common with later [[hollow-earth]] and [[breakaway-civilization]] narratives. A concealed population seems to answer the wish that someone has solved our problems. Its separation from ordinary people also raises the question of whom those solutions would serve.

Bulwer-Lytton could give healing, energy and thought a single fictional cause. An experimenter would have to say what is being measured, how the apparatus works and what distinguishes the proposed force from familiar effects. The explanation works inside the novel because its imagined events answer to it. Outside the book, that agreement has to be demonstrated.

## Ley's sliced apple

There is evidence that some people went looking for the force outside fiction. In the May 1947 issue of *Astounding Science Fiction*, the science writer Willy Ley recalled a Berlin group that devoted its spare time to Vril. He thought it called itself the *Wahrheitsgesellschaft*, or Society for Truth. His uncertainty about the name belongs in any account based on his recollection.

In [Ley's recollection](https://archive.org/details/Astounding_v39n03_1947-05_AK), its adherents knew *The Coming Race* was a novel. They believed fiction had been the means of disclosing a real power. They could dismiss the underground population while retaining Vril, then speculate that the British had used it to obtain their empire and that the Romans had possessed it too. The proposed discovery method involved contemplating an apple cut in half.

Ley remembered receiving this explanation solemnly and in confidence. He also recalled a first issue of a magazine, which he regretted not keeping. The account gives us Ley's retrospective testimony about people who believed they were recovering a secret. It supplies neither the missing magazine nor a successful demonstration.

In Ley's account, contemplating the apple was a way into the secret. The group expected a common object to disclose a hidden principle to the properly attentive observer. Its members' certainty did not depend on Bulwer-Lytton having written an instruction manual; interpreting him as a disguised messenger allowed the search to continue without one.

That interpretation is flexible. An inconvenient part of a book can remain fiction, while an attractive part becomes concealed testimony. Once the reader makes that division, the ordinary fact that the book is a novel stops counting against the claim. What is still needed is a reason, independent of the desired discovery, to decide which passages report reality.

The recalled magazine would have been useful evidence of what the group taught. It would still need to be read as the work of people seeking Vril, rather than assumed to report its discovery.

## Adding the missing connection

A later source comparison shows how the story expanded. In *The Far Side of Geography*, geographer Leslie Hepple examined tales that made Karl Haushofer, the German geopolitical thinker, a master of hidden knowledge. Haushofer's contact with Rudolf Hess gave later writers an attractive route from an intellectual figure into the Nazi leadership.

[Hepple traces an important joining of accounts](https://www.bristol.ac.uk/media-library/sites/geography/migrated/documents/far-side.pdf) to Louis Pauwels and Jacques Bergier's 1960 *Le Matin des magiciens*, known in English as *The Morning of the Magicians*. Their treatment connects Haushofer with the Vril story, although Ley's recollection does not name him. Hepple identifies that connection as an addition by the later authors.

Hepple also compares their use of Jack Fishman's book about the Spandau prisoners. He finds a reference to Hess learning geopolitics from Haushofer, but no direct statement naming Haushofer a master magician. Political teaching has acquired an occult biography in the retelling.

The alteration matters because the names on either side are real. Ley wrote about Vril seekers; Haushofer knew Hess. When a later account connects those facts, a reader may remember the reality of the endpoints and lose track of who supplied the line between them. Subsequent repetition can make the composite account look older than it is.

Even the grammar changes as the story travels. Hepple notices a French conditional in the reported attribution to Hess: the wording leaves a distance between the authors and what Hess supposedly said. Repeated as a direct declaration in a later account, the same material can sound like firm testimony. The uncertainty has disappeared in the retelling, without a new witness appearing.

Several books can repeat the same addition. If their accounts ultimately depend on the same passage, their agreement tells us about that passage's circulation. A contemporary letter placing Haushofer in the group would do different work: it could connect a named person with the organization at an identifiable time. Its value would depend on its origin and contents, rather than how often later writers cited it.

The same problem becomes more demanding when the account reaches aircraft. Establishing that a group discussed Vril would be only a beginning. An operational program would require people, materials, calculations, construction, testing and results. A technical drawing would need an attributable origin and a relationship to something built. A familiar name written beside an unfamiliar machine cannot establish that relationship by itself.

## What the legend changes

The larger story often continues through [[operation-paperclip]], [[operation-high-jump]] and [[ufos]]. Those subjects need their own records. The Thule newspaper's transfer to the Nazi Party is not a model that can simply be extended to a transfer of Vril machinery after the war. One documented transaction cannot authenticate another proposed transaction with different participants and objects.

There is a temptation to treat the known secrecy of military research as permission to fill every gap. But even a deliberately concealed project has practical requirements. Its absence from one archive might leave a question open; it would not tell us that its power source came from Bulwer-Lytton. A claim about a working power source needs results that can be examined.

The supernatural version can also change the moral proportions of the history. It gives a racist movement access to knowledge denied to everyone else, and defeat becomes the public appearance of a successful escape. Political failure is recast as technological superiority. A reader may reject Nazi politics while still accepting the legend's flattering account of Nazi capability.

Thule's documented activities bring the scale back into view. The rooms in the Hotel Vier Jahreszeiten gave people a place to meet. The newspaper gave them a readership; contacts and covert work gave them opportunities to act. Those resources helped a racist political circle operate in a city in upheaval. They are substantial enough without adding an undiscovered force.

The paper passed to the Nazi Party is an identifiable object in Thule's history. The magazine Ley wished he had kept is a missing object in the history of Vril seekers. Neither can do the work of the other. Following them separately leaves a real political organization and a smaller, stranger search for fictional power—and shows where later writers began turning the two into one.
