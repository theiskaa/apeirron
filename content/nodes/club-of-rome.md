An economy can run into a resource limit before it has used the last of a resource. It may have to devote so much work to obtaining the next tonne that it can no longer maintain everything built around the cheap supply. Farms, hospitals and factories can suffer while material remains in the ground.

That is one of the mechanisms behind *The Limits to Growth*, the 1972 report commissioned for the Club of Rome. Its central concern was the interaction between growth, physical constraints and the time it takes people to respond. The disputed curves become easier to understand once we follow what was happening inside the model.

Its warning depended on how the researchers represented technology, human responses and a very unequal world. Those choices remain open to criticism. They also make the book more interesting than its reputation as a prophecy of the year when everything would run out.

## The Club and the research team

The Club of Rome grew from discussions in 1968 involving Aurelio Peccei, an Italian industrialist, and Alexander King, a scientist and international policy official. Its [institutional history](https://www.clubofrome.org/about-us/history/) describes an effort to bring together problems usually handled separately: population, industrial development, resources and the environment.

Commissioning research and seeking the attention of officials gave the Club a way to make some problems prominent. It also gave the chosen researchers an audience beyond their field. This is one form of the private influence examined in [[cfr-trilateral]]; the Club had its own participants and projects.

The 1972 book was a report *to* the Club, written by Donella Meadows, Dennis Meadows, Jørgen Randers and William Behrens, drawing on an international MIT team directed by Dennis Meadows. The sponsor chose a project; the researchers built the account of it. A model's equations need examination on their merits; a sponsor's access and political preferences raise additional questions about how the results are presented and used.

The researchers worked with World3, building on Jay Forrester's system dynamics approach. They linked population, agriculture, industrial production, nonrenewable resources and persistent pollution. These were interacting sectors with many relationships inside them. The five headings describe the subject matter, rather than the number of things the computer calculated.

## Why growth can outlast its support

A stock is something accumulated: industrial equipment, a population or pollution remaining in the environment. A flow adds to it or takes from it. Births and deaths change a population. Investment adds productive equipment; deterioration removes it. Pollution can accumulate when releases exceed removal.

Feedback connects these processes. Production can support investment in more productive capacity, allowing further production. That is a reinforcing loop. A growing burden of extraction can draw investment away from other uses, weakening the same expansion. A response can be real and still arrive too slowly to prevent damage.

With continuing percentage growth, the burden can change considerably during a delay. A quantity increasing by 3 percent each year roughly doubles in twenty-four years. If material use per item were cut in half once, another doubling of the number produced would bring total use back to its starting point. Production would use less material than it otherwise would, while total use could continue rising.

The standard run in the [original report](https://www.donellameadows.org/wp-content/userfiles/Limits-to-Growth-digital-scan-version.pdf) develops a related problem through industrial capital. As resources become harder to obtain, more capital goes into extraction. Less remains for investment elsewhere. Eventually new investment fails to replace worn-out capacity, and industrial output falls. Agriculture and services suffer because they depend on industrial inputs.

The replacement problem is easier to picture at the scale of one factory. A factory can be producing useful goods while its machinery ages. If all the surplus that once bought replacement equipment must now pay for harder-to-obtain inputs, current production can conceal a declining ability to continue. That small-scale illustration leaves out most of World3, but makes the replacement problem visible. Existing machines allow a delay between pressure on investment and a visible fall in output.

Population has its own delays. A change in conditions does not instantly change the number of people alive or the age structure. In the standard run, population continues rising after industrial output has peaked. The curves separate in time because different parts of the system respond at different speeds.

Overshoot names the period when activity exceeds what can be sustained while existing stocks and delayed effects allow it to continue. Output can still be rising while the capacity that supports it deteriorates. Waiting for every measure to look bad would miss the period when a response could be most useful.

## A warning with assumptions

The authors did not simply announce that every mineral would disappear by 2000. They varied assumptions and examined different paths. They nevertheless made a strong conditional claim: if prevailing growth trends continued, they argued, limits would be reached within a century, with a likely decline in population and industrial capacity. Calling the runs scenarios should not make that conclusion disappear.

Technology was part of their tests. One run doubled the assumed resource base while leaving the other relationships unchanged. Industry could grow further, but the larger industrial system produced more pollution. Within the model, pollution then damaged food production and increased mortality. Increasing supply had changed which constraint stopped the expansion.

Other runs reduced pollution or raised agricultural yields. Combinations of technological and social changes could produce greater stability. The report did not impose a permanently fixed food ceiling or make collapse the only result a simulation could produce. Its argument was that solving one constraint while continuing expansion could bring another into play.

The dispute turns on the kinds of adaptation the model allows and how quickly they can work. A one-time improvement in efficiency and a continuing process of invention are different assumptions. A model can include optimistic changes and still be criticized for the way it represents future changes.

William Nordhaus made that case in [“Lethal Model 2,” published in 1992](https://www.brookings.edu/wp-content/uploads/1992/06/1992b_bpea_nordhaus_stavins_weitzman.pdf), responding to the update *Beyond the Limits* and its model. He argued that outcomes depended on assumptions about technological change and substitution between inputs. His simpler growth models showed why changing those assumptions could change the conclusion. He also challenged the empirical basis of relationships used in the limits models.

Substitution has a practical meaning. If a material becomes costly, producers may redesign a product to use less, use another material or abandon that product. The relevant question is how far these changes can go, at what cost and over what period. An appeal to human ingenuity leaves those questions unanswered. A declining curve generated under restrictive assumptions leaves them unanswered too.

Nordhaus's own account also credited the detailed documentation of the earlier model. It was possible to examine the machinery and disagree with it. His criticism of its specification should not be mistaken for evidence that the researchers concealed it.

There is a limit to the optimistic argument too. Avoiding one material constraint may increase demand elsewhere. A replacement needs its own infrastructure and may have its own waste stream. The replacement has to be assessed with those costs included. Some changes will reduce the burden considerably; others will move it.

## What later comparisons actually compared

Graham Turner's [2008 study](https://webstatic.niwa.co.nz/library/CSIROwp2008-09.pdf) compared observations from 1970 to 2000 with three published scenarios from the early World3 work: the standard run, comprehensive technology and a stabilized world. He found the observed trends closer to important features of the standard run. That was evidence against dismissing the entire exercise as already disproved.

The comparison required interpretation. Population has an observable counterpart, although measurement is imperfect. A global quantity called persistent pollution is much harder to match to a single series. Turner discussed proxies and uncertainty, and generally normalized observations to the model's 1970 values. Agreement therefore concerned subsequent behavior, not an independent recovery of every starting value.

Two lines set to begin together can later separate. Whether they do is useful information about a model's behavior, but their agreement at the beginning is part of the setup. The graph needs to be read with that setup in view.

Turner argued that agreement across interacting variables gave the model some validation. He did not present it as absolute confirmation that the projected collapse must occur. To test the more disturbing part of a scenario, the relationships that produce its downturn also need support.

Gaya Herrington's [2021 comparison](https://doi.org/10.1111/jiec.13084) used World3-03, the revised version associated with the 2004 update, rather than treating the 1972 curves as unchanged. She examined ten variables and four scenarios. The two closest were BAU2, which doubled the baseline resource assumption, and comprehensive technology. They did not have the same ending: BAU2 displayed collapse, while comprehensive technology showed more moderate declines.

Herrington emphasized that the scenarios often remained close over the period being compared. She also used proxies for quantities that cannot be observed directly. Her conclusion about whether the later declines would constitute collapse was inconclusive. A finding of two close fits with different futures cannot honestly be reduced to one guaranteed catastrophe.

Even the more optimistic of the two scenarios contains a halt in growth and later losses. If that possibility is taken seriously, a society might choose to invest in making essential services less vulnerable to interruptions in production. Such a choice need not depend on believing that one particular downturn has been predicted exactly.

These two studies concern different versions of World3 and different observations. A change in which scenario fits best may reflect changes in the world, changes in the model or the data used to represent it. Keeping track of all three is necessary before calling the comparison a record of successful prediction.

## The sentence about humanity

The Club's political reputation also rests on a passage in Alexander King and Bertrand Schneider's [*The First Global Revolution*](https://www.clubofrome.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/08/Club-of-Rome-The-First-Global-Revolution_compressed-1.pdf), published in 1991. After discussing environmental dangers as possible common adversaries, the authors conclude: “The real enemy then is humanity itself.”

The preceding sentences explain what King and Schneider meant. The authors identify human intervention in natural processes as the cause of the dangers, and argue for changed behavior. The passage does not describe a plan to kill people or establish that environmental problems were fabricated. Reading it as such supplies actions and intentions the text does not contain.

The language still deserves criticism. Calling humanity an enemy blurs the distinction between different activities and the people responsible for them. Someone with little material consumption and little power over investment choices disappears into the same accused collective as those making much larger decisions. A phrase meant to encourage shared responsibility can conceal unequal responsibility.

The surrounding chapter also questions the adequacy of existing democratic arrangements, while explicitly warning against rejecting democracy. It distinguishes cooperation between sovereign states from a single world government. There is a political argument here about urgency, competence and institutions. It can be disputed without turning the last sentence into a confession of a depopulation program.

Their appeal to urgency still leaves a question about the people who will live with a response: what role will they have in deciding it? Expert analysis can identify a danger and illuminate the consequences of delay. It cannot obtain democratic authority simply by showing that delay is costly.

## The people inside an average

The global scale that makes World3 interesting also limits what it can settle. An average amount of food per person does not tell us who can obtain it. A reduction in total material use does not say whether it came from less waste among the affluent or deprivation among people who already had too little.

These are not details to settle after the scientific work is finished. Distribution changes whether a policy can meet people's needs, who has a reason to support it and whether it can endure. Restricting a poor household's access to energy and reducing wasteful consumption by a wealthy household may both lower a global total. They have different human consequences.

Taking distribution seriously adds a requirement to the environmental warning. A policy has to reduce pressures without merely moving their cost to people with less ability to refuse. New technology, public investment and changes in consumption all need that examination. A claim about population likewise cannot decide for people how to form a family or justify coercion through an aggregate target.

The Club of Rome helped put a question on the public agenda that deserves to remain there: what happens when continued expansion weakens the systems it depends on? World3 offered a way to trace that risk, while leaving important assumptions and responses in dispute. A line representing humanity cannot tell us whose life should become harder so that the line can change direction.
