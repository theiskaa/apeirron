On December 14, 2012, Adam Lanza killed his mother at home and then killed twenty children and six adults at Sandy Hook Elementary School in Newtown, Connecticut. Two other adults at the school were wounded. Lanza died there. The state’s attorney reconstructed the killings from witness statements, emergency calls, medical findings, and physical evidence. In a different reconstruction, assembled out of television clips, parents who had lost children were accused of pretending to grieve. Alex Jones carried that false account to his audience on Infowars. Years later, some of the people accused of being actors sat in court and described what the accusation had done to their lives.

One of the clips showed Robbie Parker, whose six-year-old daughter Emilie had been killed, approaching a microphone. He smiled briefly and grew serious before speaking. Jones treated the change in his expression as evidence of a performance. But a change in expression cannot tell a viewer whether someone is pretending. Even a full recording of Parker’s remarks would not tell us who was in the school the day before. The accusation asked a few seconds of video to answer a question that had nothing to do with what the camera recorded.

The claim of staging gave that accusation a political purpose: the government had supposedly invented the killings as a pretext for confiscating guns. The shooting did prompt real legislative debate. In April 2013, a federal proposal to expand background checks received 54 votes in the Senate and failed because it needed 60. The proposal was not a confiscation measure. More importantly, the existence of a debate after the shooting was evidence of a political response, not of an arrangement made before it.

## What the inquiry had to establish

The [state’s attorney’s report](https://web.archive.org/web/20131125212413id_/http://www.ct.gov/csao/lib/csao/Sandy_Hook_Final_Report.pdf) was written to establish criminal responsibility, including whether someone else helped Lanza. It distinguishes the information responders were working with from findings investigators could make afterward. Police detained people they encountered near the school while they were still trying to learn what had happened. One turned out to be a parent holding a phone; two others were reporters. The detentions were real. They recorded what police did before they knew who those people were, not findings that any of them had taken part in the shooting.

The evidence of what happened in the school came from sources with different jobs. People inside described what they heard and saw. Emergency calls recorded reports as they came in. Investigators compared recovered evidence with the medical examiner’s findings, and the medical examiner’s office used school records, photographs, and descriptions in identifying victims. These records did not all originate in a press conference. They were produced by people solving different problems and could be compared where they overlapped. The state’s attorney concluded that Lanza acted alone. He could not establish why Lanza chose the school. The Connecticut Office of the Child Advocate later studied Lanza’s history and the services he received, but expressly said its findings could not explain why he committed mass murder. Knowing who did it did not require inventing an answer about motive.

The crisis-actor accusation used uncertainty differently. A correction could be taken as proof that the story was being changed. A parent who spoke easily could be accused of reciting a script; a parent who struggled to speak could be accused of putting on an act. The same reasoning could explain opposite observations, which meant that no interview could have cleared the person being accused. Compare that with the account of the detained parent. Investigators had a specific question about his identity that they could answer. Once they did, the initial suspicion counted for less, not more.

## What it meant to be named

Infowars gave the accusation a recurring home. In the Texas case brought by Scarlett Lewis and Neil Heslin, the parents of six-year-old Jesse Lewis, Jones acknowledged that the shooting had happened. The acknowledgment came years after his broadcasts had called it fabricated. At the damages trial Lewis described threats and recalled someone driving into her driveway on Christmas morning and taking pictures of her and her home. The Texas jury awarded the parents about $4.1 million in compensatory damages, then assessed $45.2 million in a separate punitive award. Those were the jury’s awards, not a tally of what the parents ultimately collected.

The Connecticut plaintiffs included relatives of people killed at the school and an FBI agent who responded. They testified about threats and harassment received in person, online, and by mail. As the appellate court noted, their testimony concerned both what had been said about them and what happened when other people acted on it. The injury was not the abstract effect of an erroneous belief. It included being personally identified as a participant in a hoax and having that accusation follow them outside the broadcasts in which it had been repeated.

## What the Connecticut judgment decided

The Connecticut jury did not sit to decide whether the shooting had happened. Nor did it return an ordinary verdict on liability after a trial of that issue. Before the jury heard the case, Judge Barbara Bellis entered default against Jones and Free Speech Systems for repeated violations of orders governing discovery, the exchange of evidence before trial, and a protective order covering confidential information. The withheld material included audience analytics and detailed accounting records. The two sets of records mattered for different reasons: one could help show how extensively the broadcasts had circulated, while the other could help the plaintiffs test how the company made money. The trial court had already tried a lesser sanction. The [Connecticut Appellate Court’s opinion](https://www.supremecourt.gov/DocketPDF/25/25-268/373909/20250905173307097_No.%2025-%20Petition.pdf#page=59), reproduced in the U.S. Supreme Court petition’s appendix, explains why the trial court concluded that the failures were preventing the plaintiffs from fairly pursuing their claims.

Default resolved liability, but it did not tell the jury how much harm the plaintiffs had suffered. The plaintiffs still had to present evidence on damages. In October 2022 the jury awarded $965 million in compensatory damages. The judge separately awarded about $322 million in common-law punitive damages in the form of attorney’s fees, about $1.5 million in costs, and $150 million in statutory punitive damages under the Connecticut Unfair Trade Practices Act. These were different components of the judgment, not one jury award for a single offense.

The distinction mattered on appeal. In December 2024 the appellate court upheld the default and the compensatory award but directed the trial court to vacate the $150 million statutory award. It reasoned that the plaintiffs had alleged direct harm from false statements about the shooting, not from the advertising or sale of products. That a broadcaster sells products gives a reason to ask for business records; it does not, by itself, turn every wrong committed in a broadcast into an unlawful trade practice. In October 2025 the U.S. Supreme Court declined to hear the case. Its refusal to review is not a separate ruling on the merits. Nor does the amount of a judgment say how much the people awarded it have collected.

## The question a clip cannot answer

People who encountered a short video of Parker did not see the witness statements, the work of identifying the victims, or the records that allowed investigators to test initial suspicions. Sharing the clip did not add a new source. It repeated the same few seconds, usually with the accusation attached. YouTube’s published rules now list denial or minimization of a well-documented major violent event among prohibited examples in its hate-speech policy. A rule of that kind says what a platform may remove. It does not tell us how many viewers saw a particular clip, whether a recommendation led them there, or how much of the subsequent harassment can be attributed to any one route of distribution.

The mistake in looking at Parker’s face was not failing to settle on the right interpretation of his expression. There was no need to settle it at all. The records established what happened to Emilie without asking her father to look a certain way. It took a different record, years later, to show what the family had endured after those few seconds of his life were turned against him.
