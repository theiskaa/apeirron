Take a government loan of 1,000 at five percent interest. Paying 50 a year for twenty years would pay out 1,000 in interest while leaving the principal owed. This illustrates the calculation in a passage of the *Protocols of the Elders of Zion*. It also appears in a French political satire published decades earlier, where it has nothing to do with Jews.

Philip Graves placed the passages beside one another in *The Times* in August 1921. The same rate and sequence of twenty-year periods appear in both texts. They are one of many correspondences he used to show that the supposed record of a Jewish plan for world domination had been assembled from an earlier author's work.

The *Protocols* is a fabrication. The council whose supposed intentions it reveals did not exist. The copying can be inspected. The harder question is why a fabricated confession kept being published after its construction had been exposed.

## The speaker borrowed from another book

Maurice Joly's *Dialogue in Hell Between Machiavelli and Montesquieu* appeared in Brussels in 1864. Its preface openly presents the work as fiction. The two dead political thinkers meet, and Joly has Machiavelli describe how a ruler could consolidate power while keeping some of the forms of political liberty. The contemporary target was Napoleon III's regime in France.

Joly's Machiavelli is a literary character. Reading his speeches as transcripts of the historical Machiavelli would already be a mistake. The later compiler made a further substitution: arguments written for an invented despot became words attributed to an invented Jewish leadership. Explanations from both speakers could be made to sound like an insider’s account of domination.

The borrowing extends beyond a shared suspicion of powerful people. Graves compared a scheme for taxing publications. In Joly, the ruler would burden shorter books with a stamp duty. Writers seeking to escape the tax would have to produce longer, more expensive works, which fewer people would buy or read. In the *Protocols*, a closely corresponding scheme gives books under 300 pages especially harsh treatment and predicts the same result.

The proposal goes well beyond a complaint about censorship. A shared proposal about page thresholds, taxation, expensive long books and a reduced readership points toward a textual relationship. The sequence of the reasoning matters as much as a matching phrase.

Graves also noticed the same many-armed image of the Hindu god Vishnu in both works. Joly's ruler compares his press to a being with a hundred arms reaching across public opinion. The *Protocols* gives its conspirators a corresponding image. The image traveled with the argument, alongside other distinctive details that the compiler retained.

The loan calculation works the same way. There is nothing inherently mysterious about the arithmetic: interest payments do not repay principal unless the contract provides for that. The passage turns that familiar arrangement into an accusation by placing it in a conspirator's mouth. Graves’s comparison exposes that change of speaker.

The loan calculation is spoken by Montesquieu in Joly, in an argument about borrowing responsibly and making provision to repay debt. The compiler lifted that explanation out of an exchange between speakers. That is another reason to read beyond a matched line. The relation between speakers, objection and reply changes when a dialogue becomes a conspirator’s monologue.

Graves’s demonstration did not require finding the compiler or reconstructing every stage of composition. A book can be exposed as borrowed fiction before the person who repackaged it is identified. Accounts of the document’s manufacture have nevertheless often filled the gaps with a complete story.

Graves reported receiving a French volume without its title page from an unnamed Russian refugee. The pamphlet describes its damaged arrangement, including pages bound in the wrong place. A complete copy identified at the British Museum allowed the newspaper to name the work. The refugee’s account of buying it from a former police officer could suggest an interesting history, but a reader did not have to trust that account to compare the published texts. The argument could leave its anonymous informant behind.

## The confession that explained every opponent

Joly's twelfth dialogue describes a ruler who maintains newspapers representing different political positions. Some are openly loyal. Others appear independent or oppositional but remain attached to the same government. Their disagreements help make the political order seem free while keeping fundamental challenges outside the permitted argument.

A reader of the satire can ask whether a government has made opposition harmless. The alleged confession supplies the answer in advance. Conflicting newspapers can all be assigned to the same hidden controller. Their disagreement then becomes evidence of how well the controller disguises his work.

The *Protocols* gives an imaginary enemy both the ambition to direct events and the ability to disguise direction as conflict. A revolution and its opponents, financial strength and economic collapse, could be fitted into the same alleged strategy. The text supplies the intention that a particular investigation would otherwise have to establish.

Its antisemitism is central to that operation. The accusation identifies Jews as a collective political actor, assigns them a concealed purpose and encourages the reader to interpret individual conduct through it. A Jewish person's actual beliefs or disagreements become less important than the role the document has already assigned him.

The collective accusation turns neighbors, citizens and political opponents into representatives of a supposed enemy. The fictional speakers seem to relieve the accuser of making a case about particular people: the enemy has allegedly admitted everything already.

Real abuses of power do not rescue that claim. A publisher can manipulate information, a lender can behave dishonestly, and a government can cultivate an appearance of debate. Each proposition has to be investigated in its own setting. None makes a fabricated confession into the minutes of a meeting or makes Jews collectively answerable for the conduct of a publisher, lender or government.

## A publication history, with a gap before it

The earliest known publication of the *Protocols* was in [1903](https://encyclopedia.ushmm.org/content/en/article/protocols-of-the-elders-of-zion), in the Russian newspaper *Znamia*, owned by the antisemitic publicist Pavel Krushevan. Sergei Nilus included a version in his religious writing in 1905. Publication and republication are identifiable events. The composition that preceded them is harder to reconstruct.

One familiar account places the manufacture in the Paris operations of the Russian secret police, the Okhrana, with Matvei Golovinski writing under Pyotr Rachkovsky's direction. The historian [Michael Hagemeister](https://doi.org/10.1215/0094033X-2007-020) has traced serious problems in the testimony supporting this account.

One is a chronological failure. Catherine Radziwill claimed that she saw Golovinski's newly prepared manuscript in Paris in the winter of 1904–05. The text had already been published in 1903. Hagemeister also examines doubts about other witnesses and the repetition of their stories in later accounts. His argument concerns the evidence for authorship and production, not whether the supposed Jewish proceedings were genuine.

Graves himself was clearer about some limits than later retellings suggest. He distinguished the comparisons he could show from a theory about how Joly's book might have passed through police circles. His pamphlet speculates about that route and labels it speculation. The borrowed passages do not depend on the proposed route being right.

He was also working with incomplete bibliographical knowledge. His articles treated Nilus's 1905 edition as the first publication; the known 1903 appearance corrects that chronology. A reader can retain the textual demonstration while correcting the publication date.

The different stories offered for obtaining the supposed document were another warning. Graves compared accounts involving a woman stealing it after a meeting, a friend's discovery in safes and a purported connection to the 1897 Zionist Congress in Basel. These are claims about provenance, not a chain of custody established by independent records.

The changing accounts offer the reader an illicit glimpse while keeping the source of that glimpse difficult to examine. A stolen secret can seem more valuable precisely because it cannot be checked with the people who allegedly held it. But secrecy cannot do the work of authentication. Nor does uncertainty about which propagandist compiled the text reopen the question of whether its fictional council existed.

## What exposure did—and did not—change

Graves's articles appeared on August 16, 17 and 18, 1921. Their pamphlet reprint also includes a *Times* leading article from August 18. The paper accepted the demonstration of plagiarism. It then suggested that the legend could pass into oblivion.

While repudiating the document, the leading article continued to discuss a supposedly separate “Jewish problem” and presented the newspaper's position as impartial. Graves, too, minimized the significance of the forgery's role in fostering hatred of Jews. He put greater emphasis on how it misled prosperous people about the causes of discontent among the poor.

That criticism identified a real political use: a ruler's failures could be explained away as the work of secret agitators. But the division Graves drew also let much of the surrounding prejudice stand. Rejecting a forged document did not require his newspaper to abandon the habit of treating Jewish people as a problem to be discussed from outside.

A journalist could expose the plagiarism while retaining prejudices that had helped the document find a hearing. Removing one supposed piece of evidence will not necessarily remove the assumptions that made someone willing to believe it.

For a committed defender, the next move is to retreat from authenticity to resemblance. Perhaps the document was fabricated, the argument runs, but does it not describe what is happening? That changes the claim. A resemblance to a passage about credit or newspapers cannot identify an author of events. If the supposed confession is withdrawn, the accuser has to supply evidence for the particular accusation. Continuing to use the text as if nothing had changed preserves the authority of a document whose authority has failed.

## A publisher's promise to make amends

Beginning in 1920, Henry Ford's *Dearborn Independent* published the antisemitic series collected as *The International Jew*. Ford dealerships helped distribute the paper. The United States Holocaust Memorial Museum documents how its articles used the *Protocols* and applied collective accusations to American finance, journalism and other parts of public life.

The campaign acquired a prominent industrialist’s name and an existing distribution network. A reader did not have to find an obscure Russian publication. The accusation arrived through a newspaper associated with a familiar business. Its circulation depended on people choosing to publish, finance and distribute it.

In June 1927, Ford authorized a statement to Louis Marshall, president of the American Jewish Committee. The statement acknowledged that his publications had circulated the *Protocols*, recognized them as forgeries and retracted the accusations. It also claimed that Ford had been too occupied with other activities to know what his employees were publishing.

That claim of ignorance forms part of the apology; it should not be adopted as an independent account of his involvement. The statement acknowledged that both publications were his property. Its account of delegated management moved responsibility toward the men he had chosen, while the proposed remedy depended on his ability to order a change.

Marshall’s accompanying account described negotiations that the apology’s claim of spontaneous initiative leaves out. He reported that Ford's representatives had approached him about a settlement of the dispute between Ford and Jewish people. Marshall required more than an expression of regret: retraction, apology, an end to the attacks, withdrawal of the pamphlets and a pledge against repetition. Ford signed an agreed statement, and Marshall replied with a hope that the assurances would be honored.

Marshall was addressing publication as conduct that could be stopped and repaired. Asking forgiveness was one act; recalling pamphlets and changing editorial policy were others. The people harmed had reason to distinguish a public declaration from what a publisher would actually do afterward.

The museum records that the apology appeared in other newspapers in July 1927 but did not run in the *Dearborn Independent*. Overseas circulation of *The International Jew* continued despite instructions to stop publication. Withdrawal was no longer entirely within the originating publisher's control.

Publication could continue after a prominent publisher’s retraction. Copies remained, other publishers had interests of their own, and readers could continue to invoke Ford's name after he had repudiated the material. Persistence was partly a history of these choices and channels, not simply a property of an especially persuasive text.

## A lie used as a political instrument

Nazi propagandists used the *Protocols* after its exposure. The Holocaust Memorial Museum describes Joseph Goebbels acknowledging it as a forgery while asserting an inward truth he found in it. It also documents editions circulated under German occupation during the war. Acceptance of the text's factual authenticity was not necessary for its political use.

The accusation helped present persecution as defense against a supposedly powerful enemy. Jews could be stripped of rights while propaganda depicted them as the secret authors of everyone else's suffering. Their lack of power to stop the persecution did not force the propagandist to revise the story; concealed power could always be asserted instead.

The *Protocols* was one resource in a broader antisemitic ideology, not a sufficient explanation of the Holocaust. Treating one book as the cause would obscure the institutions, decisions and people through which persecution and murder were carried out. Its use matters because it helped give an invented collective intention the appearance of documentary support.

Later stories about a [[new-world-order]] can share its themes of concealed command, controlled opposition and deliberately manufactured crises. Resemblance deserves careful comparison. It does not establish that every theory originated with this text, or that an accusation loses its antisemitic content merely because its speaker adopts a different label. What matters is whom the story identifies, what conduct it attributes to them and what evidence it offers.

The loan passage contains an ordinary calculation, moved from one work into another and made to sound like an admission by an enemy. Reading it beside Joly restores the part that the forgery conceals: the supposed enemy's voice was constructed by someone else. It belongs to the person making the accusation, not to the people being accused.
