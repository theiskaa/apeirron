In 1994, Jonathan Hay gave Andrei Shleifer a cheque for $66,000 to invest in Russian equities. Both men were senior figures in a Harvard project, funded by the American government, to help Russia develop financial markets. Their work brought them close to the people and institutions making the rules for those markets. They were also putting their own money into the market they were helping to shape.

The later court record follows the money through bank accounts, arguments over whether an investment had occurred and a repayment after investigators began asking questions. Hay argued that an indirect investment did not fall within the restrictions governing his work. The judge rejected that reading. Passing the money through another person did not remove the conflict the agreement was intended to prevent.

The phrase “shadow elite” usually conjures a closed room full of important people. In this case the revealing detail was a cheque. It joined a publicly funded advisory role to a private investment, and it left a record that the men involved would eventually have to explain.

Much of the work of influencing a decision happens before it reaches a vote. Someone supplies the advice, drafts the options, introduces a prospective investor or decides whose expertise deserves a hearing. The question is how those advantages work—and when the people using them stop being answerable to the institutions whose authority they borrow.

## The adviser’s investment

USAID’s agreement with the Harvard Institute for International Development supported a project to help Russia build capital markets and attract investment. Shleifer directed the project from Harvard. Hay was its senior Moscow-based official. Their work included assistance with legal reform and the institutions governing securities markets.

The agreement restricted covered employees from conducting business or making investments in the countries to which they were assigned. Which employees and investments fell within that language later became central to the suit. Harvard’s overseas manual explained the concern. Advisers had access to economic matters under official or semi-official auspices; transactions by them or their families could create an actual or apparent conflict between professional work and personal gain. A transaction could therefore be problematic even if it was otherwise lawful.

A government funding assistance with a new securities market should be able to trust that the adviser’s recommendation serves the project. An undisclosed investment gives the adviser another interest in the outcome. The restriction addressed the relationship between the job and the opportunity, before anyone had to prove that a particular recommendation had been bought.

Hay’s Russian activities included several kinds of investment. Besides the equities transaction, he bought into a British fund invested entirely in Russian securities. He argued that this was an investment in Britain. The [2004 judgment](https://www.neochart.com/catalog/cases/f-supp-2d/volume_323/0151_01/index.html) treated its economic substance as an investment in Russian business and found it prohibited.

A larger transaction concerned the First Russian Specialized Depository. Fund managers needed a depository to hold assets and handle the associated transactions. The manager chose investments; the depository provided the infrastructure for carrying them out. A business occupying that required place in the new market could benefit from being established early. The advisory project’s involvement with the rules and officials governing the market made private participation in such a business especially sensitive.

In 1996, money passed through Hay’s father and Elizabeth Hebert, then Hay’s partner, to help finance the purchase or capitalization of the depository. Of the $400,000 transferred, $200,000 belonged to Hay. He characterized the arrangement as a series of personal loans. The court found that he knew the intended business purpose and rejected the attempt to put the transaction outside the restrictions by passing it through other people.

The correspondence supplied evidence beyond the transfer itself. A draft investment memorandum described the depository’s relationship with the Russian securities regulator as an advantage over competitors. The regulator wanted the enterprise to be first, the draft suggested. It proposed offering an investment in the depository together with an investment in Hebert’s fund-management company.

Hebert’s company became the sixth management company licensed by the Russian regulator in August 1996, then the first to register its mutual fund in September. Other managers had therefore already obtained licenses. The draft’s claim to privileged access was a selling point whose meaning can be compared with the licenses actually obtained.

The version addressed to one prospective investor apparently never reached him. It nevertheless recorded a plan to turn the business’s relationship with the regulator into an attraction for investors. The judge examined discussions, loans, correspondence and decisions about seeking advice from Harvard counsel. He found that Hay and Shleifer had acted in agreement to finance the depository and had violated the False Claims Act’s conspiracy provision.

The loans and discussions connected the men to a common undertaking. Their personal acquaintance was the beginning of the explanation; the transactions supplied its substance.

## The university and its advisers

Holding the advisers responsible raised a further question: how much of their conduct was also the university’s responsibility? The answer varied with the legal claim.

Harvard was liable for breach of its agreement. The judge also found Hay liable on false-claims and false-statements counts, and both Hay and Shleifer liable under the conspiracy provision. He did not find Harvard part of that conspiracy. Other fraud claims failed, and some questions concerning Shleifer’s coverage under the investment restrictions remained unresolved at that stage. The ruling addressed cross-motions for summary judgment: the judge could settle some questions on the record while leaving genuinely disputed ones unresolved. Several accusations consequently remained disputed or failed outright.

One dispute turned on the boundaries of an institution. Harvard argued that the grantee was its development institute rather than the university as a whole. Shleifer’s precise employment and assignment status also mattered to whether particular rules applied to him. The answers determined who owed the government a duty under the agreement and which claims could succeed.

A person could be presented to outsiders through Harvard’s reputation while the legal responsibilities attached to a particular unit, appointment or agreement. To an outsider, Harvard’s name could suggest a single institution standing behind the work. Inside the lawsuit, the distinctions between its units and appointments became consequential.

In 2005, Harvard agreed to pay $26.5 million to settle the civil suit. Its [public statement](https://news.harvard.edu/gazette/story/2005/08/harvard-university-reaches-settlement-agreement-with-usaid/) emphasized that the court had not found institutional wrongdoing. That was the university’s characterization; it coexisted with the court’s contractual ruling against it. The Justice Department’s subsequent annual account reported $30.5 million recovered from Harvard and its agents in connection with the project. These were civil proceedings over the advisory project, not a trial of the whole privatization program.

The investigation had already ended the project. USAID suspended it in May 1997 and terminated it in June. Harvard’s development institute dismissed Hay and removed Shleifer from the project. By then the investments and financing arrangements had already taken place.

The project had contained a warning about conflicts from the outset. Enforcing it required an investigation, years of litigation and arguments over the boundaries of an institution that had lent the project its name. In the meantime, people paid to assist public institutions had pursued private opportunities in the same market.

## More than one role at once

Anthropologist Janine Wedel gave “shadow elite” a particular meaning in her work on power brokers. In a 2010 interview, she described people holding overlapping and incompletely disclosed roles—as government advisers, think-tank figures, business consultants or media commentators. The public could struggle to know whom a person represented in any given setting.

Wedel’s concern was that a person could draw authority from several organizations while remaining poorly accountable to any one of them. Each audience might see only the role presented to it: the public official heard an adviser, the investor met a consultant, and the television viewer saw an independent expert.

The Harvard advisers were doing both kinds of work at once. Advisory responsibilities and investment activity overlapped. Family and business relationships carried money and opportunities between them.

A professional title alone would not reveal that arrangement. “Academic adviser” described real work, but it did not describe the whole situation. The bank records showed how family relationships were being used; the agreement explained why those transactions breached an obligation.

The danger is often a borrowed presumption of independence. An audience hears an expert attached to a respected institution and reasonably expects that the stated professional role explains the advice. Undisclosed interests can make that expectation false. A recommendation may deserve scrutiny even before anyone can show that it was technically wrong.

Expertise can make the conflict harder to challenge. The person may understand the subject exceptionally well. The question is who benefits from the particular recommendation, what the audience has been told and whether someone else has the information needed to challenge it. Dismissing the person’s knowledge would leave the conflict untouched. The adviser’s expertise may be precisely what makes the opportunity valuable.

## The private plan and the public fight

The history of the Federal Reserve offers a different relationship between private coordination and public authority. In November 1910, Senator Nelson Aldrich and a small group of banking and financial specialists met secretly at Jekyll Island to develop a banking-reform plan. They concealed the gathering and used first names to prevent staff from identifying them.

Federal Reserve historians describe the result as a proposal for a national reserve association, with branches and a structure giving bankers substantial influence. Ideas developed at the meeting later entered the institution created by law.

But the 1913 Federal Reserve Act was not simply that proposal renamed. Democrats opposed the Aldrich plan. Carter Glass, Robert Owen and Woodrow Wilson helped produce a different arrangement, including regional Reserve Banks and a central board under public appointment. The fight over governance changed the design.

The [institutional histories](https://www.federalreservehistory.org/essays/jekyll-island-conference) describe bankers developing a design early and politicians challenging its distribution of power. One consequential change in the [legislative process](https://www.federalreservehistory.org/essays/federal-reserve-act-signed) concerned the central board: the final arrangement put it under public appointment. The ideas that survived the private meeting reached the law through an argument about authority.

Secrecy allowed a small group to work out its preferences together before facing opposition. It did not save those preferences from alteration once Congress and the administration took up the question.

Carroll Quigley’s *Tragedy and Hope* supplied a broader historical argument about private networks. On pages 949–951, he rejected a sweeping radical-right account of a plot behind American policy, then described an Anglophile network associated with the Round Table groups. He said that he had examined its papers and secret records, supported most of its aims and objected to its obscurity. His account connected lobbying, finance, institutions and personal relationships.

Quigley also described the network as much reduced by the 1960s. His account concerned organizations with a history: people built them, pursued aims through them and lost influence as circumstances changed. Turning that account into an immortal organization with unlimited power would erase the history he was attempting to tell.

## An invitation is an advantage

A private meeting need not issue orders to influence policy. It can give participants time with people they would otherwise reach only through staff, formal submissions or public campaigns. An introduction can establish trust; a shared diagnosis can make one proposal seem reasonable before its alternatives receive a hearing.

Bilderberg’s [organizers describe](https://bilderbergmeetings.org/frequently-asked-questions) their meetings as invitation-only discussions without resolutions, votes or a closing statement. They publish participants and topics while excluding reporting journalists; some journalists attend in a personal capacity. These are the organizers’ descriptions of their procedures. The absence of a formal vote would hardly prevent participants from gaining access, exchanging arguments or continuing a conversation afterward.

The Chatham House Rule, often invoked in accounts of private policy discussion, has a narrower meaning than a ban on using anything learned. It allows use of the information while protecting the identities and affiliations of speakers and participants. Chatham House describes its purpose as encouraging candid discussion.

That protection can be useful for testing an uncertain idea. It can also make it harder for an outsider to trace the source of a position that later enters public debate. When a position enters government, the public has to judge the reasons its advocate offers without necessarily knowing who helped form them.

There are also direct, documented routes from private organizations into government planning. The Council on Foreign Relations’ own history describes its wartime War and Peace Studies project as support for the State Department. It records hundreds of meetings and memoranda submitted before the project ended in 1945. Here the route into government was an organized research project with an identified recipient. The Council describes its own contribution; the entry does not establish that officials adopted every recommendation.

The advantage lies partly in being ready. A group with money, staff and access can develop an analysis while others are still trying to get an issue noticed. Officials facing an urgent decision may rely on the proposal available to them. Groups without that capacity must first find a way to be heard.

## Whose participation is the problem?

*The Crisis of Democracy*, prepared for the Trilateral Commission in 1975, shows why the content of a discussion matters more than its guest list. In his American chapter, Samuel Huntington argued that an “excess of democracy” threatened governability and called for greater restraint. He treated some political nonparticipation as having helped the system function, while acknowledging its undemocratic character.

The argument deserves criticism on its own terms: it risks treating demands for inclusion as a problem for those already governing. But the report was not the Commission’s founding document or a declaration accepted by every member. Its introduction assigned responsibility to the authors. Its published discussion recorded members defending citizens’ rights against government excess and rejecting a prescription of less democracy.

The publication consequently preserves a dispute over participation, including criticism of the restrictive remedy.

The disagreement reaches a familiar difficulty in government. Institutions need ways to make decisions, and specialist work cannot be reduced to a vote on every technical detail. Yet people affected by a policy have claims that are not exhausted by being well administered. They may want a different distribution of costs, a different objective or a chance to replace the people making the choices.

Describing a demand as uninformed can conceal a disagreement over values. A financial adviser may know more about how to organize a securities market than a member of the public. That expertise does not entitle the adviser to an undisclosed financial stake in the arrangements. Competence answers one question; authority and responsibility answer others.

## What a network can explain

The appeal of an affiliation map is that it makes relationships visible at a glance. What it often leaves out is the nature of each relationship. People who attended a discussion, financed an enterprise or agreed to conceal a transaction can all appear as names joined by the same line.

Selection also matters. A forum inviting people already likely to become senior leaders will naturally contain future senior leaders. Their later advancement cannot by itself show that the forum selected them for office. The stronger evidence would connect the meeting to a particular intervention, resource or decision.

An employment relationship carries duties that friendship does not. A loan has terms; a conspiracy requires an agreement. Those differences become visible only when the map leads back to documents and conduct. Otherwise its density can produce a feeling of understanding while leaving responsibility as obscure as before.

The Harvard case had those records. Investigators could move from an official role to an investment restriction, from a transfer to its business purpose, and from discussions to a finding of agreement. Even then, the court separated defendants and claims. The institution’s reputation did not immunize it from liability, and the individuals’ relationship did not make the institution liable for every act.

A serious objection to elite power is that some people can use public authority, private opportunity and institutional credibility together while leaving the people affected unable to see the combination. Disclosure and enforceable restrictions give the people affected a way to challenge that use of authority.

The cheque at the beginning of this account mattered because someone could follow it. It linked an adviser’s public role to an investment he was not free to make under the agreement. The finding rested on the work the government had funded, the obligation Hay had accepted and the transaction he had chosen to make. Those details gave an apparently remote exercise of influence a person who could be held responsible.
