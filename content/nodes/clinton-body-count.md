In August 1994, a magazine reporting on the first years of the Clinton administration described two lists of suspicious deaths. One, circulated by former congressman William Dannemeyer, had twenty-four names. Another, maintained by Indianapolis lawyer Linda Thompson, had thirty-four and was being updated regularly. The overlap was substantial, but the two lists did not even count the same number of deaths. What one reader might take to be a fixed set of victims was already a selection made by people with different rules about whom to include.

Among the names was Vince Foster, the deputy White House counsel whose death had set off investigations and a dispute over access to his office. The list put Foster beside victims of crashes and people reported to have died of natural causes. To see what the count does, it helps to begin with the way it was assembled, then follow one of those deaths through the records that the list compresses to a line.

## How a list became an accusation

Dannemeyer wrote to congressional leaders asking for hearings. According to the contemporary *U.S. News & World Report* article, he conceded that some of the deaths might have nothing to do with Clinton but thought the number itself exceeded what coincidence could explain. Thompson acknowledged having no direct evidence that Clinton had killed anyone and suggested that unnamed people might have been acting to control him. Rep. Andrew Jacobs Jr. put the magazine piece into the *Congressional Record* to criticize the politics of personal accusation. The official publication preserves the reporter’s account of what Thompson said; it does not certify the list or turn her remarks into congressional testimony. ([*Congressional Record*, 12 August 1994](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/CREC-1994-08-12/html/CREC-1994-08-12-pt1-PgE40.htm).)

The article described an early entry involving four Marines killed on a helicopter maintenance flight. Their unit transported the president, and Clinton had flown on that aircraft once before. A Marine spokesman described a mechanical cause; the article reported no evidence of sabotage. Thompson speculated that the crew might have known about plans concerning Bosnia. That account moves from a documented connection to an assumed secret and from the secret to an assumed motive. Nothing in the first step establishes the next two. The word *connection* makes those different ideas sound as though they came from the same record.

The lists could also change the alleged purpose of the killings. A name could be counted because the person supposedly threatened Clinton, or because the death might have served someone who wanted leverage over him. Without evidence about who acted and why, those explanations pull in different directions. The tally stays the same whichever one a reader imagines.

## What the Foster investigations examined

Foster was found dead in Fort Marcy Park, Virginia, on 20 July 1993. The United States Park Police concluded that he died by suicide. Independent counsel Robert Fiske reached the same conclusion in 1994. Two congressional inquiries also agreed on the manner of death. Kenneth Starr’s office reviewed the earlier work and conducted further investigation. That history matters, but five inquiries do not mean five separate sets of physical evidence. Later reviewers could examine the same photographs and objects, ask new questions about them and compare judgments. The value of the later work is in the tests it describes, not the number of offices named. ([Independent counsel’s report](https://archive.org/details/vincent-foster-jr-report), §§ I–III, X.)

One allegation was that Foster had died elsewhere and his body had been moved. A forensic examiner working for Starr’s office reviewed photographs, clothing and physical samples. The report describes blood beneath the body and marks on nearby vegetation interpreted as blood spatter. Investigators reported finding no signs of dragging on the clothing. They also found residue in soil from where Foster was found. Each observation has limits: a photograph can preserve a pattern without establishing what the substance was, and an absence of marks on clothing is not proof that it could not have been moved. Taken together with the medical evidence and the condition of the scene, the findings support the conclusion that he died in the park. The report also describes how the medical and physical evidence supported suicide independently of the dispute over his papers.

The writing found in Foster’s briefcase received separate scrutiny. It was undated and torn, and it did not explicitly refer to suicide. The FBI laboratory compared the original with known examples of his handwriting, and an outside examiner compared it with additional originals, including signed checks. Both attributed it to Foster. His family’s accounts of his distress gave that writing context, but the note could not establish where or how he died. Conversely, even if the note were set aside, the scene and medical evidence would still have to be explained. ([Independent counsel’s report](https://archive.org/details/vincent-foster-jr-report), §§ IX–X.)

## The dispute over his office

Access to Foster’s office and files was a real dispute. A 1996 Senate special committee majority report concluded that officials improperly restricted investigators’ access and described Clinton personal files being moved first to the White House residence and then to private counsel. In the same report, the minority argued that investigators were ultimately able to see the documents they asked for and that the Park Police inquiry into the death was not impeded. The difference was practical. A law-enforcement officer could ask to see an item White House counsel had described, but could not inspect the office independently under that arrangement. The minority emphasized that requests were met; the majority questioned what officials might never have been in a position to ask for. ([Senate Report 104-280](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/CRPT-104srpt280/html/CRPT-104srpt280.htm), majority findings on Foster’s office and minority discussion of the Park Police inquiry.)

It is possible to find the handling of the papers troubling without treating it as forensic evidence about a death. To turn a restricted review into evidence of murder, one would need to show what was withheld and how it bears on the alleged killing. A dispute over access does not itself supply the contents of a file or establish why its holder restricted it.

## The people the list leaves out

Suppose someone assembled a list of five former campaign employees who died in the ten years after an election. Five deaths among forty people would invite a different initial judgment from five among four thousand. If the group includes elderly advisers as well as young interns, the expected number changes again. A usable comparison needs a rule identifying who belongs in the group, how long each person was observed and which kinds of deaths count. It also needs a comparison with people of similar ages over a similar period. The early published lists do not provide that information, so the count alone cannot establish that deaths were unusually common. Even if a comparison did show an excess, its cause would remain to be investigated. Several deaths in one shared accident, for example, would raise the count without supplying several independent indications of wrongdoing.

Timing matters in another way. If researchers start with all staff from a certain campaign and later examine their records, they know how many people met the rule before anyone died. If they start with a death and search until they find a connection, they have no comparable rule. An official counted because she worked for the president, a Marine counted because the president used his aircraft, and a person counted through a friend of an adviser do not form a population one could reliably reconstruct. Increasing the number of entries may reflect nothing more than extending the search.

The list also changes the unit being counted. In 1996, Commerce Secretary Ron Brown died in a military plane crash in Croatia. Contemporary coverage of the Air Force investigation described interacting crew and navigation failures. Listing Brown alone gives the reader one apparently targeted victim; examining the crash means accounting for a single event involving a crew and a delegation. Any claim of deliberate interference has to explain something about the flight and the evidence investigators examined. Brown’s proximity to a president cannot do that. ([CNN coverage of the Air Force findings, 7 June 1996](https://edition.cnn.com/ALLPOLITICS/1996/news/9606/07/brown.crash/index.shtml).)

## The next name

The list’s structure makes new entries easy to add. After Jeffrey Epstein died in custody in 2019, President Donald Trump shared a post alleging a Clinton connection. The post claimed Epstein was on suicide watch when he died. In fact, as the later inspector general’s investigation records, he had been taken off that watch weeks earlier. The jail’s failure to provide a cellmate and conduct required rounds remains serious. It explains why custody deserves scrutiny, without providing evidence that the Clintons caused his death. ([NBC News, August 2019](https://www.nbcnews.com/politics/donald-trump/trump-retweets-epstein-conspiracy-theory-claiming-clinton-connection-n1041146); [inspector general’s report](https://oig.justice.gov/sites/default/files/reports/23-085.pdf), pp. i–iv.)

The same demand for evidence applies to [[seth-rich]]. The records of the DNC email theft and the records of his killing concern different events. A theory connecting them needs evidence of a transfer by Rich and evidence that it motivated his killer. Leaving the murder unresolved does not fill in either link.

Set the names side by side and the differences matter more than the resemblance. Foster’s death has a forensic account; Rich’s killing and the dispute over access to Foster’s files pose different questions. Adding the names together does not help answer any of them.
