Shorten the vibrating length of a string by half, keeping its tension and material unchanged, and its fundamental tone rises an octave. Shorten it to two-thirds, and the tone rises a fifth. A ruler can help reproduce a relationship the ear already recognizes.

This is the attraction of the Pythagorean tradition. A pleasure that seems to belong to hearing has an intelligible structure. Different instruments can share a relationship because the relationship survives a change of material. From there it is tempting to ask whether the rest of the world has a comparable order: whether number explains how things belong together, and whether understanding that order might help a person live well.

Pythagoras became the figure around whom these hopes gathered. He also accumulated achievements, travels and miracles that were recorded long after his death. An account that gives him all of them leaves little room for the people who developed Pythagorean thought, or for the discoveries that complicated it. His followers developed ideas that could also put their inheritance under pressure.

## A teacher before a textbook

Pythagoras came from Samos and established his following at Croton, a Greek city in southern Italy, around 530 BCE. No securely authentic work of his survives. His approximate dates, often given as 570–490 BCE, help locate him; they cannot supply the missing account of his teaching. The detailed surviving lives belong to writers many centuries later. A modern biography assembled from their most vivid passages can sound unusually complete because it has combined stories that never formed a consistent record.

A few verses by his contemporary Xenophanes survive through the later writer Diogenes Laertius. They describe a man, identified in their transmitted setting as Pythagoras, intervening when a puppy is beaten. He recognizes a friend's soul in its cry. The anecdote is commonly read as mockery. It gives us an early association with the passage of souls between human and animal bodies, without establishing that this encounter happened. [The surviving fragment](https://users.manchester.edu/Facstaff/SSNaragon/Ancient%20Philosophy/Texts/Texts.htm) is evidence for a reputation as well as a belief.

The belief could make ordinary conduct harder to excuse. If the creature being struck might carry a human soul, the distinction between a neighbor and an animal becomes less secure. Even someone who rejects rebirth can see how the belief might change the meaning of eating, sacrifice or cruelty. This differs from treating immortality as a reassuring opinion about one's own future. It can make demands on conduct now.

Heraclitus, another early critic, names Pythagoras while complaining that extensive learning does not confer understanding. Neither critic is interested in granting him every subsequent discovery. They show someone whose learning and religious claims attracted attention. They give much less support to the familiar portrait of a mathematician presenting a settled system of geometry, astronomy and metaphysics.

That portrait becomes elaborate in Iamblichus' *On the Pythagorean Life*, written in late antiquity. Candidates undergo prolonged testing, including five years of silence, while their property is held in common. Iamblichus describes education as a reshaping of the pupil’s character. A pupil must learn to govern speech and desire before being entrusted with knowledge. That is his ideal of education; its elaborate rules cannot simply be treated as a register kept at Croton eight centuries earlier.

Later [[secret-societies]] could adopt this picture as an ideal. That is a possible history of reception, quite different from showing that every subsequent initiation system descended institutionally from Pythagoras. Even the categories of listeners and mathematical students need care: Iamblichus also preserves a dispute about which group represented the teacher's authentic philosophy. A disagreement over inheritance has often been smoothed into a tidy ladder of grades.

## What the string can show

The musical example becomes clearer if we give the strings actual lengths. Imagine two otherwise identical strings with the same tension. One has a vibrating length of sixty centimeters, the other thirty. In the ideal string model, the shorter string's fundamental frequency is twice the longer one's. If the first produces 200 vibrations per second, the second produces 400. That frequency relationship is an octave.

Use forty centimeters for the shorter length instead. Its frequency becomes 300 vibrations per second, giving a ratio of 3:2 against the sixty-centimeter string. At forty-five centimeters it becomes about 266.7, a ratio of 4:3. These are the intervals called a fifth and a fourth. Length ratios and frequency ratios run in opposite directions: shortening the string raises the pitch. This is a modern worked illustration, not a recovered transcript of an ancient experiment.

Change the thickness or tension, however, and the comparison changes. Real strings also depart from the ideal model. What can be repeated is a relationship under specified conditions. [The modern equation](https://openstax.org/books/university-physics-volume-1/pages/16-6-standing-waves-and-resonance) makes those conditions explicit: frequency depends on vibrating length, tension, and mass per unit length.

A late discovery story makes an instructive mistake. In [Iamblichus' account, chapter 26](https://theosophylib.com/ksg/iamblichus-life-of-pythagoras/), Pythagoras hears consonant hammers, weighs them, and then hangs weights from equal strings. Weights of twelve and six are supposed to produce an octave. But for otherwise equal strings the frequency varies with the square root of the tension. Doubling the hanging weight multiplies the frequency by the square root of two. An octave requires four times the tension.

The story has preserved the right musical numbers while attaching them to an arrangement that will not yield the stated result. The error sits in the apparatus. A narrator who knew the ratios could construct a convincing discovery scene without having tested its apparatus. The proposed experiment lets a reader locate the error.

A musician can tune by ear. Ratios let the musician relate one interval to another through calculation. Ascend a fifth, then a fourth, and the frequency ratios multiply: 3/2 × 4/3 = 2. The two intervals complete an octave. A calculation connects experiences that otherwise might seem to require separate acts of judgment.

It does not follow that every pleasing sound reduces to these few ratios, or that a mathematical account of frequency exhausts musical experience. Rhythm, timbre, expectation and the use of a sound in a piece pose further questions. Even within this small example, the physical relationship, its perception, and its musical value deserve separate attention. An account of intervals leaves plenty for a musician to understand.

## What the theorem proves

The theorem bearing Pythagoras' name says that in a right triangle, the square on the longest side has the same area as the squares on the other two sides together. A triangle with sides three, four and five illustrates it: 9 + 16 = 25. A triangle with sides five, twelve and thirteen gives another instance: 25 + 144 = 169.

Long before Pythagoras, Babylonian scribes worked with such number relationships. The clay tablet [Plimpton 322](https://isaw.nyu.edu/exhibitions/before-pythagoras/items/plimpton-322/), now at Columbia University, preserves sophisticated examples from the Old Babylonian period. It gives the history an independent material anchor. Arguments continue over the tablet's exact purpose, but the earlier knowledge does not depend on an account of a Greek visitor bringing it home.

The familiar response is that Pythagoras supplied the proof rather than the relationship. That would be a significant distinction, if we possessed a reliable account of his proof. We do not. Late attributions cannot fill in its missing steps. Nor does evidence of Egyptian or Babylonian mathematics establish the detailed itinerary sometimes assigned to him. Older knowledge and personal transmission are different historical questions.

A rearrangement of four triangles shows what a proof adds. Put four copies of the same right triangle inside a square whose side is the sum of the triangle's two shorter sides, a and b. Place one triangle in each corner, with its right angle at that corner, turning each successive copy a quarter turn. The hypotenuses border a central square with side c. The outer square has area (a + b)². The four triangles together occupy 2ab. Subtracting them leaves a² + b² for the central square, whose area is also c².

The arrangement works because each central side is a hypotenuse and each central angle is a right angle: the two acute angles of a right triangle add to ninety degrees. Those geometric facts justify the picture. Drawing something that looks square would not be enough.

This is one way to demonstrate the theorem, included here to explain what a proof contributes. Checking three-four-five and five-twelve-thirteen triangles establishes two cases. The argument establishes why the relationship holds for any right triangle to which the construction applies. It neither relies on the precision of a ruler nor gets stronger when a famous name is attached to it.

## The diagonal that resists counting

Now draw a square with sides of length one. Its diagonal cuts it into two right triangles, so the theorem gives a diagonal whose square is two. We write its length as the square root of two. Can that length be expressed exactly as a ratio of whole numbers?

Suppose it can, and write the ratio p/q in lowest terms, with no factor shared by p and q. Squaring gives p² = 2q². Therefore p² is even, and p must be even as well: the square of an odd number is odd. Write p as 2r. Substitution gives 4r² = 2q², or q² = 2r². So q is even too.

Both p and q would be divisible by two. That contradicts the decision to use a fraction in lowest terms. The assumption fails: no ratio of whole numbers equals the square root of two.

In modern usage the diagonal has an irrational number as its length. Calling it a number does not undo the result. It extends what we permit that word to name beyond whole numbers and their ratios. A claim that everything is number changes its meaning when the meaning of number changes; a modern restatement can quietly remove the difficulty an older claim faced.

The result changes what measurement can mean. The side and diagonal do not have a common unit that fits into each a whole number of times, however small the proposed unit becomes. This is incommensurability. The diagonal has a perfectly definite length; the difficulty belongs to the proposed way of expressing it.

A decimal such as 1.414 can approximate that length as closely as a practical job requires. The proof addresses the exact relationship. No improvement in instruments can turn the approximation into a fraction that the argument has shown impossible. A discovery about the limits of counting has been made through reasoning about an ordinary square.

Later stories connect Hippasus with a forbidden disclosure and death at sea. Iamblichus preserves variants involving incommensurable quantities, a geometrical solid, expulsion and divine punishment. The variation makes a confident account of either the discovery or a murder untenable. Nor can we supply a happy ending in which the school simply expanded its number system and solved the trouble. The mathematical result survives independently of these stories.

The square supplies a correction that the discovery legend can obscure. If an account of order requires every magnitude to be a ratio of whole numbers, a square is enough to defeat it. Mathematical inquiry can expose a limit in a mathematical worldview.

## From a scale to a cosmos

Philolaus, active in the fifth century BCE, gives the tradition a more definite philosophical voice. Fragments of his work describe the world through things that limit and things that are unlimited, fitted together by *harmonia*. A scale offers an example: a range of possible pitches acquires a determinate arrangement through particular intervals. In another fragment, knowing a thing requires number. [The fragments and their interpretation](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/philolaus/) leave room for disagreements about exactly how strong that claim is.

It is possible to hear two proposals in this account. We understand a thing by grasping its measurable arrangement. Or the thing is, in its deepest nature, that arrangement. The second proposal needs an argument beyond the success of the first. The ratio shared by two differently built instruments does not tell us everything about either instrument.

Aristotle's [*Metaphysics*, I.5–6](https://classics.mit.edu/Aristotle/metaphysics.1.i.html) reports Pythagoreans treating number as a principle of things and finding numerical relationships in music and the heavens. He also distinguishes their position from Plato's separation of mathematical principles from sensible things. Calling both views mathematical Platonism conceals a disagreement that mattered to their ancient critic. The slogan “all is number” cannot do the work of specifying which view is intended.

A triangular arrangement of ten dots in rows of one, two, three and four, associated with the *tetractys*, makes the attraction of symbolic number easy to see. The rows make the sum 1 + 2 + 3 + 4 visible, and those four integers supply the ratios just examined. A compact figure can hold several relations in memory. Moving from these properties to a complete account of justice, the soul or the heavens requires further choices about what the numbers represent. Those choices deserve to remain visible in [[sacred-geometry]] as well.

Philolaus' astronomical system moves the Earth around a central fire, with the Sun also orbiting that center. This dislodges the Earth from the middle, but it is not heliocentrism. Its unfamiliarity is worth preserving: an ancient thinker could challenge one inherited assumption while keeping others that a modern astronomer would reject. Calling him an early Copernicus makes the proposal easier to praise and harder to understand.

Aristotle supplies an uncomfortable example of number guiding the heavens too firmly. According to his report, the Pythagoreans added a counter-earth to complete ten moving celestial bodies, the number they regarded as perfect. This is a critic's account of their reasoning. Taken on its own terms, it shows how a preference can become a demand made of the world: if nine bodies are available, a tenth must exist.

The proposed harmony of the heavens was also supposed to make a sound. In [*On the Heavens*, II.9](https://classics.mit.edu/Aristotle/heavens.2.ii.html), Aristotle describes the reply to an obvious objection. We fail to hear the music because it has accompanied us from birth; familiarity hides it. He rejects the theory. We need not accept his own celestial physics to recognize the problem with an explanation that turns an absent sound into evidence of uninterrupted listening.

Changing a specified string length yields a result someone else can check. A cosmic analogy must earn that same confidence through evidence suited to the heavens. The pleasure of finding a shared pattern can motivate inquiry. It can also make an imagined order difficult to surrender.

## What harmony says about a soul

The religious and mathematical interests meet sharply in Plato's *Phaedo*. Simmias suggests that a soul might resemble the harmony of a lyre: invisible and beautiful, yet dependent on the instrument's arrangement. Break the instrument and the harmony disappears, although the wood and strings remain. The suggestion challenges the hope that the soul survives the body. [Socrates argues against it](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/1658/1658-h/1658-h.htm); the dialogue does not simply endorse a Pythagorean doctrine.

One reply turns on something Simmias has already accepted: that learning is recollection and that the soul therefore existed before this life. A harmony, Socrates observes, comes after the instrument and its strings have been arranged. It cannot exist before its ingredients. Simmias cannot consistently keep both accounts. This reply has force within their conversation because he accepts the earlier argument. A reader who rejects recollection will still need another reason to reject his picture of bodily dependence.

Harmony can carry an argument in more than one direction. It can suggest something independent and enduring: the same proportion can recur in many instruments. It can also suggest dependence: this performance ends when this instrument is destroyed. An analogy supplies both possibilities. Choosing between them requires an account of what a soul is and what its relation to a body actually involves.

The passage remains relevant to [[consciousness]], though the ancient question about a soul and the modern study of experience are not interchangeable. A stable relationship can outlast its physical instance without preserving that particular instance's experience. The difference between a reproducible arrangement and this particular life remains to be explained. A musical analogy helps state that problem. It cannot settle it.

Pythagorean thought holds mathematical inquiry and a disciplined life close together. It loses little when its founder ceases to be responsible for all later mathematics. A proportion can be heard, a geometric relationship proved, a way of treating animals reconsidered. Each asks for a different kind of attention. The tradition becomes most demanding at the point where reverence for its teacher must allow the inquiry he inspired to change the answer.
