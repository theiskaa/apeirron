One entry in the report on Georgia’s November 2003 parliamentary election concerns a thousand votes. At an overseas polling station in Lughansk, the original protocol, the formal record of the count, gave the Industrialists party one vote. The final protocol gave them 1,001. The OSCE’s election observers included the discrepancy in their account of the results process.

A thousand votes appeared between two versions of a record. Whoever paid for an observer’s training, that change still needs an explanation.

Foreign assistance was part of the movements known as the color revolutions. So were grievances that foreign assistance did not invent. Understanding their relationship means following money and following the count, without allowing either investigation to replace the other. A grant can explain how an organization acquired equipment or trained volunteers. It cannot, by itself, tell us whether an election result was false. Evidence that a result was false cannot tell us whether the organization challenging it was free of outside influence.

Georgia’s Rose Revolution in 2003 and Ukraine’s Orange Revolution in 2004 brought these questions together. In both, organized opposition challenged an election result, and pressure outside official institutions helped change what happened inside them. The shared methods attracted attention: youth groups, memorable symbols, election monitoring, sustained demonstrations and help from abroad. The repeated organizations and techniques suggest an appealing explanation: a change of government arranged elsewhere, with the crowd supplying its public face.

To judge that explanation, we have to know what a sponsor could decide.

## The election before the revolution

Georgia’s crisis began with a parliamentary election on November 2, 2003. President Eduard Shevardnadze’s authority was at stake, but voters were not choosing his successor in that contest. The presidential vote came later, after the parliamentary dispute had forced a change of leadership.

The observation mission described systematic fraud, especially in Adjara and Kvemo Kartli, and a results process that disregarded voters’ choices. It also recorded domestic monitoring and meaningful opportunities for political participation. The country had enough public competition for people to challenge the result, while the machinery meant to record that competition failed them. The [observers’ account](https://www.osce.org/files/f/documents/2/0/22205.pdf) describes political space that was being contested and abused.

As protests developed, the opposition itself was divided. Several parties did not join the demonstrations. Protesters prevented the new parliament from convening on November 22; Shevardnadze resigned the following day. On November 25 the Supreme Court annulled the proportional part of the election. The annulment did not erase every constituency result. A presidential election followed on January 4, 2004, with a separate repeat parliamentary vote to come.

A parliamentary election can combine a national contest between party lists with separate constituency contests. Invalidating one part does not automatically decide the other. Voters who rejected a national tally could therefore be left with a parliament containing constituency winners whose results remained in force. The dispute left more to resolve than who had won a national tally. Shevardnadze’s resignation required a new presidential election while the parliamentary results still had to be addressed.

Consider the position of a Georgian voter who believed the count had been falsified. The existence of a foreign-funded monitoring group would create a legitimate question about the group’s interests. It would not oblige that voter to accept a fabricated protocol. Nor would anger at fraud settle whether entering parliament was justified, which opposition leader deserved office, or how a later government should govern. A movement can have a strong claim about one wrong and an uncertain claim to the authority it gains by resisting it.

## What the grant record actually shows

Donors published accounts of some of the assistance. These give a more useful starting point than the inference that similar flags must have the same sponsor.

The National Endowment for Democracy’s [2004 annual report](https://www.ned.org/docs/annual/2004AnnualReport.pdf), on its printed page 62, lists $42,296 for Georgia’s Liberty Institute. Its stated purpose included training youth activists through Kmara, with a camp, a manual and other materials. This report covers the year after the Rose Revolution. It describes work with an established movement; it cannot serve as a complete budget for the November 2003 protests.

A grant description can establish a recipient, an amount and an intended activity. Following the relationship further would mean examining how money was disbursed, who selected participants, what training involved and which decisions remained with the recipient. An annual report usually gives much less information about those choices than about the purpose the donor wishes to advertise.

NED describes itself as a private nonprofit supported through a congressional appropriation. Its [own explanation](https://www.ned.org/about/faqs/) emphasizes that its nongovernmental status permits work in politically difficult settings, including with groups reluctant to receive money directly from the US government. That arrangement puts distance between an organization and an embassy without making the public source of its money disappear.

It also creates an ambiguity for recipients. A group may gain the means to challenge its government while becoming dependent on a funder whose priorities its members do not control. Imagine an organization offered support for election monitoring but little for investigating the donor country’s favored investor. No instruction to falsify anything is necessary. The difference in available resources can affect which work becomes possible. Whether that happened in a particular organization would depend on its funding choices and the work it undertook.

The relevant US statute [prohibits NED funds from financing candidates’ campaigns](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/22/4414). That restriction gives an investigation something specific to test. It does not prove that every program complied, or that training an organization has no political consequences. Election monitoring can alter the balance between an incumbent who controls election administration and an opposition that needs an independent account of the result.

Calling all such work neutral understates its effects. Calling it all a covert operation erases differences that determine how it can be scrutinized. A published grant, an undisclosed instruction and a fabricated result leave different records and carry different responsibilities. The history of [[operation-ajax]] gives ample reason to take foreign intervention seriously. It does not supply the missing instructions for every later uprising.

## A poll is a different kind of evidence

The phrase “independent count” can conceal several activities. An exit poll asks people how they voted after leaving a polling station. A parallel vote tabulation collects results recorded at stations and independently aggregates them; depending on its design, it may cover a sample rather than every station. Examining a disputed protocol compares particular records. A parallel tabulation can reproduce an error already present in the station results, even while correctly detecting changes introduced later. A sample also needs a design that lets it say something about the places it did not cover. Having observers at many stations is not equivalent to independently recounting every ballot.

Suppose, as a simple example, a polling station counts 600 valid votes: 330 for one candidate and 270 for another. Observers retain a copy of the signed result. The district later reports the totals in reverse. The comparison identifies a discrepancy in the transfer of that station’s result. Observers can present the two records, and officials can be required to explain which is authentic and how the change occurred.

Now suppose an exit poll estimates that the first candidate won 55 percent nationally, while official results give that candidate 45 percent. The gap is a serious reason to investigate. It does not locate the error. The poll could have missed particular voters, encountered unequal willingness to answer, or been conducted badly. The official count could have been falsified. Both sets of data could contain problems. Examining the sample, questionnaires, response patterns and station records can distinguish possibilities that the headline comparison cannot.

The sponsor is relevant to that examination. A funder might favor a particular result; a pollster might face pressure to produce it. But checking a poll requires more than identifying a motive. A government that dismisses an unfavorable poll because of its funding should still answer discrepancies in its own records. A donor defending its poll should make its methods open to examination.

This matters in Ukraine because NED’s own 2004 report went beyond describing assistance. Its account of the Democratic Initiatives Foundation’s exit polling called the data “incontrovertible evidence” of fraud. That is a donor’s confident assessment of work it supported. The polling helped challenge the official account, but the adjective asks a statistical instrument to carry more certainty than it can supply on its own.

An election challenge becomes stronger when different records converge. A poll, a station protocol, an observer’s account and a judicial hearing answer different questions. Agreement among them is more informative than several organizations repeating the same poll. Counting the number of institutions endorsing an allegation tells us little if all their claims come from one source.

## What changed the Ukrainian result

Ukraine’s 2004 presidential contest centered on opposition candidate Viktor Yushchenko and incumbent prime minister Viktor Yanukovych. After a first round on October 31, the November 21 runoff produced a disputed result and mass protests. Unlike Georgia’s initial contest, this election directly determined who would become president.

The OSCE mission’s [final report](https://cdn.osce.org/sites/default/files/f/documents/5/f/14673.pdf) describes problems extending beyond a disagreement between an exit poll and the announced totals. These included pressure on public employees and students, misuse of official resources and heavily biased coverage. Voters’ ability to choose could be impaired before a ballot reached the box.

The decisive legal step came on December 3. Following a five-day hearing, the Supreme Court invalidated the central election commission’s result because the electorate’s will could not be established. The remedy was a repeat runoff on December 26. The judgment required another vote rather than awarding the presidency on the basis of an exit poll. Election rules and the commission’s composition changed before the repeat, which the observers assessed as substantially closer to the required standards.

A court can reject a count without knowing what an accurate one would have shown. An opposition can establish that the declared result is unreliable without establishing its own total. Repeating the contest gives the electorate another opportunity to answer that question. It also creates another contest to scrutinize, rather than turning the movement’s claim into an exemption from scrutiny.

Street pressure mattered to the political situation in which these decisions were made. An account confined to court procedure would leave out the demonstrators and the authorities’ response. The court was also an institution whose reasoning and procedures could be examined. Yanukovych challenged the repeat election, which Yushchenko won. The observers criticized the apparent public disclosure of the court’s rejection before its official announcement. That procedural failure deserved criticism even though they judged the repeat vote an improvement.

A foreign government may see an electoral dispute as an opportunity to strengthen a preferred political alignment. It may offer assistance selectively, treat similar abuses differently elsewhere, or prefer an opposition figure for reasons unrelated to voting rights. People receiving that assistance do not have to share every aim of the sponsor. Their temporary agreement can be enough to sustain a relationship.

The leverage in such a relationship lies partly in what happens when the parties disagree. A donor able to withdraw nearly all an organization’s resources occupies a different position from one funding a small project among several. A recipient with members, local income and alternative supporters has more room to refuse. Grant conditions and decisions made during a disagreement can reveal that balance much more clearly than a list of people who have met.

## How a method travels

The movements did borrow from one another. Political scientist Mark Beissinger’s [study of the revolutionary wave](https://mbeissinger.scholar.princeton.edu/sites/g/files/toruqf2826/files/mbeissinger/files/beissinger.modularrevolution.pdf) describes Georgian activists visiting Serbia in 2003 and receiving help from veterans of Otpor, the movement associated with the challenge to Slobodan Milošević. His account follows the circulation of tactics, training and symbols into later movements, including Ukraine’s Pora. The common features had real channels of transmission.

Borrowing a method can be consequential without placing the borrower under the teacher’s command. A movement may learn how to recruit, communicate and maintain a presence in public. It still has to persuade people that joining is worth their time and danger. People may accept a tactic while disagreeing about candidates, policy or what to do after the incumbent leaves.

Gene Sharp’s *[From Dictatorship to Democracy](https://www.nonviolent-conflict.org/wp-content/uploads/2016/03/FDTD7.pdf)* explains why organization matters. His account treats political authority as dependent on cooperation: officials administer, workers supply skills, institutions transmit orders and people obey. Nonviolent resistance attempts to interrupt that cooperation. The book offers practical strategic advice, while placing the main burden on domestic action and describing foreign assistance as a supplement.

Its central problem is therefore larger than how to make a good logo. A symbol may help people recognize an opposition and see that others share it. Sustained action requires arrangements through which that recognition becomes cooperation: somebody must convey information, make decisions and support people facing reprisals. Consider a public employee asked to join a stoppage. Agreement with the demand may be easy; deciding whether colleagues will join, whether a dismissal can be challenged and how to meet next month’s expenses is harder. A campaign that cannot answer those practical concerns may have a widely recognized symbol and very few people able to act on it. The techniques of promotion discussed in [[bernays]] illuminate part of this work, but do not explain how every organization functions.

Writing about a 2001 visit to Serbia with Sharp, Albert Einstein Institution staff member [Chris Miller](https://peacemagazine.org/archive/v17n4p14.htm) described divisions between Otpor and some larger NGOs. His report also recalled separate campaigning approaches: an aggressive anti-Milošević effort and a broader effort to get people to vote. Miller was an advocate of the methods, reporting conversations after the event. Even that sympathetic account describes internal choices and disagreements, rather than a perfectly synchronized organization.

Opponents can learn the same tactics and prepare for them. Beissinger’s analysis includes the way incumbent governments adapted to the revolutionary wave. Success in one place can encourage activists elsewhere while warning the government they face. A recognizable movement may find it easier to attract attention and harder to organize without interference. Copying a successful campaign may therefore reproduce its most recognizable features while losing the advantage it once had.

## The movement after it wins

Removing an incumbent leaves a second problem: limiting the people who replace him. A protest can unite people around a count they reject while concealing disagreements about the government they want. Once the immediate demand is met, those disagreements become unavoidable.

Georgia’s January 2004 presidential election brought Mikheil Saakashvili to office. The OSCE’s [report on that election](https://cdn.osce.org/sites/default/files/f/documents/a/e/24600.pdf) described improvements over November and also criticized the absence of a genuinely competitive environment, political imbalance in election commissions and misuse of administrative resources. International approval was qualified from the start. A popular victory did not answer every question about the conditions in which opponents could compete.

In Ukraine in 2010, a later election allowed voters to reject the political camp that had prevailed in 2004. The final runoff was between Yulia Tymoshenko and Yanukovych, the candidate associated with the disputed 2004 result. This time Yanukovych won. The OSCE’s [2010 assessment](https://cdn.osce.org/sites/default/files/f/documents/e/9/67844.pdf) found that the election met most commitments and offered voters a genuine choice, while recording problems including politically influenced media coverage and inadequate complaint handling.

Yanukovych’s victory leaves the history of individual grants open to investigation. It also gives an account of the Orange Revolution something to explain: the candidate it opposed could return and win an election assessed favorably by international observers. Ukrainian voters could act against the preferences of the people who had celebrated the earlier movement. That freedom says something about the election; it gives no advance judgment on how the winner would use office.

Democracy promotion deserves scrutiny over whose political activity receives resources and what dependency that support creates. Its sponsors also have to answer for how they respond when an election goes against their wishes. The standard should be more durable than a preference for whichever candidate happens to be challenging an unfriendly government.

The voter whose choice disappears in a falsified count has a claim against the incumbent. The same voter has a claim against the leaders who gain power by challenging it. Accepting the first claim should never require surrendering the second.
