When a federal court sentenced Lawrence E. King Jr. to fifteen years in prison in 1991, the case concerned money missing from an Omaha credit union. By then, two grand juries had separately considered claims that children had been sexually abused in a network connected to King. Neither supported the broad accusation. Years later, a man who alleged abuse obtained a civil judgment against King. The judgment was entered by default, without King defending himself against the claim.

## The missing money and the foster-care file

Federal regulators closed the Franklin Community Federal Credit Union in November 1988. Franklin had been formed to serve people of modest means. Its manager, King, was prominent in Republican politics and entertained at national conventions. While the institution was operating, he could appear both as the manager of a neighborhood credit union and as someone with access to a political world far beyond Omaha. The collapse forced an accounting of the first role and brought scrutiny to the second.

The eventual criminal result was clear. King pleaded guilty to embezzlement and other offenses and was sentenced to fifteen years. Contemporary sentencing coverage put the disappearance at about $39 million. This established a large financial crime. To show that some of the money paid for organized abuse, an inquiry would have to connect particular expenses or transfers to particular acts. The size of the loss could explain why legislators opened an investigation; it could not explain who was abused or where. ([Sentencing coverage, June 1991](https://web.archive.org/web/20231217112354/https://www.orlandosentinel.com/1991/06/19/ex-credit-union-manager-gets-15-years-for-fraud/).)

There was a separate reason for the Nebraska Legislature to investigate. In December 1988, the *New York Times* reported that officials from the state Foster Care Review Board had turned over a file to the legislature’s Executive Board in a closed meeting. People familiar with it described reports gathered over roughly two years from schoolteachers, social workers and others responsible for children’s welfare. The file concerned abuse of foster children; the legislative inquiry would examine whether reports of child sexual abuse were connected to the failed credit union. ([Contemporary account of the handover](https://web.archive.org/web/20120518054957/http://www.nytimes.com/1988/12/25/us/nebraska-inquiry-is-given-file-on-sex-abuse-of-foster-children.html).)

That chronology rules out a simple account in which every underlying complaint started with publicity about the credit union. It does not establish that the file described one organization. A teacher might have heard a child’s account directly; a caseworker’s note might repeat something a teacher already reported. To assess whether two reports corroborate each other, an investigator has to find out when each was made, whose account it records, and whether the two people could have learned the shared detail from the same source. The file gave the legislature a reason to ask those questions. The contemporary article, based on people familiar with a closed file, cannot answer them entry by entry.

## The county and federal grand juries

Accounts gathered by the legislative committee described abuse at parties allegedly tied to King. Names and excerpts from testimony then made their way into public reporting. A memorandum from former state senator John DeCamp named people he considered central to the inquiry, and a local television station obtained videotaped testimony. A new witness now had more than one way to learn names or details that had once been known to only a few people. That change makes the timing of accounts more important; it does not prove that any particular witness copied one.

In July 1990, the Douglas County grand jury called the broad allegations a “carefully crafted hoax.” Its public report, quoted at the time, said it had found no credible evidence for the alleged ring and exonerated the five people named in DeCamp’s memorandum. It suggested the hoax had been scripted by people familiar with Omaha and did not name who had supposedly scripted it. Two witnesses recanted accounts they had given to the legislative inquiry; other witnesses stood by theirs. The committee’s chairman, Loran Schmit, disputed the county jury’s conclusion. ([July report coverage](https://web.archive.org/web/20110622040350/http://www.nytimes.com/1990/07/29/us/omaha-grand-jury-sees-hoax-in-lurid-tales.html); [*Chicago Tribune*, August 1990](https://web.archive.org/web/20231217112354/https://www.chicagotribune.com/news/ct-xpm-1990-08-27-9003120126-story.html).)

A federal grand jury reached its own conclusion in September 1990 that the allegations of a sexual-abuse and drug operation were unfounded. It also indicted Alisha Owen for perjury after she told the legislative committee she had been abused. That was a federal accusation, not a conviction. In a separate state prosecution, Owen was convicted on eight counts of perjury. The Nebraska Court of Appeals later upheld the denial of her second motion for a new trial. The conviction is a finding about the statements at issue in the state case. It does not establish what happened to other children whose cases were in the foster-care file. ([Federal grand jury coverage, September 1990](https://web.archive.org/web/20110604234214/http://www.nytimes.com/1990/09/27/us/omaha-tales-of-sexual-abuse-ruled-false.html); [*State v. Owen*](https://case-law.vlex.com/vid/state-v-owen-no-893740889).)

## What the crash record shows

The committee’s investigator, Gary Caradori, died with his eight-year-old son when their plane crashed near Ashton, Illinois, on 11 July 1990. The county grand jury's public rejection of the allegations followed later that month. The committee lost an investigator who could have explained how he obtained the interviews and what he had been able to check. The proximity of the crash and the report helps explain why people suspected interference. It does not identify the cause of the crash.

The Lee County sheriff’s records describe an aerial search that found debris roughly half to three-quarters of a mile from the impact site. Local officers worked with federal aviation investigators to secure and survey the area. At the inquest, the coroner relayed an FAA specialist’s assessment: the structure had been overloaded, with no evidence that it had weakened through fatigue. This describes how a structure failed without identifying what put it under that load. The coroner also read a medical finding that the examination of the pilot disclosed no signs suggesting an explosion in flight. These are specific findings from the records available here, not a reconstruction of the entire flight or a substitute for a final aviation report. ([Lee County sheriff’s supplementary report and coroner’s inquest](https://archive.org/details/GaryCaradori), case 90-221 and inquest pp. 29–31.)

Later accounts treated the crash as the loss of evidence that would have proved the case. A record of what Caradori had collected would help establish what was lost. A separate investigation would have to establish that someone interfered with the plane. Neither can be inferred from the fact that he died before he could finish the work. The available crash records give no sound basis for treating his death as corroboration of the abuse allegations.

## One plaintiff, one default

Paul Bonacci alleged that King had subjected him to repeated sexual abuse. He pursued a civil case with DeCamp as his lawyer. Judge Warren Urbom entered a default judgment against King in 1999. The *Executive Intelligence Review*, which had championed the wider allegations, published excerpts from the ruling. It quoted the judge as treating the allegations as true as to King because of the default, and reported awards of $800,000 in compensation and $200,000 in punitive damages. The appellate docket identifies the underlying district court case and records the ensuing appeal, but does not reproduce the trial court’s full decision. ([Appellate docket, No. 99-2208](https://archive.org/download/gov.uscourts.ca8.99-2208/gov.uscourts.ca8.99-2208.docket.html); [published excerpts and award figures](https://larouchepub.com/eiw/public/1999/eirv26n12-19990319/eirv26n12-19990319.pdf), printed pp. 65–66.)

A default judgment can provide real legal relief without resolving disputed facts the way a contested trial does. Under the federal rules, failing to deny an allegation when a response is required generally admits it for the case; the amount of damages is treated separately. A court can take evidence to determine what to award. So a judge hearing evidence of harm after default does not mean King cross-examined witnesses and a jury then decided whose account to believe. Nor does a judgment against King establish the liability of anyone else Bonacci named. ([Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 8](https://www.law.cornell.edu/rules/frcp/rule_8); [Rule 55](https://www.law.cornell.edu/rules/frcp/rule_55).)

No contradiction requires us to discard either record. The broad criminal inquiry did not find support for a network it could charge, while a narrower civil claim obtained a judgment against a man who did not defend it. Compare the contested Maxwell trial discussed in [[epstein]]: the government had to prove the charges to a jury, and Maxwell had the opportunity to challenge the evidence. The process by which each court reached its result affects what that result can support outside the case.

## What Franklin can establish

The gap in the public account is most apparent where the inquiry began, with the foster-care file. The public case became a dispute about a ring. The children who were the subjects of those reports needed someone to find out whether they were safe, even when their experiences did not fit the larger story. Checking how each report was handled would tell us something no general verdict about the scandal can: whether the adults who first received it followed up when they were in a position to help.
