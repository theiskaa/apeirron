A computer can produce an image of a city. Whether it could produce the people who live there, including their experiences of living there, is a much larger question. The simulation hypothesis begins when those two achievements are allowed to come together: a world is computed, and some of the processes within it are conscious observers.

From their point of view, the city would have streets, weather, neighbors, and a history. They could investigate its physical laws without knowing what supports them. Discovering a computational implementation would change their account of what the world is made of. It would not automatically make the street they walked down disappear.

The possibility alone tells us very little about our own situation. To get from a conceivable computed city to the claim that this city is computed takes an argument. Nick Bostrom offers one based on the number of observers an advanced civilization might create. It is a more unusual argument than the familiar suggestion that physics sometimes looks like a computer game, and much more dependent on what counts as a conscious person.

## What Bostrom actually argues

Nick Bostrom's [“Are You Living in a Computer Simulation?”](https://simulation-argument.com/simulation.pdf), published in 2003, gives the hypothesis its best-known statistical form. It concerns advanced civilizations capable of running what he calls ancestor simulations: computed histories containing people sufficiently like earlier humans to count as observers with experiences like ours.

The argument assumes that a suitable computation can support consciousness. It also considers a level of technological development at which a civilization could run many such histories. Neither assumption is a discovery that present-day computers contain conscious people. They are conditions under which the counting argument becomes relevant.

Bostrom's three alternatives are roughly these: civilizations like ours almost never reach the relevant technological stage; civilizations reaching it almost never run substantial numbers of ancestor simulations; or simulated observers with experiences like ours greatly outnumber the original ones. The third possibility would give us a reason to take our own simulated status seriously, if we accept the corresponding way of locating ourselves among those observers.

The result is a conditional constraint. It does not assign one-third probability to each option. It does not show that the first two are implausible, or that improving graphics hardware makes the third inevitable. A view in which conscious simulations are impossible would reject an assumption behind the calculation rather than choose among the alternatives on its terms.

Even after granting the assumptions, the disjunction is less comforting than a confident claim that future civilizations will both thrive and simulate their ancestors. Those expectations, taken together, produce a large population of simulated observers. The interesting question is why we should expect to be among the comparatively few originals.

## A small example of a large number

Imagine ten original civilizations, each producing one comparable history of humanlike observers. One of them later runs a thousand equally populated ancestor simulations. In this simplified example there are ten original histories and a thousand simulated ones. An observer selected uniformly from the relevant people would be much more likely to belong to a simulated history.

The thousand simulations do not have to be made by a thousand different civilizations. A small number of prolific simulators could dominate the population. Conversely, a large number of advanced civilizations would contribute nothing to that simulated population if none created conscious observers of the relevant kind.

This is why the argument depends on a product of quantities: how often civilizations reach the required stage, how often they undertake the relevant simulations, and how many eligible observers the simulations contain. Large values in one part can compensate for small values in another. The details matter more than an intuition that someone, somewhere, would probably want to try it.

The example is arithmetic, not evidence that the imagined populations exist. Changing its numbers can reverse the result. If the one successful civilization runs only one small simulation, the original observers may dominate. If the simulations contain unfeeling behavioral models, they contribute no conscious observers at all. If most simulated histories differ radically from ours, the comparison requires further attention to which observers are relevant to our evidence.

Nor does the second alternative require every advanced individual in every civilization to refuse every simulation. What matters is the total population of the relevant simulated minds. Restrictions, preferences, costs, or technical obstacles might make that population small. Their prevalence is unknown; describing them as impossible to defend would make the argument more forceful than its premises allow.

A long history of technological progress does not supply those missing quantities. A trend in computing can motivate a scenario, but reaching a particular level of computation, maintaining a civilization, and choosing to create conscious ancestors are separate events. We know very little about how often they occur across civilizations.

## The observer doing the counting

Even with populations specified, a further step remains. Why should we treat ourselves as though selected from them in that particular way?

Ordinary probability calculations often have a clear sampling procedure. A lottery ticket is drawn from a defined collection. Here there is no observed machine drawing an observer from the universe. The reasoning concerns self-location: given that there are observers with experiences like these, how should an observer use that information to judge where they are?

The choice of comparison class can alter the result. Are we comparing all conscious beings, all humanlike beings, people with our apparent technological history, or moments of observation with particular features? A simulation of a world with unfamiliar physics may contain minds without contributing many observers who have our evidence. A single individual could also have many copies, raising questions about whether to count persons, histories, or observation events.

Bostrom addresses such issues within a broader framework of observation-selection effects. They are not a minor correction to a calculation otherwise grounded in a known census. They help determine what is being counted and why the resulting fraction should guide our belief.

David Kipping's [2020 Bayesian analysis](https://www.mdpi.com/2218-1997/6/8/109) illustrates how model assumptions affect the answer. Its near-even result is conditional on the analysis and its starting probabilities. It is not a measurement that our world has a roughly fifty-percent chance of being simulated. A model can clarify the consequences of assumptions without providing independent evidence that those assumptions describe reality.

Someone reading “fifty percent” may understandably imagine that half the work of detecting a simulation has been done. No such observation produced the number. Kipping is exploring what follows from a choice of models and starting probabilities. The exercise helps expose where a numerical belief comes from, but the precision of its output should not make those starting choices disappear.

## Can a computed person feel anything?

A simulation of a fire does not burn the computer. That familiar objection identifies a real distinction between modeling a phenomenon and producing it. A weather model represents a storm; it does not make the room wet. It does not follow that every process represented by computation has the same relationship to its implementation.

Some things are defined by an organization of operations. A computer implementing arithmetic performs arithmetic, rather than merely illustrating a calculation for someone else to perform. Whether consciousness belongs on that side of the distinction is the contested question. It cannot be settled simply by choosing either fire or arithmetic as the analogy.

Functionalist theories of mind emphasize the relations among inputs, internal states, and behavior. They make room for the possibility that different materials could support the same kinds of mental processes. A theory on which particular biological properties are indispensable would give a different answer. Both would still need to explain what features distinguish a conscious implementation from an ordinary representation.

Imagine a simulated patient who asks for an analgesic, describes a headache, and responds as expected when the simulated medicine arrives. The program might reproduce every observable feature of the encounter. Whether anyone suffered would still depend on what the implementation can support. That is where the [[hard-problem]] enters Bostrom's arithmetic: his multiplier counts subjects of [[consciousness]], not convincing performances of one.

There is also a difference between a person using virtual reality and a person implemented within it. A present-day headset changes the sensory input reaching a biological brain. The person remains outside the computer that supplies the scene. An ancestor simulation of the sort Bostrom discusses would have to instantiate the observer as well as provide an environment. Success at the first task does not establish that the second is possible.

A world could therefore be partly simulated without its inhabitants being digital minds. An external brain might receive computed input, or an environment could contain both users and internally implemented subjects. These scenarios raise related skeptical questions but produce different populations to count. The specific simulation argument depends on which scenario is being proposed.

The distinction also affects the word “observer.” Bostrom needs minds with genuine experiences, not just characters whose dialogue includes claims of awareness. If those experiences cannot be instantiated computationally, the population multiplier central to his argument is unavailable. If they can, the moral consequences extend to the simulators as well as to the people asking whether they are simulated.

## How much world would have to be computed?

A full physical model of a universe and a sufficiently convincing environment for a population of observers are different computational projects. Bostrom's argument concerns the latter. It does not require assuming that every particle in every distant galaxy is calculated at the finest possible resolution at every moment.

Ordinary simulations often work at different levels of detail. A model can represent an unresolved process through its effects on a larger system. Computer graphics can omit objects outside a view or substitute a simpler model at a distance. These techniques make limited simulations useful without reproducing everything their subjects represent.

A simulated civilization capable of experiments creates a harder requirement. Its observations must remain mutually consistent. An astronomer can record a spectrum; another can examine the record years later; a third can design an instrument to test an inference from it. A simulator that produces only a convincing first impression has not necessarily produced a stable world that survives these connected investigations.

“Compute only what is observed” therefore shifts a problem rather than supplying a complete architecture. What must be tracked to generate the right observations? How are records, interactions, and different observers kept consistent? Which physical processes inside an instrument matter to its result? The answers depend on the simulated laws and the level of fidelity required.

Resource limits in our physics constrain what computers in our universe can accomplish. Applying those limits to an imagined external reality adds an assumption that its physics is similar. Allowing completely unknown external laws avoids some familiar limits but also removes our basis for estimating what the simulator could do. An unrestricted possibility is difficult to rule out precisely because it gives up many of the constraints that would make it informative.

Nested simulations sharpen that issue. A simulated civilization might run simulations of its own, but each additional level would need an implementation and resources within the preceding one. Infinite layers of equally detailed worlds do not follow from the mere possibility of one simulated world. The capacity, fidelity, and number of levels have to be specified.

## Why physics is not a list of computer clues

The Planck length and Planck time are often described as a universe's pixels and refresh rate. These scales are constructed from fundamental constants and mark the regime in which familiar gravitational and quantum descriptions are expected to require a deeper account. Their existence does not establish a lattice of spatial pixels or a universal sequence of time steps.

A computed model can approximate a continuous system without the model's steps becoming observable features of the represented physics. Conversely, a physical theory with discrete elements would not by itself establish a computer outside the universe. Discreteness, computation, and simulation by an external agent are different claims.

The speed of light is similarly more than an engineer's arbitrary limit on information transfer. In relativity, the invariant speed helps define the structure of causal relations between events. Calling it a processor speed supplies an analogy after the fact. To turn the analogy into evidence, a model would need to explain its particular predictions and why they distinguish the proposed implementation from ordinary physical accounts.

Quantum measurement is another frequent candidate. It is compared with a game drawing a scene only when a player looks. But “observation” in a physical experiment need not mean a conscious person inspecting the result. Instruments and their interactions matter. Quantum correlations are not explained by saying that a programmer waited for someone to pay attention; an actual model would have to recover the quantitative predictions that experiments test.

The appeal of these comparisons is understandable. A world with mathematical laws, information limits, and observer-dependent descriptions invites the language of computation. Yet resemblance depends on which features we select from our own technology. We should expect computers designed to model physics to share some useful structures with physical theories. That relationship does not tell us which side, if either, is being implemented by an external system.

Research in digital physics can be interesting without supporting an external programmer. A proposal that physical evolution has a computational description concerns the laws themselves. The claim that those laws run as a project in another world adds a setting and often an intention. Success in the first project would not automatically demonstrate the second.

## What could an experiment test?

Silas Beane, Zohreh Davoudi, and Martin Savage examined a particular numerical scenario in [“Constraints on the Universe as a Numerical Simulation”](https://arxiv.org/abs/1210.1847). They considered a cubic spacetime lattice and the consequences of a specific discretization, using lattice calculations in particle physics as a starting point. Such an implementation can introduce preferred structures absent from an exactly rotationally symmetric continuum.

One proposed signature concerns the highest-energy cosmic rays and patterns related to the lattice's orientation. This is a consequence of a specified model, not a prediction that every simulator must leave a detectable grid. A different implementation could produce different effects or avoid the proposed signature.

A failure to find the predicted pattern could rule out versions of this lattice at particular scales. A positive result would be more intriguing, but it would first give physicists evidence about the structure of spacetime. Whether that structure was being computed elsewhere would remain a further inference. The experiment has a defined target even if its title invites a much larger story.

This makes the test more useful than a vague search for glitches. A proposed glitch can be explained away by adding intentions or powers to the simulator. A particular implementation must live with its consequences. When advocates revise the model to avoid a failed prediction, the revision should be examined as a new proposal with new assumptions.

The broad hypothesis may remain compatible with everything we observe. That makes it a possible philosophical setting for the observations; it does not make every observation evidence in its favor. Evidence becomes discriminating when one account gives us a reason to expect something that another account does not.

## A virtual world can still be a world

David Chalmers's [*Reality+*](https://consc.net/reality/) separates the question of simulation from the fear that nothing would be real. If a computed environment contains stable objects with causal relationships, those objects can be real digital objects. Their implementation differs from what an inhabitant might have assumed; their existence need not be an illusion.

Consider a bridge within such a world. If it supports travelers, can be damaged, and connects two places in a consistent environment, discovering its implementation does not erase those relationships. Someone could be mistaken about what ultimately constitutes the bridge while correctly judging that it crosses the river. The distinction resembles other discoveries that revise an account of matter without abolishing ordinary objects.

The same argument is especially important for minds. If a simulated person genuinely feels pain, calling the setting digital does not make the pain pretend. Friendship, work, and loss could also have real consequences for the people involved. This conclusion remains conditional on the presence of conscious subjects; it does not follow merely from a program displaying convincing characters.

Chalmers's position therefore concerns the reality and possible value of virtual life as well as the skeptical question about our own location. It does not imply that every illusion is accurate or that a virtual world is independent of its implementation. An inhabitant could still make ordinary mistakes within it. The world could also depend on a system and people the inhabitant cannot directly access.

That dependence would create further questions about power. A simulator might be able to alter or terminate the environment. Its inhabitants could have an interest in the conditions supporting their lives, just as people have an interest in the conditions supporting life in any world. Realness and independence are different properties.

## An old question with a new premise

The hypothesis has predecessors in skeptical philosophy, but they should not be treated as earlier discoveries of computation. [[descartes]] used a powerful deceiver to test what could survive radical doubt. Plato's cave examines education and the relationship between appearances and understanding. Traditions grouped under [[gnosticism]] connect the world's origin to religious accounts of ignorance and liberation. Their purposes and commitments differ.

Bostrom adds a particular population argument to the possibility of a generated world. That is more specific than the observation that appearances can mislead. [[kant]]'s distinction between appearances and things in themselves likewise does not describe a hidden server; [[maya-nonduality]] does not simply translate into computer graphics. Comparisons can illuminate the different accounts without making them identical.

The question that remains practical is how much the hypothesis permits us to infer. It supplies no established identity for a simulator, no known reason for creating our world, and no demonstrated way to obtain information about the outside. Stories about a programmer's preferences can be made consistent with almost any situation. That flexibility makes them poor grounds for claims about what people should expect.

Bostrom exposes a tension among expectations about future civilizations and our own self-location. Philosophy of mind asks whether the proposed observers could exist. Particular physical models can be tested for their consequences. Chalmers asks what a discovery of simulation would mean for a life already being lived. None of those inquiries needs the claim that memory errors, quantum experiments, or a moment of déjà vu have exposed the source code.
