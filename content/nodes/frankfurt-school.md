Imagine a studio commissioning a film about corruption. Its hero may break the rules and embarrass his superiors, but the ending has a condition: he must find someone inside the institution who can put things right. The story can be angry about a particular official while remaining reassuring about the system around him.

The producer might like the resolution, the distributor find it easy to market and the audience enjoy it. Those judgments could converge before anyone asked what political habits the finished film encouraged.

This is an example of the problem Max Horkheimer and Theodor Adorno called the culture industry. Their concern was larger than propaganda concealed inside entertainment. They asked what happens when cultural work is organized around predictable returns, familiar forms and an audience treated as a market. Could the pleasures through which people escape their daily routines also reconcile them to those routines?

Their answer sometimes makes the audience seem almost powerless. Yet a business can constrain what gets made while viewers remain capable of judging it. The ability to sell a familiar pleasure and the ability to secure obedience need to be examined separately.

## What the Frankfurt School was trying to explain

The Frankfurt School is a name for a tradition associated with the Institute for Social Research, rather than a membership organization with a single doctrine. The institute’s [own history](https://www.ifs.uni-frankfurt.de/history.html) dates its foundation to 1923, funded by Felix Weil. Under Horkheimer it developed an interdisciplinary approach to social criticism. Nazi persecution forced it into exile; it moved to Columbia University in New York in 1934 and later returned to Frankfurt, where a new building opened in 1951.

For these thinkers, fascism posed a problem that economic explanations alone seemed unable to settle. Understanding the organization of production did not fully explain allegiance, obedience or the willingness to persecute others. Their work brought questions about personality, authority and culture into the study of society. That broader interest did not make the economy disappear.

Horkheimer’s 1937 essay *[Traditional and Critical Theory](https://blogs.law.columbia.edu/critique1313/files/2019/09/Horkheimer-Traditional-and-Critical-Theory-2.pdf)* questioned the separation between specialized knowledge and the social purposes it serves. A researcher could make an existing process more efficient without asking whether the process was desirable. Critical inquiry would examine the organization of society that gave the research its task. In the postscript included with the essay, he insisted on economic analysis while rejecting the idea that state ownership alone settled whether a society had become emancipatory.

A study of workplace scheduling, for example, might identify the arrangement that produces the most output. Another might ask who sets the schedule, who bears the fatigue and whether greater productivity could mean shorter hours. Both need accurate information. They differ over which decisions are treated as open and whose purposes define success.

The critical approach therefore has political commitments. It objects to exploitation and seeks conditions in which people can govern more of their lives. It has to explain how a proposed change would serve those aims and how the people affected could judge it. To study a film in this way is to ask how its form, production and reception relate to those commitments. A critic who assumes the answers has turned a method of inquiry into a way of approving a conclusion already reached.

## Why an industry can offer so much choice

In the culture-industry chapter of *[Dialectic of Enlightenment](https://internt.ht.lu.se/media/utbildning/dokument/kurser/ISLP10/20111/The_Culture_Industry.pdf)*, Horkheimer and Adorno described film, radio and magazines as an interlocking commercial system. They argued that differences between products could organize consumers into categories while preserving a common pattern beneath the apparent variety. What looked like the spontaneous expression of public taste also reflected the economic arrangements through which tastes were supplied and reinforced.

Take the imaginary film from the opening. A studio could make three versions: one set in a police department, another in a hospital, a third in a newspaper. Each might have a different star, mood and advertised audience. If every version resolves institutional failure through the discovery of one exceptional good person, the variety leaves a particular assumption intact: the institution can be repaired without changing who has power within it.

That observation would not prove that all three films were artistically identical. A performance, a visual choice or an unresolved detail might complicate the formula. It would identify a repeated resolution worth examining. Production records could show whether the ending was a condition of the commission, a writer’s preference or a decision made during editing. The recurring form and its explanation would then be open to separate judgments.

Adorno later clarified in *[Culture Industry Reconsidered](https://www.sfu.ca/~andrewf/Culture_industry_reconsidered.shtml)* that “industry” should not be understood only as a literal manufacturing process. His emphasis was on standardized products and organized distribution. A film still requires particular people to make particular creative decisions. The claim concerns the pressures under which those decisions are made and the forms that reach large audiences.

A commission can limit a work before the first sentence is written. Imagine a writer told that a series must have a recognizable setting, a conflict that resets each episode and a protagonist who remains available for another season. These conditions allow ingenuity. They also make some stories difficult to tell. An ending that permanently destroys the setting or changes the protagonist’s position may undermine the reason the series was commissioned.

The formula has advantages. Familiar structures let audiences enter a story quickly, let artists work within a shared language and can make unusual details more visible. A good criticism would show where a convention limits the story and where an artist makes something of it. Repetition alone cannot decide the work’s quality or political effect.

The comparison with [[bernays]] is useful within those limits. Public relations organizes messages to achieve an effect. The culture-industry thesis asks how a whole field of production can favor certain effects without each work being a commissioned publicity campaign. An intentional sales strategy and an accumulated convention are different things to investigate.

## The work of having fun

The original chapter carries the argument into leisure. Horkheimer and Adorno suggested that commercial amusement continued aspects of the work from which it promised relief: repetition, prescribed responses and recovery sufficient to begin again. Entertainment could make the existing arrangement bearable while leaving little room to imagine another one.

Consider someone who finishes an exhausting shift and chooses the least demanding thing available. The choice can be welcome and fully intelligible. Treating it as evidence of stupidity would miss how the working day has already shaped the evening. The person may have little appetite for a difficult film because attention, like time, has been spent elsewhere.

Yet the critic can become unfair here. Rest is a good in its own right. Someone should be able to enjoy a comedy without having to demonstrate that the evening produced a political insight. A theory that finds domination in every undemanding pleasure risks turning free time into another assignment, this time issued by the intellectual who knows what the tired viewer ought to want.

An evening could be different if shorter hours left time to recover, see friends and take part in making something. An artist with a secure income could afford a commercial failure. Changing those conditions would expand what people could choose without requiring them to justify tonight’s entertainment to a critic.

This is also why a large menu does not settle the question of freedom. Choosing between products is one kind of control over an evening. Having a say in working hours, the use of common space or the conditions under which culture is made is another. A person can have many options in the first sense and very little authority in the second.

The distinction makes the argument less dependent on a diagnosis of private taste. We can examine wages, access, ownership and working conditions without declaring that pleasure secretly means misery. It also brings the theory closer to a problem people can contest together: how much of their time and creative capacity is actually theirs to arrange.

## What the wedding viewers thought

In *[Free Time](https://xenopraxis.net/readings/adorno_freetime.pdf)*, Adorno recalled institute research into German reactions to the televised wedding of Princess Beatrix and Claus von Amsberg. Researchers expected the prominence given to the event to be reflected in the importance viewers assigned it.

Questions about enjoyment and questions about political significance produced a less uniform picture. People could enjoy the spectacle while judging its broader importance critically. Adorno acknowledged that the researchers’ expectations had been too simple. He also said the full analysis had been postponed and declined to give an exact proportion. This is a tentative account of findings, not a finished demonstration about every audience.

The questions allowed two reactions to appear together. Viewers could enjoy watching the ceremony and give it little weight in their account of public life. Interest had not required them to adopt the importance assigned to it by the coverage.

A skeptical viewer can still contribute attention to a program, and a business may benefit from people who mock what they watch. Economic success and agreement are separate outcomes. A company might secure one while failing to secure the other. Treating every profitable encounter as ideological victory would hide that difference.

Adorno’s *Culture Industry Reconsidered* also conceded that research had not produced an airtight demonstration of the regressive effects of particular products. He continued to argue for cumulative influence. The concession leaves a task for the theory: explain what changes, in whom, through which repeated experiences, rather than let the scale of the industry stand in for knowledge of its effects.

The distinction resembles a limit in [[manufacturing-consent]]. An explanation of the pressures shaping media production does not automatically establish what audiences accept. A study can show what gets funded or published; finding out how people understand it requires another inquiry. Neither task becomes unnecessary because the other is difficult.

Consider a commercially released protest song. Suppose listeners use it at a rally, while the label sells records and the artist gains a career. The money does not make the organizing imaginary. The organizing does not abolish the label’s power over distribution or the terms of the artist’s contract. To judge whether dissent has been contained, we would need to follow what people can do with the song and what the business can prevent them from doing. A theory that calls every sale containment has decided the outcome before examining it.

## Marcuse and the problem of deciding for others

Herbert Marcuse pushed the issue of satisfaction further in *[One-Dimensional Man](https://www.marcuse.org/herbert/pubs/64onedim/odmintro.html)*, published in 1964. He asked how improving living standards and satisfying desires could help contain demands for a different society. In its [first chapter](https://www.marcuse.org/herbert/pubs/64onedim/odm1.html), he distinguished vital needs from needs shaped by interests that perpetuate domination. Gratification did not, in his account, settle whether a need served the person who felt it.

Suppose a worker buys convenience because work leaves little time, then takes additional shifts to pay for it. The purchases may be useful, even necessary under the circumstances. They can also help maintain a schedule the worker would prefer to change. The satisfaction is real; the arrangement producing the need remains open to criticism.

But someone who likes their life is entitled to ask what authority the critic has to declare their desires false. Marcuse recognized the problem: individuals would ultimately have to answer for themselves, and no tribunal could simply claim the right to determine their needs. His introduction also held open two possibilities, the containment of fundamental change and forces capable of breaking through it. He left that conflict unresolved.

A critic can reasonably ask what people would choose with more security, information or time. The question becomes coercive when the critic treats disagreement as proof that the person is incapable of answering. Then the only acceptable expression of autonomy is agreement with the person who has diagnosed its absence.

Marcuse’s 1965 essay *[Repressive Tolerance](https://www.marcuse.org/herbert/publications/1960s/1965-repressive-tolerance-fulltext.html)* makes that danger especially concrete. He argued that formally equal tolerance could preserve an unequal political order. He went on to advocate withdrawing tolerance from movements of the Right while extending it to the Left, including restrictions at the level of speech and assembly. His proposed exclusions reached beyond an immediate threat of violence to positions such as opposition to expanded public services.

The diagnosis and remedy deserve separate judgments. A wealthy speaker and a poorly resourced opponent may possess the same formal right and radically unequal ability to use it. That is a real problem for democratic discussion. It does not follow that a philosopher, a party or a movement can reliably decide which opinions should lose protection.

Such a decision requires someone to classify opponents and enforce the result. The people making it can be mistaken, self-interested or answerable to those already powerful. Calling the restriction liberating does not remove those risks. There is also a difference between declining to publish an argument in one’s own magazine and preventing its author from assembling elsewhere. Both involve selection, but the second reaches into another person’s ability to organize.

The authority to prevent assembly would need limits, a way to correct mistakes and a procedure through which a person could challenge an exclusion. Marcuse’s confidence in rational judgments about progress does not resolve that institutional problem.

The objection concerns a proposal Marcuse actually made. If implemented, it would give some people the power to prevent others from speaking and organizing in the name of emancipation.

## From an argument to an alleged master plan

A different account makes the Frankfurt School responsible for much of modern cultural change. In a [speech published by Accuracy in Academia in 2000](https://www.academia.org/the-origins-of-political-correctness/), William Lind described political correctness as Marxism translated into cultural terms. He linked the institute’s work to movements around feminism, sexuality and race, presenting criticism of existing culture as a strategy for destroying it. His account explicitly emphasized the émigrés’ Jewish identities alongside their Marxism.

Horkheimer and Marcuse did seek political change. Their writing supplied arguments that later readers could use. The dispute concerns how much a common source of ideas explains about what those readers eventually did.

But influence has a history that a list of resemblances cannot supply. To explain why an institution adopted a policy, one would need to know who proposed it, which arguments they used, what alternatives were considered and who had the authority to decide. A policy can resemble part of a philosopher’s argument without having been derived from it. Readers can also borrow a phrase while discarding the author’s larger position.

Jewish identity adds nothing to that explanation. It does not specify a purpose, a route of transmission or a decision. Making it do causal work encourages suspicion of a population where an argument about particular writers and institutions is required.

Lind also points to Marcuse’s wartime intelligence employment. The [family-maintained account of his life](https://www.marcuse.org/) describes work for the Office of Strategic Services analyzing Germany. That employment is relevant to his biography. It does not make his later readers intelligence employees, or establish that a later essay was an assigned operation. The same distinction matters in [[counterculture-psyop]], where surveillance, disruption and sponsorship have to be traced through their particular records.

The culture-industry argument draws attention to choices made before an audience encounters a work. Budgets, working conditions and decisions about distribution affect the work that becomes available. Those pressures can persist even when audiences are skeptical and artists ambitious.

The wedding viewers in Adorno’s account retained room to judge. Giving that judgment practical consequences would require more than congratulating them for seeing through a spectacle. They would need time, access and institutions through which to make or support something else. That is where a criticism of culture begins to concern the freedom of the people who produce and enjoy it.
