Asked who would make decisions in a resource-based economy, one short answer on the Venus Project's website begins: “No one does.” It goes on to describe decisions reached through information about technology and the Earth's carrying capacity, gathered by computers and sensors. The intention is to replace competing political and commercial interests with attention to what people need and what the physical world can provide.

The answer promises to make provision less dependent on who wins a political argument or can afford a purchase. Information can rule out a plan that requires water a region does not have. It cannot, without further choices, determine whose proposed use of the remaining water should take priority. Someone still has to specify what the system is trying to achieve and what people can do when they dispute its answer.

This is where the Venus Project’s account of physical provision becomes a claim about social authority. Its renderings make a different world imaginable. The proposal has to explain how decisions in that world would be made, corrected and contested.

## A center built to present a civilization

Jacque Fresco and Roxanne Meadows developed a body of designs, models and media around the proposal. The project's [own history](https://www.thevenusproject.com/portfolio/past-works-by-the-venus-project/) describes their purchase of ten acres in Venus, Florida, in 1980, later expanded to 21.5 acres. It records three residences and seven other structures, including workshops, an editing suite, a lab and domes that housed scale models. Meadows participated in design and construction as well as the illustration and model-making through which the future cities became visible.

These were real buildings and real work. They were not a working example of the proposed worldwide economy. A center that develops and presents ideas can demonstrate a construction process or make a spatial arrangement understandable without demonstrating that an entire system of production and distribution functions as claimed.

A complete-looking plan can make the remaining work harder to see. Housing, transport, agriculture and research can be given positions in a circular plan, making them appear parts of a coordinated whole. That is a useful design exercise. It leaves open how people obtain resources, change a plan or withdraw from an arrangement once they inhabit it.

Fresco's proposal appears in *Zeitgeist: Addendum* and *Moving Forward*. Those films give the project a place in a larger criticism of money and social institutions, examined in [[zeitgeist]]. The proposal's merits do not depend on accepting every claim made in the films. Nor does being presented as their solution supply the evidence needed to establish that it works.

## What replacing money would mean

The project's [definition of a resource-based economy](https://www.thevenusproject.com/faq/what-is-a-resource-based-economy/) proposes treating planetary resources as the common heritage of humanity. Goods and services would become available without money, debt, credit or barter. Production and distribution would be organized around resources and needs, with automation helping provide abundance.

This is more than an argument for accurate environmental accounts or generous public services. It proposes replacing monetary exchange as the means of access. The central appeal is that people need food, shelter and care themselves, rather than the money through which access to them is ordinarily obtained. A shortage of purchasing power can coexist with goods or capacity that could meet a need.

That observation supplies a reason to examine how access is organized. It does not show that every shortage is artificial. Land, materials, labor, energy and time can be unavailable in the combination needed for a particular task. Removing a payment requirement would leave those constraints to be handled by another procedure. The short definition asserts that existing technology could provide abundance, while the general FAQ acknowledges disagreement about the adequacy of future energy supplies. The physical case still needs to be established for the system proposed.

Resource accounts could identify a production process that uses less energy, a component worth repairing or a material that should be recovered. But choosing a technically efficient way to meet one need differs from deciding which needs receive attention first. Several projects can each be well designed while drawing on the same limited supply.

Physical accounting invites comparison with [[technocracy]], whose earlier movement proposed energy certificates and a technical administration. The comparison does not establish that every feature of the older scheme appears in Fresco's design, or that a common concern with resources proves one uninterrupted organization. The operating rules need to be compared, not assumed.

## A water budget still contains choices

Consider a hypothetical community planning its water supply. Measurements can establish storage levels, expected inflows and losses from pipes. Engineers can estimate the demand created by new housing, an agricultural project or a different treatment process. A model can show that all the requests cannot be met under a dry-season assumption.

The community would know that its favored combination was infeasible and could examine the expected consequences of alternatives. It would not, on its own, establish whether housing should expand, existing farming should receive protection or residents should accept greater restrictions in exchange for another use.

Describing all three as needs does not settle their relative claims. Nor does putting them in a database. The model needs rules about priorities, acceptable risk and the weight given to people who already depend on an arrangement. Changing one of those rules can change the recommendation without changing the measured quantity of water.

Uncertainty adds another choice. The model might work with a likely inflow and a lower estimate for a prolonged dry spell. Keeping more water in reserve could protect existing users while postponing a new project. Using the likely estimate could permit the project while accepting a greater risk of restrictions later. More accurate forecasts would help, but they would not determine which risk the community should accept or whose plans should be delayed.

The people affected may also know things the initial survey missed. A resident might identify seasonal demand that an annual average conceals. A maintenance worker might know that a reading is unreliable. Public input can improve the account of the physical system as well as challenge its purposes. Residents need to know how their input could alter a decision when the groups affected still disagree.

The objection applies whether the calculation is performed in one building or across many computers. Distributed hardware can reduce dependence on one machine. It does not necessarily distribute authority over the priorities. A network can operate reliably while implementing a rule that residents have no effective way to revise.

## The reply about public feedback

The Venus Project's [response to the economic calculation objection](https://www.thevenusproject.com/faq/how-do-you-respond-to-what-critics-refer-to-as-the-economic-calculation-problem/) explicitly rejects the picture of its founders or another elite deciding every allocation. It describes multiple computers worldwide, redundancy, transparency and continuous input from the public. Needs and available resources would guide provision. This is a fuller proposal than the short answer that no one makes decisions.

Public feedback is important. It gives the proposed system a way to learn beyond what its designers initially know. Feedback needs a defined power to change something. A comment channel, a right to correct a measurement, a vote on a priority and an appeal against a decision are different arrangements. A person allowed to comment may still have no way to appeal.

In the water example, a system might collect every resident's preference yet still require a rule for resolving conflicting requests. It might use an agreed minimum provision, a temporary ration, a negotiated compromise or another procedure. Each choice has consequences. A scientifically informed administration could make those consequences available for discussion without pretending that discussion has become unnecessary.

Errors can concern the target as well as the reading. A sensor can reveal that supply has fallen below a target. Discovering that the target neglected a group of residents requires another kind of correction. A system needs ways to revise an inadequate purpose, as well as improve its performance at the purpose already assigned.

None of this establishes that markets always solve the problem well. Purchasing power is an unequal way to express a need, and some consequences of a transaction fall on people outside it. Prices, public rules and shared provision can all deserve criticism. A moneyless proposal should be judged on the alternative it supplies, rather than excused from explaining that alternative because the existing system has failures.

## Automation and the work that remains

Automation can reduce the effort needed for a task. A resource-based economy would still have to arrange the work of designing, installing, maintaining and revising its equipment. A machine that removes one form of drudgery can create another requirement for reliable human attention. The important measure is the full service provided, including what happens when the equipment fails.

In the hypothetical water system, monitoring would not itself repair a damaged connection. Someone would need the skills, replacement parts and authority to intervene. If the usual person were unavailable, the system would need a fallback. Treating maintenance as a temporary inconvenience would give an incomplete account of the resource requirement.

Motivation also deserves more than a choice between wages and laziness. People can value useful work, learning, care and cooperation. They can also dislike a necessary task, have commitments that conflict or disagree about what the community is entitled to ask of them. A system organized around voluntary contribution should explain how it handles an essential job that remains undone.

The answer may include redesigning the task, sharing its burden or changing the level of service. Each could be reasonable under particular conditions. None follows merely from the absence of money. The proposed freedom from compulsory work depends on how these cases are handled in practice.

## From a proposal to a test

The project's published [aims and phases](https://www.thevenusproject.com/the-venus-project/aims-and-proposals/) distinguish its existing Florida center from a proposed research city and later network of cities. They describe a possible transition through a partly cooperative, money-using society. That is an outline for investigation, not a report that the complete economy has been demonstrated.

There have also been design efforts beyond the familiar models. Its history describes the Center for Resource Management work begun in 2017, with professional volunteers producing drawings, specifications and planning records. The organization says funding prevented that project from concluding. That account describes preparation for a research facility, rather than an operating experimental city.

The [current general FAQ](https://www.thevenusproject.com/learn/faq/) also discusses local and regional habitats, contingencies and the use of money to fund research toward a different system. It acknowledges governance and human behavior among its difficult problems. Its stated approach includes work that could begin before a worldwide system exists.

A shared repair facility could test whether pooling skills and parts reduces the materials needed to keep household equipment usable. The evidence would include repair success, waiting times, labor, parts and requests it could not meet. Residents' experience would matter alongside material savings. A facility that conserved resources by leaving people without necessary equipment would not have achieved the same result.

Requests could exceed the available work hours even if parts were plentiful. A resident waiting for a failed cooker might want priority over a less urgent repair booked earlier. The facility would need a stated rule and a way to reconsider its application. It would also need to release cancelled appointments promptly. Completed repairs alone would not reveal how well people who remained on the list had been served.

Scaling would require new tests. An arrangement among volunteers who share an aim may behave differently when participation is wider, priorities conflict or supplies are disrupted. A small success can support another step without validating every institution imagined for the destination.

## Abundance and disagreement

The Venus Project asks why present arrangements should define the limit of what is possible. Better design and a different organization of access can be worth investigating even where the full moneyless proposal remains unestablished. An objection to comprehensive redesign need not become an argument against repairing a badly organized service.

Its hardest promise concerns what happens after provision improves. Some conflicts might diminish when people have secure access to necessities. Other disagreements concern ways of living that abundance does not automatically reconcile. People may want different uses of land, different forms of association or the ability to make something their neighbors consider wasteful.

That does not mean every preference can be satisfied. A resource constraint may require a refusal, and a well-supported public rule may override a particular request. The safeguard is that the refusal has an identifiable basis and can be challenged through a procedure with the power to reconsider it. Unlimited consumption is not a condition of freedom; an unanswerable allocation system would still be a problem in an affluent society.

The process of challenge needs resources too: time to hear an appeal, access to the information behind a refusal and someone with authority to change the result. A resident should not have to persuade the same unreviewable system that issued the refusal. Cooperation is more credible when it includes a way to disagree.

If a request for new housing were refused because water was reserved for farming, residents should be able to inspect the forecast and ask why that priority prevailed. Better information would make the trade-off clearer. They would still need a procedure capable of reconsidering it.
