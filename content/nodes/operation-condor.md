The lease for a building on Venancio Flores in Buenos Aires helped investigators establish who ran a clandestine prison. Intelligence personnel had signed it, one using a false identity. Survivors recognized their captors. Service records linked the men and the building to Argentina's state intelligence organization, SIDE. The building was known as Automotores Orletti.

During 1976, Argentine and foreign agents used Orletti to hold and torture people taken from the streets and homes around them. Many were political exiles from neighboring countries. Leaving the country that wanted them had brought them within reach of a cooperating service.

Operation Condor made that cooperation more systematic. Beginning with an agreement among Southern Cone intelligence services in November 1975, it developed arrangements for sharing information, pursuing opponents abroad, holding and transferring captives, and organizing attacks beyond South America. Its participants could supply one another with something a visiting agent otherwise lacked: local knowledge, transport, documents, a prison, or freedom from interference by the local police.

The surviving records show services recognizing one another’s authority to pursue people while withholding from those people the protection of any state. Intelligence cooperation gave a government access to an opponent who had escaped its territory.

## Organizing the pursuit

Cross-border repression preceded Condor. Security services already exchanged information and assisted one another against political opponents. Exile created a practical problem for them. A government could imprison or kill an opponent at home, but someone who escaped might organize abroad, testify about abuses, or lobby another government. Neighboring countries contained communities of refugees whose knowledge and contacts remained politically useful.

Chile had been under military rule since the September 1973 overthrow of Salvador Allende. General Augusto Pinochet’s government used its intelligence service, DINA, against opponents at home and abroad. In late November 1975, DINA hosted representatives from Argentina, Bolivia, Paraguay, and Uruguay in Santiago. DINA chief Manuel Contreras helped turn existing cooperation into a standing arrangement. The [closing record of the meeting](https://nsarchive.gwu.edu/document/33619-document-1-dina-summary-acta-de-clausura-de-la-primera-reunion-interamericana-de) proposed an office coordinating information about people and organizations considered subversive. The delegates chose the name Condor at the suggestion of the Uruguayan delegation, in honor of the host country.

The delegates were organizing a continuing relationship, rather than requesting help with one named fugitive. The agreement provided for further meetings and required collective approval before another country could join. It asked services to make the arrangement work through their own people and facilities. Cooperation would become a standing expectation, available before the next target had been chosen.

Members were to compile a directory of intelligence contacts, give one another's requests priority, and send immediate alerts when a suspect traveled or was expelled. They would standardize personal files and study how to connect their equipment. An interim code system would precede a more durable cryptographic arrangement. Intelligence personnel in embassies could provide direct liaison. The document also proposed cooperation in publishing material against subversion.

A record about one person could now travel ahead of that person. The receiving service would not need to reconstruct the allegations or decide independently whether they justified action. Its partner's designation could initiate surveillance, an arrest, or a request for more information. The term subversive did much of the work that a criminal charge and supporting evidence should have done.

The founding document's three phases concerned implementing this system: begin contacts and exchanges, assess and develop the arrangements, then approve and fund the proposed system. They were not a three-step authorization culminating in worldwide assassination. Later intelligence reports described a different three-part progression from information exchange to joint operations and overseas killing teams. The later description concerned operational reach; the founding phases concerned implementation.

Brazil participated in the developing arrangements and became a formal member in 1976; Peru and Ecuador joined later. Argentina was still under Isabel Perón when its representatives attended the founding meeting. Its March 1976 military coup strengthened the conditions for a much more extensive campaign against exiles there.

## A prison shared across borders

Argentina became central to the network because so many of the people sought by neighboring governments lived there, and because its security institutions could supply the means to seize and hold them. Foreign requests and visiting personnel could draw on institutions already equipped for repression.

The [Argentine prosecutors' account of Automotores Orletti](https://www.mpf.gob.ar/plan-condor/ccd-automotores-orletti/) explains how such work fitted into a national structure. SIDE personnel and associates operated the site as a base known internally as OT 1.8. The group included intelligence employees, military personnel, and men outside the service's regular staff. Aníbal Gordon, one of its leading figures, had worked with the far-right Argentine Anti-Communist Alliance.

The prosecution traced the base through personnel files, internal codes, the lease, handwriting analysis, and testimony. The records placed the base within the service’s structure. Prosecutors also argued that its work required arrangements with the military and police controlling the surrounding territory. Large armed abductions could proceed without the local authorities treating the captors as criminals.

Orletti held people associated with foreign political organizations, particularly from Uruguay, Chile, and Bolivia. Foreign security personnel worked there with the Argentine group. Prisoners could be questioned by people who knew their home country's political organizations while remaining outside any acknowledged prison system.

This arrangement divided responsibility without reducing anyone's contribution. One group could locate a person, another carry out the abduction, another interrogate, and another arrange a transfer. A family asking the ordinary authorities about a missing relative would encounter no arrest record and no recognized place of detention. Each stage gave another institution the opportunity to deny holding the person.

The group also took prisoners' property. Prosecutors distinguished the seizure of resources to weaken political organizations from opportunities for individual enrichment, including falsified documents used to appropriate assets. Political persecution and theft could reinforce one another within the same operation. Property seized from captives could finance further operations or enrich their captors.

Orletti's use as a detention center ended after two prisoners escaped in November 1976. The captives’ destinations after their transfers remained concealed.

## One family's route through the system

The [Inter-American Court's judgment in *Gelman v. Uruguay*](https://derechos.org/nizkor/uruguay/doc/gelman3.html) follows a family's movement through this machinery. María Claudia García was nineteen and about seven months pregnant when Argentine and Uruguayan personnel seized her and her husband, Marcelo Gelman, in Buenos Aires on August 24, 1976.

Both were held at Orletti and then separated. Marcelo was killed; forensic investigators recovered his remains in 1989. María Claudia was secretly taken to Montevideo and held by Uruguay's Defense Information Service. She gave birth and was later separated from her daughter. The judgment records conflicting accounts of her subsequent fate.

The child, María Macarena, was raised in a Uruguayan police officer's family and registered as that couple's daughter. In 2000, she met her biological grandfather, Juan Gelman. DNA testing confirmed the relationship. A court later corrected her birth registration.

The abduction had crossed a border, divided a family, and replaced a child's identity in official records. Finding one person required reconstructing what several institutions had done to the others.

## Taking the operation farther

By mid-1976, American intelligence reports described plans to operate beyond the member countries. A [CIA report of July 28](https://nsarchive.gwu.edu/document/33622-document-4-cia-intelligence-information-cable-structure-and-operational-plans-condor) distinguished a communications component, Condortel, from an operational component, Condoreje. The central archive was to be in Santiago, with a forward coordinating office in Buenos Aires. Messages would pass through Condortel.

The source described plans to locate and kill targets in France. Argentina, Chile, and Uruguay were expected to take the leading role. Possible targets extended beyond people accused of armed activity to an opposition politician and potentially members of Amnesty International. The report was unevaluated intelligence, with portions still withheld. It records what the CIA was being told about plans, rather than proving that each contemplated attack took place.

A further document gives those plans an organizational form. In August 1977, the CIA circulated a translation of a Condor agreement dated September 1976, titled [*Teseo Regulation, Operations Center*](https://nsarchive.gwu.edu/document/33628-document-10-cia-cable-text-september-1976-agreement-condor-countries-regulating). The covering report said it was unknown whether the agreement remained current. The text laid out how people would be selected and attacked abroad.

The operations center would be in Argentina, staffed by representatives of participating services. Intelligence teams would identify and follow a target; a separate operational team would intercept the person, carry out the operation, and escape. Most team members would not know their counterparts in the other group. Services were to provide weapons, explosives, documentation, clothing, and electronic equipment when requested.

Representatives would propose targets and choose them by a simple majority. The regulations also specified working hours, accounts, and contributions. Each country was to provide $10,000 for a common fund, with monthly dues for the office. Overseas expenses were estimated at $3,500 per person for ten days, plus an initial clothing allowance. A killing mission could be budgeted, staffed, and reported through ordinary office procedures.

The agreement requested personnel for intelligence, operational, and reserve teams. A member service was to have replacements ready if a difficulty arose. At the end of a mission, each team would account for its expenses. Disagreement over a target would be recorded and sent to the services, while a majority decision could carry the proposal. The organizers anticipated absences, disputes, and arguments over money, and provided ways to keep the missions going.

An FBI cable sent by attaché Robert Scherrer a week after the Letelier assassination described overseas teams in similar terms. It also cautioned that no information then established planned attacks in the United States. Scherrer raised the Washington bombing as a possible instance to investigate. The cable supplied a lead while the murder investigation was still taking shape.

## The bombing in Washington

On September 21, 1976, a car bomb in Washington killed former Chilean minister Orlando Letelier and his colleague Ronni Karpen Moffitt. Michael Moffitt, who was also in the car, survived. Letelier had become a prominent opponent of Pinochet abroad. His distance from Chile had not placed him beyond DINA's reach.

DINA operative Michael Townley left a [written account in March 1978](https://nsarchive.gwu.edu/document/30799-document-3-townley-papers-relato-de-sucesos-en-la-muerte-de-orlando-letelier-el-21). He described receiving the instruction to kill Letelier through DINA operations chief Pedro Espinoza, who attributed the decision to Contreras. Espinoza explained that the Condor relationship could supply Paraguayan passports and American visas.

That first documentation plan failed. Townley said the passports were returned after the visas were canceled. He later entered the United States with Chilean documentation under another name. He described recruiting Cuban exiles, accompanying them to Washington, and leaving for Miami before the bombing. The account connects the mission to Condor's assistance while also showing how the eventual route differed from the initial arrangement.

Townley was writing while exposure threatened his own safety. His account names superiors and minimizes some aspects of his role. He presented himself as carrying out Contreras’s orders and described the parts he said he had delegated. His account supplied investigators with people, documents, and journeys to check.

In May 1987, a [CIA assessment](https://nsarchive.gwu.edu/document/22206-document-01-cia-pinochet-s-role-letelier) concluded that Pinochet had personally ordered the assassination and subsequently directed efforts to conceal responsibility. That assessment drew on intelligence about relations between Pinochet and Contreras and the management of the investigation. The assessment said credible reporting on Pinochet’s own role had first become available in mid-1978, as the American investigation approached indictments and requests for extradition.

## Washington's warning, and its withdrawal

American officials had substantial information about Condor before September 1976. Harry Shlaudeman, the State Department's senior Latin America official, sent Secretary of State Henry Kissinger a report on August 3 about the Southern Cone governments' view of their struggle as a third world war. It described security cooperation, operations across one another's territory, and plans to pursue enemies in Europe.

Shlaudeman did not dismiss the existence of armed revolutionary groups. His report discussed attacks, the revolutionary organizations’ efforts to coordinate, and the fear felt by officials who believed themselves and their families to be targets. He also described the military’s institutional interest in continuing to see a war: its authority, budgets, and fear of eventual retribution. Defeating an armed organization did not necessarily reduce the incentive to find new enemies.

Shlaudeman recognized how the category of subversion was expanding to encompass peaceful dissent. He also recognized the danger of persecution by foreign police acting on information the target could not see or challenge. His report combined criticism with an attempt to preserve American relationships. It acknowledged real guerrilla violence and differences among the governments while warning that the campaign could continue long after the armed left had been defeated.

On [August 23, the State Department instructed ambassadors](https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1969-76ve11p2/d241) to express concern about reported assassination plans. The cable treated exchanges of general security information as useful. It distinguished these from government-directed killings and expressly warned American agencies against identifying individuals who might become targets.

Delivering a diplomatic warning, or démarche, required further decisions. Ambassadors raised objections about whom to approach and the consequences of doing so. A September 16 message from Kissinger's traveling delegation said he had declined to approve the proposed instruction for Uruguay and had directed that no further action be taken. On [September 20, Shlaudeman told his deputy to stand the ambassadors down](https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1969-76ve11p2/d245), explaining that there had been no recent reports of an intention to activate the scheme. Letelier and Moffitt were killed the next day.

The correspondence establishes a decision to abandon a warning about international murder. It does not show that Kissinger had advance knowledge of this particular bombing. Nor can anyone establish from the cable that a delivered warning would certainly have stopped it. Officials had enough information to prepare an intervention, then withdrew it while the threat they had identified was developing.

The CIA faced related choices. In a July 1976 memorandum, its Latin America division chief Raymond Warren asked what action could forestall the overseas attacks and how to prevent the agency from being blamed for participating. The memorandum asked for measures to prevent illegal attacks while guarding against accusations of agency participation.

The agency's later account of its relationship with Contreras supplies another part of the problem. The [2000 report *CIA Activities in Chile*](https://derechos.org/nizkor/chile/doc/hinchey.html) acknowledged contacts from 1974 to 1977 despite knowledge of abuses. By April 1975, intelligence reporting identified Contreras as an obstacle to improving human rights conditions. An interagency decision nevertheless directed continued contact because his position made him valuable.

The report described one payment in 1975 after a proposal for a paid relationship had been rejected and the instructions were mishandled. Contact continued after officials understood what sort of organization Contreras commanded. The agency said it admonished him about abuses and sought information; he could remain an interlocutor while people under his authority were being tortured and disappeared.

Within Washington, informed criticism coexisted with decisions to protect access and withdraw warnings. Condor's member services exercised their own initiative. Relations with American officials were valuable to the services, even when those officials sought limits on what their partners did.

## Establishing responsibility afterward

Survivors, relatives, lawyers, journalists, and investigators exposed parts of the system long before its files were accessible. Paraguay’s police archives, discovered in December 1992 after Martín Almada sought access to records about his imprisonment, supplied an extraordinary body of corroboration. A [Paraguayan government account](https://cultura.gov.py/2011/05/el-descubrimiento-del-archivo-del-terror-en-1992/) describes files on prisoners, photographs, statements taken under torture, and records of surveillance. Photographs could contradict official explanations of a prisoner’s injuries: a person said to have been hurt resisting arrest appeared uninjured on arrival.

The archive also preserved records of cooperation with foreign services. Alongside later declassification, it helped connect episodes governments had kept separated. Records made to identify opponents and manage their detention could now help identify the people and institutions responsible.

Proving an international criminal arrangement required connecting those episodes to institutions and decisions. A transferred prisoner, a message, a travel document, and a service record could corroborate one another. The Orletti lease mattered because it helped tie a place described by survivors to the service that had obtained and operated it.

Argentina's major Condor trial began in March 2013 and concluded in May 2016. According to the [prosecution's account of the proceedings](https://www.mpf.gob.ar/plan-condor/el-juicio/), it initially covered 174 victims across the joined Condor and Orletti cases. Fifteen of the seventeen defendants who reached judgment were convicted, with sentences from eight to twenty-five years; two were acquitted. Most convictions included participation in the criminal association as well as offenses committed within it.

That figure describes the cases before that court. It is neither a count of everyone victimized through Condor nor a total for the dictatorships' repression. Domestic detention, torture, and murder were much wider. Condor added the arrangements that enabled persecutors to cooperate across borders. Treating all the region's victims as victims of one operation obscures how particular people were seized and which institutions could have protected them.

Some defendants died before judgment; others were convicted. Proceedings took decades, and no sentence could restore a murdered relative or the years spent searching. Even so, the institutions' papers could be turned against their denials.

Condor made refuge conditional on the cooperation of governments that wanted the refugee silenced. Its participants shared the capacity to locate and seize people while denying responsibility for their fate. Reconstructing the route through a prison, a border crossing, and a falsified record restored an account the system had been designed to prevent anyone from giving.
