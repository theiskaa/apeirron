The 1998 paper acknowledged a limit: its authors had not proved an association between MMR vaccination and the syndrome they described. The report concerned twelve children. It could raise a question about their histories, but it could not determine whether vaccination increased the rate of autism.

Public advice went further. As the later [Walker-Smith appeal judgment](https://caselaw.nationalarchives.gov.uk/ewhc/admin/2012/503) recounts, Andrew Wakefield used the press conference accompanying publication to say he could no longer support MMR. Two clinical colleagues, John Walker-Smith and Simon Murch, supported the existing vaccination policy pending firmer evidence. The authors were not speaking with one voice.

The study already fell short of the public advice, before the later investigations exposed problems with the report itself. A small study can identify something worth investigating. Changing a recommendation for an entire population requires evidence able to answer a larger question. The design could not justify that change.

Investigators checked the report against the records behind it. Other researchers tested the causal claim in larger populations. These were separate developments: discrediting Wakefield could not, on its own, establish the safety of MMR, just as a defense of his reputation could not repair the missing comparison.

## Twelve children were not a population study

The [five-page report](https://briandeer.com/mmr/lancet-paper.pdf) appeared in *The Lancet* on February 28, 1998. It described children with bowel symptoms and developmental difficulties. Nine were reported as having autism; the diagnoses were not identical across all twelve. The paper said parents associated the onset of behavioral symptoms with MMR in eight cases, and described a short interval between vaccination and those symptoms.

The paper did use comparison material for some laboratory and tissue examinations. Those comparisons answered narrower laboratory questions. What it lacked was an appropriate comparison for the claim about vaccination causing autism: a way to establish whether the pattern occurred more often after MMR than without it.

Suppose a clinic sees families who already suspect that a child's change followed a vaccine. A series assembled from those referrals may contain many such histories even if vaccination did not cause the changes. The selection brings the suspected connection into the sample before anyone analyzes it. The resulting count says little about children who never came through that referral route.

The proposed bowel connection required further work too. Finding gastrointestinal symptoms alongside developmental differences would not establish that vaccination caused either, or that the bowel condition caused the developmental change. Each step in that proposed chain needed evidence. The presence of bowel symptoms could not complete that causal chain on its own.

The parents' accounts were therefore important observations to record accurately. Their importance did not remove the need for comparison. Nor did it permit uncertainty in a history to be rewritten as a precise sequence favorable to the hypothesis.

## What changed when the records were checked

Brian Deer's [2011 investigation](https://www.bmj.com/content/342/bmj.c5347) described both an undisclosed financial relationship and discrepancies in the children's histories. Wakefield had been retained for work connected to litigation against vaccine manufacturers before the paper appeared. Deer reported that the payments eventually totaled £435,643 plus expenses. The undisclosed relationship existed before publication; the total describes payments accumulated over the arrangement.

One comparison concerned a child whose first behavioral symptom was placed in the paper a week after vaccination. Deer reported a hospital discharge account describing changes beginning earlier, before MMR. The difference altered the sequence on which the proposed link depended.

An undisclosed interest does not mathematically falsify a study. It gives readers a reason to scrutinize the design, selection and reporting more closely. Here the scrutiny identified questions about the very dates and descriptions carrying the argument. The questions reached into the result, beyond the missing disclosure.

In [March 2004](https://briandeer.com/mmr/lancet-retraction.pdf), ten co-authors formally withdrew the interpretation suggesting an MMR-autism link. Their statement said the data had not established a causal connection, while retaining their interest in the children's gastrointestinal findings. It was a partial retraction of an interpretation, not yet withdrawal of the whole paper.

The journal [fully retracted it in February 2010](https://briandeer.com/solved/lancet-retraction-2010.pdf), following the General Medical Council's findings. The notice identified false claims about consecutive referral and ethics approval. The reasons concerned false descriptions of the study, including how its patients had arrived. They went beyond a disagreement over interpretation.

The report remained in the journal for twelve years before full retraction. That history justifies questions about editorial scrutiny and the response to contrary records. It does not make the causal claim more credible because it took a long time to withdraw it.

## The doctors did not receive the same judgment

The [2012 appeal judgment](https://caselaw.nationalarchives.gov.uk/ewhc/admin/2012/503) records different outcomes. The GMC found Wakefield and Walker-Smith guilty of serious professional misconduct and ordered their removal from the medical register in 2010. Murch was not found guilty of serious professional misconduct. Both of the doctors ordered removed initially appealed; Wakefield subsequently abandoned his appeal.

Walker-Smith's appeal succeeded. The court found that the panel had inadequately addressed whether he believed he was providing clinical care or conducting research. It quashed both the misconduct determination and his removal. This was a substantive failure in the reasoning about his conduct, not a minor correction to his punishment.

The successful appeal changed the judgment of Walker-Smith's conduct. An appeal about one doctor's conduct does not restore the retracted paper, decide another doctor's case or demonstrate that MMR causes autism. The three doctors' roles and decisions cannot be represented by one disciplinary verdict.

There are several standards involved. Whether a procedure was justified for a particular patient, whether research had appropriate approval, whether an author accurately reported a history, and whether a vaccine changes a population's risk are different questions. A finding on one can inform the others without answering them all.

## Testing the proposed bowel mechanism

One version of the bowel hypothesis suggested something that could be looked for in tissue: persistent measles-virus material associated with autism and gastrointestinal illness. That was a narrower prediction than the claim that vaccination causes autism, and it called for a different comparison from a population registry.

In a [2008 study led by Mady Hornig](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0003140), researchers examined samples from 25 children with autism and gastrointestinal problems and thirteen with gastrointestinal problems alone. Both groups had received MMR. Tissue was obtained during clinically indicated examinations. Three laboratories tested coded samples for measles-virus RNA without knowing the children's diagnostic group. One laboratory had participated in the earlier positive findings.

Confirmed viral RNA was detected in one child in each group. The researchers found no association between its detection and autism. They also found no supporting temporal pattern linking MMR, bowel symptoms and autism. The result was not an absence of every trace in every sample; it was failure to find the proposed difference between the groups.

The comparison gave the control group a specific job. Bowel symptoms were present on both sides, so simply finding an abnormality in a sick child could not establish the autism-specific association. Coding the samples reduced the chance that knowledge of diagnosis would influence laboratory handling or interpretation. A small, targeted study could examine this proposed mechanism while leaving the broader risk question to other designs.

## Testing the claim beyond the original report

Population studies could investigate children outside the original referral group. Researchers could compare vaccination and diagnoses across much larger populations, follow the time between them, and examine whether a proposed susceptible group showed a different pattern.

A [2019 Danish study](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30831578/) linked registry information for 657,461 children born between 1999 and 2010, with follow-up through August 2013. The researchers accounted for measured differences including age, sex, family history and other factors. They found no increased autism risk associated with MMR, including in the subgroups and post-vaccination periods they examined. The adjusted hazard ratio was 0.93, with a 95% confidence interval from 0.85 to 1.02.

The estimate is compatible with no difference; it is not a demonstration that MMR prevents autism. The interval describes statistical uncertainty in that analysis, rather than every possible source of bias. The authors also noted that they had not reviewed individual medical charts. Follow-up began at age one and ended at different ages, so the study did not observe every child for an identical decade. The large sample helped make a useful comparison; its size alone would not make an inadequate comparison sound.

The susceptible-child question was also examined in a [2015 US study](https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jama/fullarticle/2275444). It used insurance records for 95,727 children with older siblings. Of these, 1,929 had an older sibling diagnosed with autism. The researchers found no association between MMR and increased autism risk in either sibling-history group. They also found lower vaccination uptake among children with an autistic older sibling.

The families' vaccination decisions could themselves affect the comparison. The authors discussed how parents noticing developmental concerns might defer vaccination. Such selection could leave some higher-risk children in the unvaccinated group and make vaccination appear protective. The observation needs interpretation; it cannot simply be turned into the opposite causal claim.

The study's higher-risk group was much smaller than its total sample, and the children were privately insured with continuous coverage. Billing records can miss care received elsewhere or misclassify diagnoses. These limits affect precision and generalizability. They do not support substituting the original selected case series for the comparisons it lacked.

Raw case counts would be especially misleading where most children receive a vaccine. Most diagnoses of a condition unrelated to vaccination could then occur among vaccinated children simply because that group is larger. The number in each group and the time observed are needed to interpret the cases. The same problem applies when comparing a small higher-risk subgroup with the much larger remainder of a study.

The distinction can be understood without following every statistical model. A story about a change after an injection asks us to notice a sequence. A comparison asks whether that sequence occurs more often because of the injection, while accounting as well as possible for other differences. The latter is the question the vaccine-autism claim needs to answer.

No finite study tests every conceivable subgroup. A further subgroup claim needs a clear definition and a test of its prediction. Otherwise an unspecified exception can outlast any result without acquiring evidence of its own.

## MMR and thimerosal were different hypotheses

The mercury-preservative controversy is often folded into the MMR story. [MMR never contained thimerosal](https://www.cdc.gov/vaccine-safety/about/thimerosal.html). A concern about that ingredient therefore cannot explain an alleged effect of the combined MMR vaccine through exposure to it.

Thimerosal was used to prevent contamination in some multi-dose vaccine vials. The [1999 joint statement by US public-health agencies and the American Academy of Pediatrics](https://www.cdc.gov/mmwr/preview/mmwrhtml/mm4826a3.htm) recommended reducing or removing it as a precaution. The statement distinguished known risks from vaccine-preventable disease from an uncertain potential risk associated with cumulative exposure. It did not report a demonstrated autism effect and emphasized maintaining protection against the diseases.

The question could be tested more directly than by comparing two national trend lines. In a [2003 Danish study](https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jama/fullarticle/197365), researchers examined 467,450 children, comparing receipt of a pertussis vaccine containing thimerosal with a formulation of the same vaccine without it. They found no statistically supported increase in autism or other autistic-spectrum disorders and no evidence of an increasing risk with the estimated ethylmercury dose.

This was a study of a particular preservative exposure and vaccine history. It was not a second MMR experiment. Keeping the hypotheses distinct matters because rejecting one does not require pretending that every ingredient, formulation and medical intervention has the same effects.

Nor does precautionary removal establish that a suspected harm was known and concealed. A precaution can be justified while a risk remains uncertain. Explaining that decision honestly requires saying what prompted it, what was known at the time and what later research found, rather than retrospectively claiming that officials already possessed complete certainty.

## What was lost while the argument continued

Gaps in vaccination left measles opportunities to spread. Public Health Wales [records 447 confirmed cases](https://phw.nhs.wales/reports/measles-surveillance-and-epidemiology/) in the Mid and West Wales area during the outbreak centered on Swansea in 2012–13, with 64 hospital admissions. The agency links the outbreak to historically low uptake, temporally associated with inaccurate reports about MMR side effects. Its account also describes the large catch-up campaign that followed.

Those figures concern confirmed cases in a defined place and period. They should not be mixed with notifications of suspected cases or presented as a calculation of how many illnesses one newspaper article or one doctor caused. The episode shows what gaps in protection can permit without supplying a complete account of every family's decision.

The argument can also crowd out attention to the child's present life. Autism involves a range of characteristics and support needs. The [National Institute of Mental Health's overview](https://www.nimh.nih.gov/health/publications/autism-spectrum-disorder) describes genetic and environmental contributions to development that remain under investigation. It does not offer one cause that explains every autistic person.

A study can give strong evidence against an MMR effect without identifying why one particular child is autistic. That leaves a real question for the family. It does not return the unsupported explanation to equal standing with the evidence that tested it. A clinician who answers the causal question accurately may still have much to do in responding to the family's need for information, assessment and support.

Wakefield's loss of credibility leaves several responsibilities intact. The early report needed a comparison it did not have. The published histories needed checking. Each doctor's conduct needed a fair judgment. Families needed a response that took their observations seriously without turning an observation into a cause.

Being accurate about MMR and being attentive to an autistic child's life are compatible obligations. The failure of one proposed explanation should leave the child more visible, not less.
