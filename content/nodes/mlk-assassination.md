Martin Luther King Jr. went to Memphis to support sanitation workers. The men were seeking safer conditions, better pay, and recognition of their union. Their signs said “I AM A MAN.” For King, the dispute belonged to the same struggle as voting rights and desegregation: people doing necessary work were entitled to dignity and a decent living.

He returned after a March demonstration had ended in violence, intending to help organize another nonviolent march. On April 3, 1968, he addressed supporters at Mason Temple. The following evening, he was shot while standing on the balcony outside his room at the Lorraine Motel. He was thirty-nine.

James Earl Ray pleaded guilty to the murder in 1969, then spent years trying to withdraw his plea. King's family eventually came to believe that Ray had been framed. In 1999 they won a civil verdict finding that Loyd Jowers and others, including government agencies, had participated in a conspiracy to harm King. A Justice Department inquiry rejected the allegations on which that case rested.

Behind these disputes lies a fact that gives official assurances an unusually difficult burden: the FBI had conducted an abusive campaign against King before it investigated his murder. The evidence of that campaign is extensive. The evidence identifying the gunman and any accomplices has to be followed through a different set of records. The conflict over trust has followed the case through a guilty plea, a congressional inquiry, and a civil trial that answered a different question from a criminal prosecution.

## King in his final year

At Riverside Church in April 1967, King publicly condemned the Vietnam War and linked its costs to the deprivation of poor Americans. He also described the suffering of Vietnamese civilians and the contradiction of opposing violence at home while remaining silent about American violence abroad. His [“Beyond Vietnam” address](https://kinginstitute.stanford.edu/encyclopedia/beyond-vietnam) drew criticism from major newspapers and some people within the civil rights movement.

The disagreement was about strategy as well as the war itself. Critics feared that his position would weaken alliances needed for civil rights legislation. King considered the issues connected through moral responsibility, military expenditure, and the lives of the poor. His opposition cannot be explained merely as a provocative phrase extracted from a speech.

He was also developing the Poor People's Campaign, intended to bring people of different racial backgrounds to Washington to demand economic change. The proposed sustained presence in the capital would press demands for employment, income, and housing. The campaign was being organized when he went to Memphis.

The [sanitation strike](https://www.archives.gov/education/lessons/memphis-v-mlk) made those concerns concrete. It involved municipal workers, local officials, wages, and the conditions under which Black labor was valued. King's return after the failed March demonstration was also a test of whether disciplined nonviolent action could be rebuilt under pressure.

The work brought him into disputes with public officials, business interests, and some former allies. Memphis was one immediate confrontation within that wider effort. There was a concrete next step: another march, for which organizers were negotiating with the city and a federal judge when he was killed.

## What investigators connected to Ray

The shot struck King shortly after 6 p.m. on April 4. He was pronounced dead at St. Joseph Hospital at 7:05 p.m. Across from the Lorraine stood a rooming house where Ray had rented a room that afternoon under the name John Willard. A shared bathroom at the rear overlooked the motel.

A bundle abandoned near Canipe's Amusement Company contained a Remington .30-06 rifle and other belongings. Ray had bought the rifle in Birmingham after exchanging a different weapon the previous day. Store employees, sales records, and his own later admissions connected him to the purchase. His fingerprints were found on the recovered rifle; fingerprints also connected him with other items in the bundle.

The [House Select Committee on Assassinations](https://www.archives.gov/research/jfk/select-committee-report/part-2a.html), or HSCA, later examined that evidence alongside witnesses, documents, and Ray's accounts. The link between him and the weapon was not merely a police assertion that an unfamiliar gun must have belonged to the suspect. He admitted buying it and transporting it to Memphis. His alternative explanation concerned what happened afterward and who directed his movements.

The fatal bullet presented a real limitation. Firearms examination could not conclusively identify the recovered rifle as the weapon that fired it. The bullet's characteristics were compatible with that type of rifle, but it did not yield the individual match that would have made this part of the case simpler.

The examination established compatibility with a type of weapon while leaving its individual source unresolved. The purchase, presence, abandoned property, witness evidence, flight, and plea supplied the other parts of the case against Ray.

The firing position was also examined. Engineering work for the House committee found that the trajectory could be consistent with the bathroom window or the garden area behind the buildings. The medical and engineering work depended partly on reconstructing how King was standing when the bullet struck. The available information did not fix the vertical angle precisely enough to select one of those positions by geometry alone. The committee combined it with witness testimony and concluded that the shot came from the bathroom.

Marks on the windowsill and bathtub were less decisive than they sometimes appear in accounts of the case. Investigators could not securely attribute them to the weapon or the assassin rather than another object or police examining the scene. The committee therefore gave those marks less weight than the witnesses' accounts of activity at the rear of the building.

Ray's subsequent flight took him through Canada to Europe. He was arrested at Heathrow in June 1968 using a passport in the name Ramon George Sneyd. By then he had lived as a fugitive under aliases for more than a year, since escaping prison in 1967. Investigators could trace purchases, accommodation, and documents associated with those identities. Whether someone assisted him was a question to pursue through those transactions, beyond a judgment about how capable a petty criminal ought to be.

## A guilty plea and a disputed explanation

Ray pleaded guilty on March 10, 1969, and received a ninety-nine-year sentence. The proceeding did not provide a full contested trial of the prosecution's case. It did, however, include questions from the judge about Ray's understanding and agreement, a stipulation of facts, and evidence presented to a jury as Tennessee procedure then required. The familiar statement that no jury heard any evidence is incorrect.

Ray soon sought to withdraw the plea. He challenged his lawyer Percy Foreman's advice, the circumstances of confinement, and his understanding of what a guilty plea would permit him to do later. Those allegations were examined in subsequent proceedings and by the House committee, which concluded that the plea had been knowing and voluntary. Ray continued seeking a trial until his death in 1998.

Later proceedings included an evidentiary hearing in 1974 and appellate review. The courts considered whether Foreman's advice, financial arrangements involving rights to Ray's story, and other circumstances had made the plea involuntary. They rejected that claim. The result preserved the conviction while leaving the shooting evidence without the full contested trial that Ray sought after changing his position.

Ray's principal alternative account involved a man he called Raoul. He said this man had recruited him for criminal work, supplied money, and directed purchases and travel that brought him to Memphis. William Pepper, who later represented Ray and the King family, pursued that account as an explanation of how Ray could have been used without knowing the full purpose.

The attraction is clear. It links otherwise scattered transactions and gives the abandoned evidence an alternative meaning: a package identifying a convenient suspect rather than property dropped during escape. The difficulty is establishing the person and the instructions independently of the account they are meant to explain.

The House committee compared Ray's successive versions with receipts, travel information, witnesses, and his own earlier statements. It found inconsistencies and rejected Raoul as Ray described him. It also examined financial support and criminal associations instead of treating every unexplained expenditure as evidence of intelligence funding.

Its conclusion nevertheless left room for accomplices. The [1979 report](https://www.archives.gov/research/jfk/select-committee-report/part-2b.html) found a likelihood of conspiracy based on circumstantial evidence, including possible criminal associations and the prospect of financial reward. Its discussion of a proposed payment for killing King was not proof that a completed payment or instruction to Ray had been traced. The committee judged the circumstances suggestive while retaining Ray as the gunman.

That investigation treated Ray as responsible for the shooting while asking who might have assisted or encouraged him. The proposed reward supplied a possible incentive; criminal contacts and transactions supplied possible routes of assistance. The committee lacked direct evidence tying the St. Louis offer to the murder, even though it believed Ray likely knew about it. The proposed links through associates remained circumstantial.

## The FBI's campaign

The FBI's conduct toward King supplies powerful reasons to distrust its role. The [House committee's review](https://www.archives.gov/research/jfk/select-committee-report/part-2e.html), drawing on bureau files and the earlier Senate investigation, describes surveillance and efforts to discredit him that developed well before 1968.

A security investigation into the Southern Christian Leadership Conference had begun in 1962, ostensibly concerned with suspected Communist influence. Telephone surveillance began in 1963 with authorization from Attorney General Robert Kennedy, while the FBI also used microphones to record conversations. Monitoring gathered private conversations, and the collection of derogatory personal information became an objective in itself.

The difference between the public justification and the actual campaign can be followed inside the bureaucracy. Assessments that Communist influence was limited did not satisfy J. Edgar Hoover. William Sullivan's division altered its emphasis under pressure from the director. King's effectiveness as a speaker and organizer was treated as a reason to regard him as dangerous.

The bureau circulated derogatory material, tried to undermine support and honors, and used friendly media contacts to spread attacks. A package mailed to King in 1964 combined a surveillance recording with an anonymous threatening letter. Congressional investigators understood its final passage as suggesting suicide to avoid exposure. The account of who prepared and approved every stage involved conflicting testimony, but the operation's origin in the FBI was established.

The bureau was trying to undermine King's relationships, support, and public standing. After his death, the same agency's investigators would be gathering the evidence on which assurances about the assassination depended.

The campaign is often grouped under [[cointelpro]]. Its chronology is broader than the formal program designation applied to King in 1968. The earlier harassment should remain visible without backdating every administrative label. Similarly, the bureau's language about discrediting or neutralizing a target cannot be translated automatically into a documented order to shoot him. The acts established in these records are serious in their own terms.

The congressional investigation found failures in the search for a possible conspiracy as well as a substantial case against Ray. It also found improper interference with Ray's rights: correspondence between him and his attorney had been intercepted, copied, and passed to FBI headquarters despite a court order protecting its confidentiality. The court had permitted screening correspondence for jail security, while protecting the content of communications with counsel. The FBI received copies of three letters and circulated them within the bureau. In one covering memorandum, an official drew attention to a passage about addresses in Miami that Ray did not want a writer to visit before trial. That was attention to the substance of the defense's affairs, beyond checking for a plan to escape. The committee rejected the explanation that the correspondence was being examined only for security.

The House committee concluded that government agencies had not participated in the assassination. That finding was separate from its condemnation of the FBI's campaign and its belief that Ray may have had assistance. The same investigation could accept evidence of grave official misconduct while reaching a narrower conclusion about the murder.

## Protection, surveillance, and the fire station

Accounts alleging a prepared killing zone often describe police protection and observers being removed shortly before the shot. Some personnel changes were real. Their functions and dates need to be kept distinct.

The House committee examined the withdrawal of a police security detail, the reassignment of two Black firefighters, and detective Ed Redditt's removal from a surveillance position at the fire station near the motel. The changes were not all one order issued just before King stepped onto the balcony.

Redditt's case is revealing. He had described his role in ways that suggested protection. The committee compared his statements with those of his partner and superiors, his written reports, and the way he worked. Watching from the fire station while concealing his observation was surveillance. He was not accompanying King as a bodyguard, and he acknowledged the distinction under questioning.

Redditt was removed roughly two hours before the shooting after officials received information they interpreted as a threat to his life. The history of that warning contained uncertainties and conflicting recollections. The committee examined them and concluded that his supervisors perceived a real danger to him. His partner, Willie Richmond, remained at the observation post.

Richmond's continued presence left a police observer in position. The sequence was less complete than the account of every observer being cleared away, and the record about the perceived threat gave investigators a reason for Redditt's departure to examine.

Military intelligence has also figured in later allegations. The Army's domestic monitoring of political activity is a documented history. Claims of a military team positioned to participate in or photograph this assassination involve an additional account of particular personnel, assignments, and timing. Those claims became part of the dispute surrounding the civil case and the subsequent federal review.

## What the 1999 jury decided

Pepper's investigation brought witnesses and allegations together over many years. For the King family, his work offered a way to challenge the account of Ray's sole responsibility and to seek an examination outside the agencies they distrusted.

Loyd Jowers, who had operated Jim's Grill beneath the rooming house, became central to that effort. In 1993, after decades of giving a different account of his activities, he publicly claimed participation in a plot. His story connected local figures, money, a weapon, and an alternative firing position behind the grill. If reliable, a participant's admission could supply something much stronger than a theory based only on institutional motive.

The family sued him. In November 1999, a civil jury began hearing the case. On December 8, it answered yes to questions asking whether Jowers had participated in a conspiracy to harm King and whether others, including governmental agencies, were parties to that conspiracy. It awarded the family the symbolic sum of one hundred dollars.

The [transcript's jury instructions and verdict](https://archive.org/details/CorettaScottKingEtAl.VS.LoydJowersEtAl) show exactly what sort of finding this was. The judge instructed jurors to use the civil standard: an allegation had to be more likely true than not. He also told them they did not have to identify the other conspirators. The verdict did not list agencies or individual officials and did not adjudicate criminal charges against them. They were not defendants represented in the case. The wording of the second question referred to the conspiracy as alleged by the defendant. Jowers's side was itself asking the jury to look beyond him, and the broad form of the verdict allowed an affirmative answer without deciding which official did what.

Jowers himself did not testify at this trial. His statements reached it through other material. That absence matters in a case so dependent on the meaning and reliability of his admission.

The proceeding was not entirely without disagreement. His lawyer, Lewis Garrison, challenged testimony implicating Jowers and argued that any role he played was small. Garrison also disputed Pepper's effort to clear Ray, directing the jurors to his plea and inconsistencies in his account. Yet in closing he asked the jury to accept a broader conspiracy involving government agencies. The parties disagreed over responsibility within the alleged plot without providing a full defense of the agencies accused of participating.

The family's victory was real. So were the limits of the question and evidence the jury considered. It did not vacate Ray's conviction or provide a criminal acquittal. A broad answer on a civil verdict form cannot supply the detailed identification and testing that the judge expressly told the jury it did not have to complete.

## The evidence challenged afterward

The Justice Department's inquiry had begun in 1998, before the civil trial. Attorney General Janet Reno limited it to new allegations from Jowers and former FBI agent Donald Wilson, together with leads arising from them. It was not a fresh investigation of every aspect of the assassination. As the trial proceeded, federal investigators examined its evidence and pursued additional interviews and records.

The resulting [2000 report](https://www.justice.gov/crt/overview-investigation-allegations-regarding-assassination-dr-martin-luther-king-jr) rejected the central claims. Its case was more substantial than an announcement that officials did not wish to investigate. It compared versions of alleged participants' stories and described evidence inconsistent with them.

Jowers had changed important details, including his account of handling the rifle and who fired or disposed of it. The admission placed him in the story as a participant, but its changing details left investigators with incompatible descriptions of his role. The federal inquiry attempted to interview him; he declined.

Betty Spates had supplied a claim about seeing Jowers with a rifle. Investigators compared that account with other statements in which she denied being at the grill at the relevant time or knowing anything about the killing. They also checked a list of patrons and interviewed witnesses. The report concluded that her changing accounts did not reliably corroborate Jowers.

Some of those contradictions were mentioned in the civil trial, including by Garrison. The disagreement concerns their weight and the fuller record, rather than the simple assertion that no one had ever questioned a witness. The absence of a recovered alternative murder weapon also left the proposed rifle story dependent on recollections that conflicted about what had happened to it.

The military allegations led investigators to witnesses and photographs that could be compared with the trial evidence. One account placed soldiers on the fire station roof photographing the assassination. Federal investigators traced the account through the reporter who had recorded it, interviewed military personnel and firefighters, and compared it with photographs taken shortly after the shooting. They found evidence of an earlier visit to inspect a surveillance location, but did not substantiate personnel on the roof at the moment of the murder. Photographs judged to have been taken probably within a minute of the shot showed an empty roof. The report also recorded the original reporter's doubts about uncorroborated sources whose accounts had reached the trial through interview notes.

The Justice Department was examining allegations against public institutions that included the department itself and the FBI. That conflict should remain visible. Its conclusions nevertheless contain checkable claims about documents, photographs, and changes in testimony. The dispute could then turn on whether a recollection had changed, what a photograph showed, or whether an alleged witness had actually been present. Those comparisons gave its conclusions a basis that could be examined independently of an official assurance.

## What remains to be accounted for

The record is not frozen at 2000. In July 2025 the National Archives announced the online publication of more than 230,000 pages concerning the assassination. Its [announcement](https://www.archives.gov/news/articles/nara-releases-230k-mlk-assassination-files) explained that some material had previously appeared through freedom-of-information requests and was now being gathered online with minimal redactions. Publication makes records available for examination; it does not, by itself, certify the allegations that a particular file contains.

The strongest case identifies Ray as the gunman through several kinds of evidence, while the fatal bullet lacks a conclusive individual match to the recovered rifle. The House committee's concern about possible assistance and financial incentive deserves to be read alongside its rejection of Raoul and government participation. The civil verdict and the federal review deserve the same examination of their evidence and limits.

King's family was entitled to answers from institutions that had worked against him. Those institutions owed more than an assertion of confidence in their own investigation. The disclosures about the campaign against King made independent scrutiny essential, even where that scrutiny continued to point to Ray as the gunman.
