Imagine paying ten euros for lunch. With a debit card, the payment draws on money your bank owes you. With a digital euro, if one is issued, you would spend a claim on the central bank. The amount and the meal could be identical. The institution responsible for the money would be different.

A central bank digital currency, or CBDC, would make that change. Much of the payment system would still need to be designed. A central bank could issue the money while commercial firms provide wallets and customer service. Payments could be recorded centrally without the issuer holding the customer's name. Some transactions could happen between devices while both are disconnected from the network.

For someone whose payment is refused, the important difference may be who made the decision and whether anyone will put it right. A new currency could give people another dependable way to pay. It could also make more of their spending depend on permissions they cannot meaningfully challenge. The design needs to explain the access it offers and the power it gives others.

## The money behind the balance

Most money people encounter digitally is already a record in a computer. Moving a bank deposit through a phone app does not make it a CBDC. Putting the deposit on a blockchain would leave the bank as the debtor. The defining feature is whose liability the money is: who owes the holder its value.

The [2020 report by seven central banks and the BIS](https://www.bis.org/publications/central-bank-digital-currencies-foundational-principles-and-core-features.pdf) distinguishes commercial-bank deposits from central-bank money. Banks already use electronic central-bank balances for settlement. Cash makes central-bank money available to the public. A retail CBDC would extend that public access into digital payments. A wholesale CBDC, intended for financial institutions, concerns a different set of users and transactions.

For the lunch customer, the distinction may be invisible until something goes wrong. The value of a central-bank claim does not depend on the solvency of a commercial bank. That does not make a wallet immune to theft, a device immune to failure, or the currency immune to inflation. Different risks need different protections.

It also does not require everyone to become a direct banking customer of the central bank. The Federal Reserve's [January 2022 discussion paper](https://www.federalreserve.gov/publications/files/money-and-payments-20220120.pdf) considered an intermediated model: private firms would provide wallets or accounts, while the underlying currency remained a Federal Reserve liability. The proposal also contemplated identity checks by those intermediaries. The identity of the issuer and the organization holding a customer's details were separate design decisions.

Commercial firms could remain involved at almost every encounter a customer has with the currency. Their involvement would not necessarily disperse power: a common rulebook or shared access system could still leave users with little choice. The wallet provider, issuer and supervising authority each need scrutiny.

## A payment with a condition

Consider two hypothetical arrangements. In one, a customer agrees that a seller will receive payment only after a parcel arrives. Once paid, the seller can spend the money normally. In the other, a government distributes money that can be spent only on a list of approved goods, and the restriction continues to govern its use.

The first arrangement carries out the payer's agreement about a transfer. The second restricts what the recipient can do with the value received. The word programmable can describe either arrangement while leaving the recipient's freedom almost unexplained.

The Bank of England makes this distinction in its [2026 design-phase update](https://www.bankofengland.co.uk/the-digital-pound/progress-update-digital-pound-design-phase). Its proposed digital pound could support user-approved conditional payments, while restrictions on how or where the money itself could be spent would be excluded. The Bank says programmable money would be prohibited through primary legislation and prevented by the architecture. Those are commitments for a proposed system, to be judged against the eventual law and implementation.

China's [July 2021 e-CNY white paper](https://www.pbc.gov.cn/en/3688110/3688172/4157443/4293696/2021072014364791207.pdf) also describes smart contracts for payments under agreed conditions. It sets out a two-tier system in which authorized operators provide services under central-bank management. The document establishes what the issuer intended those functions to do. It does not show that every ordinary yuan expires, or that every wallet has been connected to a single social-credit score.

A food-only benefit and an ordinary wage payment would require different scrutiny. A restriction attached to a particular public benefit might have a specified purpose and a legal basis. Extending it to someone's wages would reach much further into their life. An extension to wages would need an authority able to impose the restriction and a system able to enforce it. The recipient would need a way to contest the decision.

Software does not settle those questions. A description of what software could do leaves open what the particular currency is built and authorized to do. Expiry, spending restrictions and account freezes need their own evidence. The power to block a payment also needs examination in existing bank and payment systems; the absence of a CBDC does not settle whether that power is acceptable.

## Privacy depends on who sees what

A transaction can identify someone to a wallet provider without identifying them in the central bank's settlement system. The provider may need to know its customer, while the settlement record uses an identifier in place of a name. That separation can limit access to personal information. It is not the same as leaving no record.

The ECB's [October 2025 preparation report](https://www.ecb.europa.eu/euro/digital_euro/progress/html/ecb.deprp202510.en.html) describes this approach for an online digital euro. Payment providers would conduct customer checks; the Eurosystem would settle payments using pseudonymous identifiers. The report says identities would not be visible to the Eurosystem and describes separated processing intended to minimize data exposure. Its offline design aims to keep sensitive payment details on the devices rather than expose them to the issuer or providers.

A payment provider and a central bank would see different parts of the record. Assessing privacy means examining how well that separation holds. A pseudonym remains useful to someone who can connect it to a person. The relevant inquiry includes the provider's records, who can obtain them, how long they survive, and whether information from separate parts of the system can be joined.

The PBOC's white paper describes a different balance, which it calls managed anonymity. It proposes greater anonymity for small-value use and traceability for higher-value payments, with wallet limits varying according to customer checks. It also describes information barriers and restrictions on arbitrary requests. These are the issuer's account of its safeguards, rather than an independent demonstration that every transaction is protected as promised.

Some privacy problems arise from ordinary attempts to make the system work. In their [2023 joint opinion](https://www.edpb.europa.eu/system/files/documents/2023-10/edpb_edps_jointopinion_022023_digitaleuro_en.pdf), Europe's data-protection authorities identified specific problems in the proposed digital-euro regulation. Checking that someone stays below a holding limit could require identifiers to be accessible across providers. The authorities asked for a clearer justification of that access point and clearer rules for fraud-related data processing. They also recommended stronger privacy for low-value online transactions.

A holding cap may reduce financial risk, but enforcing one cap across several wallets requires some way to recognize that they belong to the same user. It is possible to support the purpose of the cap while objecting to an unnecessarily intrusive way of enforcing it. The opinion concerned the 2023 proposal; it is not a finding that every concern has since been resolved or ignored.

The connection to [[mass-surveillance]] concerns what is collected, who can search it, what can be inferred, and what survives after its original use. A promise that the central bank cannot see a name answers only part of that inquiry.

## What offline payments can and cannot promise

Cash changes hands without asking a network to approve the transfer. Reproducing that convenience digitally is difficult because digital information can be copied. An offline recipient needs protection against accepting value that the payer has already spent elsewhere.

Suppose someone loads value onto a device, pays one shop and then tries to pay another with a copy of the same value. Neither shop can consult the central system while disconnected. The devices need a way to prevent the second payment or establish who bears the loss if it succeeds. Requiring a connection for every check would solve that problem by surrendering the offline service people wanted.

The Bank of England's [April 2025 offline experiment report](https://www.bankofengland.co.uk/report/2025/digital-pound-experiment-report-offline-payments) examined four solutions using demonstration systems, with no real money changing hands. Secure chips helped protect keys and payment operations. Reconciliation after devices reconnected could reveal double spending, although detecting a loss afterward did not prevent it at the moment of purchase.

Keeping transaction records could help detect counterfeit or repeated spending. Omitting a record improved anonymity but removed information needed for those checks. Limits on offline use could reduce exposure while also interrupting payments unexpectedly. The experiments demonstrated possibilities and remaining difficulties, rather than a finished currency or a complete security assessment.

For a shopkeeper accepting a payment during an outage, these details determine whether the sale is dependable. For the customer, they determine whether an offline wallet reaches a transfer limit, requires an expensive device, or shares a payment history when reconnected. The name offline tells them too little about those conditions.

It can still be valuable. A payment method that continues through a network failure offers something an online-only service cannot. The benefit has to survive the conditions under which people will actually need it, including the limits on devices, electricity and opportunities to reconnect.

## A wallet people have a reason to use

There are financial consequences beyond the payment itself. A bank uses deposits as a source of funding. If customers move substantial balances into central-bank currency, the bank may need other funding. During a panic, the ability to move quickly into a central-bank claim could intensify withdrawals. The Federal Reserve's 2022 paper discusses both problems and possible mitigations, including limits on holdings and a currency that pays no interest.

This does not mean banks merely collect a fixed pile of savings and lend it out. As the Bank of England explains in [its account of money creation](https://www.bankofengland.co.uk/quarterly-bulletin/2014/q1/money-creation-in-the-modern-economy), a new loan creates a corresponding deposit. Banks still face funding costs, payment obligations and regulatory constraints. A customer moving a balance into central-bank money changes how that balance is held; it does not by itself transfer all lending decisions to the state. The distinction is developed further in [[money-as-debt]].

To reduce deposit outflows, an issuer may design a CBDC for limited everyday balances. A user may want more from it. A tightly capped wallet may be suitable for daily purchases but unsuitable for keeping a salary. Constraints introduced to protect the financial system can make the product less attractive to the people expected to adopt it.

Even a working system can struggle to find users. In [*Nigeria's eNaira, One Year After*](https://www.imf.org/-/media/files/publications/wp/2023/english/wpiea2023104-print-pdf.pdf), published in 2023, IMF researcher Jookyung Ree examined the currency's slow initial adoption. The study reported an average of roughly 14,000 transactions a week, small relative to the number of downloaded wallets. It described limited acceptance and competition from familiar payment methods, while cautioning that it was too early to judge the project's fate.

A person needs places to spend a currency before opening a wallet is useful. A merchant needs customers who want to use it before accepting it is worthwhile. That difficulty applies even when the issuer has built reliable infrastructure. First-year results are evidence about that stage of the eNaira, not a measurement of its present use or a verdict on every other CBDC.

For someone without a bank account, the practical difficulties may begin before the first payment. Offering a wallet does little for someone unable to meet enrollment requirements, obtain help, or pay local merchants. The argument for public digital money is strongest when it identifies an actual barrier and shows how the proposed service removes it. Counting downloads cannot do that work on its own.

## The ability to choose something else

There is no single worldwide decision to issue a retail CBDC. The [BIS survey covering 2024](https://www.bis.org/publications/paper-159-advancing-tandem-results-2024-bis-survey-central-bank-digital-currencies-and-crypto) found that 85 of 93 responding central banks were exploring retail currencies, wholesale currencies or both. Exploration included different stages of work, and wholesale projects were more advanced in aggregate. The number measures interest, not a commitment to give every person a state wallet.

Political decisions have also diverged. A [January 23, 2025 US executive order](https://www.whitehouse.gov/presidential-actions/2025/01/strengthening-american-leadership-in-digital-financial-technology/) directed agencies to stop CBDC development and prohibited establishment, issuance or promotion, except where required by law. That was a policy directive with a legal qualification, not a permanent constitutional settlement.

The ECB's [current account of the digital-euro project](https://www.ecb.europa.eu/euro/digital_euro/faqs/html/ecb.faq_digital_euro.en.html), checked in November 2026, describes preparations for a possible first issuance in 2029, on the working assumption that the necessary legislation is adopted in 2026. It describes the digital euro as an addition to cash. A preparation program and an intended launch date should not be mistaken for a currency already in public circulation.

Keeping cash is more demanding than promising not to abolish it. People must be able to obtain it and find places that accept it. Otherwise a nominal choice may offer little help when an electronic payment is declined or a network fails. A person told that the new currency is voluntary should be able to pay for necessities without joining it.

A public payment option could reduce dependence on a few private providers. It could also create a common point of exclusion if too many decisions about access converge there. The design should be judged by what happens to an ordinary person whose payment fails: whether they can understand the reason, challenge an error, recover access and still buy what they need while waiting.

People would have to live with the service and the power it gives others, long after the launch promises have been made. A useful addition would leave people with more dependable ways to pay, including a workable way to decline the new one.
