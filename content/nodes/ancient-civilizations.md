At Göbekli Tepe in southeastern Anatolia, people shaped limestone into pillars and stood them in circular buildings more than eleven thousand years ago. In the debris, excavators also found grinding stones. Wear on their surfaces and tiny mineral remains of plants indicate that many had been used to process cereals. The pillars show what the builders could organise. The grinding stones bring us closer to the daily work of feeding the people who gathered there.

The site does not fit a simple sequence in which settled farms produced surplus grain, cities followed, and only then did people build monuments. The people of this region were changing how they lived, ate and gathered over a long period. The discovery brought some of that work into view. It did not, by itself, identify a civilisation elsewhere that had taught them how to do it.

## What the builders had around them

The earliest monumental buildings at [[gobekli-tepe]] date to the latter part of the tenth millennium BCE. Small and large buildings at the site have been interpreted in different ways. Whatever the use of the largest structures, the builders belonged to a region where people were already experimenting with plants, stone tools and communal buildings. A [2019 study of grinding tools at the site](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0215214) compares the buildings and material with other early sites in Upper Mesopotamia and notes evidence for cereal grinding before domestication. Processing grain is not the same thing as raising a fully domesticated crop, and neither activity by itself tells us where the people doing it slept or how long they lived in one place.

This matters because one version of the lost-civilisation argument begins by ruling out the people who actually lived near the monuments. If they were not farmers in the later sense, the argument runs, they could not have supported the work. But the evidence from food preparation and from comparable buildings in the region gives archaeologists something to investigate besides a missing teacher. Nor does the presence of [[megaliths]] on two continents show that the same people or techniques travelled between them. To establish a connection, excavators would look for distinctive shared practices, independently dated evidence of which came first, and traces of contact that could explain how the practice moved.

## A coastline really did disappear

Rising seas have covered places where people once lived. At Tel Hreiz, off the Carmel coast in what is now Israel, underwater archaeologists documented buildings, burials, wooden remains and a long row of boulders on the seaward side of a Neolithic settlement. The site dates to between about 7,500 and 7,000 years ago. The investigators interpret the boulders as a wall built to protect the village from encroaching water and waves. They are much larger and rounder than the stones used in the village buildings. The closest plausible sources were nearby riverbeds and river mouths, so the villagers would have had to bring them to the site. The alignment follows the coast as it would have been when people lived there. Together with the dates of the settlement remains, those observations make the wall more than a straight line on a modern seabed.

Tel Hreiz gives substance to a useful criticism: a record made mostly on dry land will omit some coastal history. It also shows what it takes to fill part of that gap. A collection of features, remains and dates allowed investigators to identify a settlement and propose a reason for an unusual structure. They did not find the remains of an older society that had passed on its knowledge to people across the sea. Loss of evidence makes some questions harder to answer; it does not supply the missing answer.

The [[younger-dryas]] was a cold interval near the end of the last Ice Age. In 2007, Richard Firestone and colleagues proposed that an extraterrestrial event helped trigger it, pointing to material in sediments around the start of the interval. An independent team led by Todd Surovell later examined magnetic grains and microscopic spheres from seven sites, two of which were included in the earlier study. They did not reproduce the reported concentration pattern. Their test was of particular geological evidence, not of the existence of any particular community. Even a confirmed impact would not establish that an advanced maritime society existed at the time, or that its people and practices disappeared in that event. And the global rise in sea level after the Ice Age took place over millennia, not all at once at the end of the cold interval.

## What survives in a story

Graham Hancock has proposed that survivors of a maritime civilisation transmitted their knowledge to later societies. In the material he publishes about *Fingerprints of the Gods*, accounts of culture-bringers and floods are among the grounds for looking for those survivors. Stories are evidence for what people told one another. To use one as evidence for a specific prehistoric event, it helps to be able to tell where and when the account took shape, how it travelled, and which details can be checked without relying on the story itself.

Plato’s *Timaeus* gives [[atlantis]] a setting in a distant past and an ending in earthquakes and floods. Its priest places the ancient Athens of the story nine thousand years before his conversation with Solon. Converting that number to a modern calendar date can produce an apparent fit with the end of the Ice Age. The calculation tells us where the story puts the event if its chronology is taken literally. It does not tell us whether Plato’s chronology is a record of events. To establish that, the match would need evidence from somewhere other than his dialogue.

## A history without a deciphered text

The Harappan cities of the Indus region can be investigated through their buildings, objects and the river landscapes around them, even though their short inscriptions remain undeciphered. A 2012 study traced changing river conditions and settlement patterns, including a decline in settlement sizes and a shift in the distribution of sites eastward from about 3,900 years ago. It offered a sequence to test against material evidence rather than treating urban decline as the disappearance of everyone who lived there. Not being able to read the inscriptions leaves important questions open. It does not leave the cities without a history.

The next evidence for a lost coastal society might come from a seabed survey. To change the account of how later societies developed, a discovery would need to do more than date an impressive site earlier than expected. It would need to show what the people there knew, who they were in contact with and what appeared in the societies said to have learned from them. At Göbekli Tepe, that work starts with the people who left the pillars and grinding stones. The excavation has given us more to learn about them, not a reason to look past them.
