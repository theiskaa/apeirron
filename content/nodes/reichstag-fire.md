On the night of February 27, 1933, the debating chamber of the German parliament burned. Police found Marinus van der Lubbe inside the building. The twenty-four-year-old Dutch worker admitted setting fires and insisted that he had acted alone. Hitler’s government announced a different explanation: the blaze was the opening move in a Communist uprising.

The next day, President Paul von Hindenburg signed a decree suspending seven constitutional protections. Police could interfere with personal liberty, homes, correspondence, speech, meetings, associations, and property beyond the limits that ordinarily applied. The measure had no fixed expiration date. The authority granted that day remained after the prosecution ended and parliamentary government had been dismantled.

The identity of everyone involved in the arson remains disputed. The destruction of political opposition is recorded in laws, arrests, and parliamentary proceedings. Those records show how an accusation about a single night became authority to imprison people who had no demonstrated connection to the fire—and how disabling those people helped the government acquire still more power.

## A government already using force

Hitler had become chancellor on January 30, four weeks before the fire. Hindenburg appointed him at the head of a coalition with conservative nationalists. The Nazis were the largest party in parliament, but they had no majority of their own. Their conservative partners helped bring Hitler into office while expecting to retain influence over him.

An election was scheduled for March 5. This gave the new government an immediate opportunity to turn control of ministries and police into an electoral advantage. The campaign was already being conducted under coercion. A February 4 decree restricted the press and political meetings. In Prussia, Germany’s largest state, Hermann Göring directed the police apparatus; members of the Nazi SA and SS were enlisted as auxiliary police. Men who had attacked political opponents in the streets could now act with official authority.

Opposition organizers were already working under threats, restrictions, and violence when the Reichstag burned. After February 27, the government could present a much wider assault as an urgent defense against insurrection.

The Communist Party, or KPD, was a large organized opponent with its own newspapers, meeting places, and elected representatives. It was also an opponent whom many conservatives feared. Announcing that Communists had attacked parliament allowed the government to pursue an existing political enemy while claiming to protect the state itself.

The evidence available that night could not support the announcement. Finding van der Lubbe in the building established a suspect. His revolutionary beliefs gave the police a political context to investigate. Neither fact established orders from the KPD leadership or an impending national revolt. The government nevertheless acted on the larger accusation before a trial could test it.

At Nuremberg in 1946, Göring acknowledged that lists of Communists to be arrested had existed before the fire. He maintained that the blaze precipitated action against them. Göring was trying to justify his conduct, but his admission helps explain the speed of the arrests. The authorities were not beginning with an unknown criminal network and identifying its members through evidence from the burned building. They already had political targets.

## What the decree authorized

The [Reichstag Fire Decree](https://germanhistorydocs.org/en/nazi-germany-1933-1945/decree-of-the-reich-president-for-the-protection-of-the-people-and-state-reichstag-fire-decree-february-28-1933.pdf) was formally a presidential measure for the protection of the people and state. Its preamble identified Communist violence as the danger. Its operative provisions made no corresponding requirement that each person searched, silenced, or detained be connected to the arson.

The first provision suspended seven articles of the Weimar Constitution until further notice. The following provisions allowed the national government to take over powers in a state considered unable to restore order and required state and local authorities to comply. The decree also imposed penalties for disobedience and made certain serious offenses punishable by death.

These powers affected the practical means of opposition. A party needs somewhere to meet, a way to publish, communication among its members, and organizers who can remain at liberty. Police could now reach each of those activities through the same emergency authority. An organization might still exist on paper while losing the means to function.

There was a constitutional route by which emergency measures were supposed to be controlled. [Article 48](https://germanhistorydocs.org/en/weimar-germany-1918-1933/ghdi:document-3937) permitted the president to act when public security and order were seriously endangered, including temporarily suspending specified rights. It also required prompt notification of the Reichstag and allowed parliament to demand that the measures be withdrawn. Article 50 required presidential decrees to carry a responsible minister’s countersignature.

The February decree therefore passed through offices that had recognizable constitutional powers. Hindenburg’s participation mattered. So did the ministers who prepared and implemented the measure. The people directing the emerging dictatorship used existing offices to dismantle the restraints on those offices.

Parliament’s power to demand withdrawal became much less useful as deputies were arrested, driven into hiding, and prevented from organizing. The emergency power helped disable the institution authorized to restrain it. The safeguard remained in the law book as the deputies needed to exercise it were removed.

The decree’s duration compounded this problem. It did not set a date when the government would have to renew its case. Nor did its text require a finding by an independent body that the supposed Communist emergency continued. A temporary suspension under the constitution became an enduring basis for police power. The decree remained in force until the regime’s defeat in 1945.

Those twelve years were maintained through coercion as well as support. Some Germans supported the repression; others cooperated, accommodated themselves, or benefited. People subjected to detention, censorship, and violence had diminishing opportunities to register their refusal. The measure endured in a country where refusing it became increasingly dangerous.

## Detention without a criminal conviction

The phrase *protective custody* made imprisonment sound like a precaution. Under Nazi rule, it allowed political detention without the prosecution having to obtain a criminal conviction. The police could decide that someone was dangerous and keep that person confined. Prisons and concentration camps became places to put opponents whose cases did not need to survive an ordinary criminal trial.

Article 114, one of the provisions suspended by the decree, had required that a detained person be told by the following day who had ordered the detention and why. It also provided an opportunity to object without delay. Those were practical protections for someone who had been taken away: information about the decision and a means of challenging it. Suspending the article removed a constitutional obstacle to holding people without those protections.

A criminal accusation ordinarily creates a question that a court must resolve: what act did this defendant commit, and can the state prove it? Political custody displaced that question with an administrative judgment about future danger. An acquittal of a particular offense could no longer be assumed to restore a person’s freedom. The authorities could continue treating the person as an enemy.

The anarchist writer Erich Mühsam was among those swept up after the fire. His prison identification photograph survives. He was eventually held at Oranienburg, where he was tortured and killed in July 1934. The government’s response had reached a writer and activist outside the alleged Communist command behind the blaze. The emergency furnished a way to dispose of opponents as a category.

The decree operated through police stations, prisons, and improvised places of confinement. Police officials made arrests; SA and SS members guarded and abused prisoners; administrators provided places of confinement. The legal change gave these actions official cover and weakened the remedies available to the people subjected to them. It brought state authority and party violence closer together.

A surviving [custody order for Herbert Fröhlich](https://perspectives.ushmm.org/item/protective-custody-order-for-herbert-froehlich), issued by the Berlin Gestapo on April 21, 1936, shows the decree still doing work three years later. Fröhlich was a married carpenter. The order invoked the February 1933 decree while alleging extortion connected to homosexuality, an accusation he contested. A witness’s statement was declared credible. Public safety and order supplied the justification for custody.

A final note requested authorization to hold him until the investigation ended because it could not be completed within seven days. The form recorded a name, occupation, address, and administrative references, but supplied no fixed release date. An authority introduced against an alleged Communist uprising was being used to detain a man over an entirely different allegation.

Fröhlich’s complete case cannot be reconstructed from this order. The police’s chosen legal authority, however, is printed on the form: they were still referring back to the fire.

## The vote in the opera house

The March 5 election took place while this repression was underway. The Nazis won 43.9 percent of the vote. With their nationalist coalition partners, they had a parliamentary majority. They still lacked the two-thirds majority required to transfer constitutional lawmaking power to the cabinet on their own.

The government sought that transfer through the Enabling Act. The surviving parliament met in the Kroll Opera House, near its burned home. SA men were present to intimidate the deputies. Communist representatives were prevented from taking their seats; some Social Democrats were detained or absent. The opposition confronting the bill had already been physically reduced.

Even so, the votes of other parties mattered. The Catholic Center Party supported the measure after negotiations and assurances from Hitler. Conservative and other non-Socialist deputies also voted for it. The Nazis had used repression to change the composition of the chamber. They also obtained the cooperation of political leaders outside their party.

Otto Wels spoke for the Social Democrats on March 23. His [speech](https://germanhistorydocs.org/en/nazi-germany-1933-1945/ghdi:document-1497) made a practical constitutional argument. The government already possessed a majority with which to govern through parliament. It could propose economic legislation and seek support. Wels challenged its demand to eliminate the scrutiny of elected representatives, especially when the press was already unable to operate freely.

He also addressed the treatment of defeated opponents. A government could act against violence without placing an entire opposition outside the protection of law. That was the choice his colleagues were being asked to endorse. His argument came from within a chamber where the consequences of refusing were visible in the missing deputies and the men in uniform.

The act passed by 444 votes to 94, with the attending Social Democrats casting all the votes against. It was promulgated on March 24. The tally records a decision made under repression. It also records that some deputies refused, and that enough others supported the measure to make its passage possible.

## Two laws doing different work

The fire decree and the [Enabling Act](https://germanhistorydocs.org/en/nazi-germany-1933-1945/ghdi:document-1496) are often compressed into a single emergency maneuver. They changed different things. The decree opened the way for extensive interference with people and organizations. The act allowed the cabinet to make laws without obtaining the Reichstag’s agreement, including laws departing from the constitution.

The act’s wording preserved the institutions of the Reichstag and Reichsrat, the body representing the states, and left the president’s powers formally untouched. It also had an expiry date: April 1, 1937, with an earlier end if another cabinet replaced the existing one. Those provisions could offer reassurance to people willing to imagine that the cabinet would respect the boundaries surrounding its new authority.

Yet the central power had already moved. A parliament that permitted the government to legislate without it could continue meeting while losing its role as a necessary place of decision. A provision protecting an institution’s continued existence did little for the independence of its members when opposition politicians could be arrested and their organizations suppressed.

The Enabling Act’s original term was finite; the fire decree’s was open-ended. The government acquired overlapping routes to power, with different legal forms and different supposed limits. Their combined use made it progressively harder to enforce any remaining restriction.

The treatment of van der Lubbe illustrates a further step. The February decree increased the penalty for specified offenses, including arson, to death. But the Reichstag had burned before that decree took effect. On March 29, the government enacted a separate [law imposing the death penalty retroactively](https://encyclopedia.ushmm.org/content/en/article/law-for-the-imposition-and-implementation-of-the-death-penalty), extending the relevant provision to acts committed between January 31 and February 28.

Van der Lubbe was executed on January 10, 1934. The law had been changed after the act for which he was condemned. The government had secured a new power to legislate and promptly used it to alter the punishment for an act already committed. This was a further decision, taken a month after the fire.

## The trial did not establish the announced conspiracy

The Reichstag fire trial ran from September to December 1933. Alongside van der Lubbe stood the German Communist politician Ernst Torgler and three Bulgarian Communists: Georgi Dimitrov, Blagoi Popov, and Vasil Tanev. The prosecution had an opportunity to substantiate the Communist conspiracy invoked when the emergency measures were introduced.

It failed to convict those four defendants. They were acquitted; van der Lubbe was convicted. The outcome was an embarrassment for a government that had publicly assigned collective responsibility months before the evidence was tested in court.

An acquittal of these defendants did not establish that van der Lubbe had acted alone. Nor did it identify a Nazi arson team. It established the failure of the case against the people presented as his Communist accomplices. Meanwhile, the powers justified through the accusation remained in place. The government’s ability to imprison opponents had become detached from the success of its prosecution.

The building was a crime scene, but it was also a propaganda subject almost immediately. Nazi accounts presented a Communist assault. Anti-Nazi campaigning presented the fire as a Nazi operation. Once each side had an account serving a larger political purpose, witnesses and documents entered a contest over credibility as well as a reconstruction of the evening.

The possibility of Nazi responsibility deserves examination. The regime benefited greatly, had already embraced violence, and soon demonstrated its capacity for organized murder. Historians arguing for Nazi participation have also offered claims about the physical fire and specific witnesses. Those claims concern how the arson was carried out; political benefit alone cannot tell us who entered which room or ignited which material.

## Why the arson question remains difficult

Fritz Tobias’s investigation, published in articles beginning in 1959 and subsequently as a book, argued that van der Lubbe had acted alone. Hans Mommsen supported that interpretation in a substantial 1964 study. Their work challenged a widely circulated assumption that the political beneficiaries must also have organized the fire.

Mommsen’s [article](https://www.ifz-muenchen.de/heftarchiv/1964_4_2_mommsen.pdf) is useful for another reason. It follows the instability of witness accounts. People close to the investigation changed their explanations; later recollections could incorporate rumors or postwar interests. In his reconstruction, the response also involved panic, the expectation of a Communist revolt, and the opportunistic use of the emergency. A rapid and brutal political response did not require every official to possess the same prior knowledge.

Historian Benjamin Carter Hett has challenged the lone-arsonist account. In a [2016 interview explaining his research](https://www.wolfgangmichal.de/2016/06/30/benjamin-carter-hett-fuer-den-spiegel-ist-das-eine-prestigefrage/), he emphasized contemporary judgments about whether one man could have produced the fire so quickly. He also questioned the postwar reliability of police officials whose accounts helped Tobias’s reconstruction. Such men had reasons to minimize their participation in Nazi crimes and present their work as professional policing.

Hett’s argument puts pressure on two parts of the account at once: the feasibility of the physical sequence and the credibility of the people explaining it afterward. The technical question concerns time, access, materials, and the spread of fire through the building. The historical question concerns when witnesses spoke, what they could actually know, and whether later testimony was shaped to protect them. The disagreement persists partly because the physical reconstruction and the assessment of witnesses are themselves contested.

Göring’s alleged boast is a good example of the difficulty. At Nuremberg, prosecutor Robert Jackson confronted him with an affidavit from General Franz Halder. Halder reported that, at a luncheon in 1942, Göring had claimed responsibility for setting the Reichstag on fire. Göring denied that the conversation had occurred.

The [trial transcript](https://avalon.law.yale.edu/imt/03-18-46.asp) preserves an allegation by an identified witness and a denial by the accused. Halder was describing a remark made years after the blaze, rather than an operation he had watched in 1933. Even if Halder remembered the remark accurately, a boast years later would leave open who actually carried out the arson. Göring’s denial added a further dispute about whether he had said it at all.

A further controversy emerged in 2019 over a statement attributed to former SA member Hans-Martin Lennings. In a notarized account dated November 8, 1955, Lennings said he had helped transport van der Lubbe to the Reichstag and found signs of a fire already underway. The timing he alleged would challenge the familiar sequence in which van der Lubbe entered and set the fire on his own.

The document’s late public emergence raised questions about both the night itself and the subsequent handling of evidence. Lennings was describing the journey twenty-two years afterward. His account raises specific questions about the men accompanying him and the time of arrival; corroboration of those details would give it much greater weight.

A [2019 parliamentary exchange](https://dserver.bundestag.de/btd/19/130/1913006.pdf) is sometimes treated as if it settled the issue officially. The government’s answer did not endorse the allegations in the legislators’ questions. It said that it knew the document through media reporting and left the historical assessment to researchers. The allegations and the reply have different authors and different evidential standing, even though they appear in the same printed document.

The German parliament’s own 2023 historical account still described responsibility for the blaze as disputed. By contrast, the decisions that followed the fire are preserved in the regime’s own official measures.

## The accusation outlived its case

The political transformation required repeated decisions. Hindenburg signed the decree. Ministers and police officials implemented it. Party formations supplied violence. Non-Nazi deputies helped pass the Enabling Act. Later legislation widened or consolidated authority. The fire gave these people an occasion to act. It did not relieve them of responsibility for the choices they made.

The case belongs in an account of [[control-systems]] because it shows how the means of correction can be attacked along with the opposition itself. A newspaper closed under emergency authority could not freely challenge the emergency. A detained organizer could not arrange resistance. A deputy kept out of parliament could not use parliament’s formal power to demand withdrawal of a decree. The damage reinforced itself through the institutions that might otherwise have interrupted it.

The prosecution eventually failed to convict four men accused of joining the Communist conspiracy. That failure did not restore the liberties suspended in its name. By then the government had acquired ways to silence its critics and make laws without their agreement. The emergency accusation no longer had to survive an independent test for the emergency powers to survive.
