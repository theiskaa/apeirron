In December 2000, Florida officials were trying to decide what to do with ballots that counting machines had rejected. Some had a mark that did not register as a vote. Others showed more than one candidate marked. A person could look at a ballot and see evidence of a choice that the machine could not read. But two people examining the same ballot might disagree about whether the evidence was sufficient. In a presidential contest with a certified margin of 537 votes in Florida, the rules for making that decision mattered.

The state dispute was not primarily about whether someone had secretly changed the totals. It was about which ballots should count and whether officials were treating comparable ballots alike. In other elections, the questions have been different: Did someone interfere with ballots before they reached the count? Did equipment record a result incorrectly? Did people circulating an allegation have any evidence for it? The same demand for an investigation can apply to each question, but the same evidence cannot answer all of them.

## The voter and the mark

The Florida Supreme Court ordered a manual recount in December. The U.S. Supreme Court stopped it. In [*Bush v. Gore*](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USREPORTS-531/pdf/USREPORTS-531-98.pdf), the majority concluded that the recount procedures lacked adequate standards for treating ballots equally and that a constitutionally sufficient recount could not be completed within the applicable deadline. The dissenters disagreed about whether the recount should stop rather than continue under revised procedures. The certified result stood. A ruling on the standard and deadline was not a finding about what every voter whose ballot had been rejected meant to do.

A recount does not rerun an election. It cannot ask a voter to make the choice again or recover a mark they meant to make but did not. It can examine an existing record under known rules. That is why decisions about which records to keep and how to interpret a mark matter before a race becomes close, not just after one side asks for another count.

Congress enacted the Help America Vote Act in 2002, providing federal assistance for election administration and voting equipment and setting minimum requirements. The act required a permanent paper record for manual audits. It did not say that a voter had to be able to read and verify that paper as a record of their selections. A paper record generated by the same equipment that made the electronic count is useful only to the extent that officials have reason to trust it as evidence of what voters chose. A hand-marked ballot read by a scanner leaves a record that can be examined without asking the scanner to count it again.

## When the problem comes before the count

In North Carolina’s Ninth Congressional District in 2018, Republican Mark Harris appeared to finish ahead of Democrat Dan McCready. The contest was not certified. In hearings the following February, state investigators described coordinated interference involving absentee ballots and a political operative working for Harris’s campaign. After several days of testimony, the board voted unanimously to order a new election. It did not declare McCready the winner. Even if a hearing can establish that people interfered with ballots, it may be unable to reconstruct a count that includes all the votes people actually cast.

That kind of inquiry depends on evidence about particular ballots, who handled them and what witnesses say happened. The board was not simply guessing from an unusual numerical pattern. Nor could officials have resolved the question by counting the same ballots more carefully. A correct tally of the ballots in custody does not establish that every lawful ballot reached that custody or that no one interfered before it arrived.

The inquiry into Russian interference in the 2016 presidential election asked a different question again. The Mueller report documented a social media operation and the theft and release of political organizations’ and campaign-related material. It did not establish that Trump campaign members conspired or coordinated with the Russian government in those interference activities. The evidence of theft and attempted influence was not evidence that ballots were altered. Nor did it measure how many people, if any, voted differently because of what they saw. Attribution of an influence operation is not a recount of votes or a way to estimate an alternative election result.

## A check on Georgia’s count

After the 2020 presidential election, some of the most widely repeated allegations concerned voting equipment switching votes. That is a claim about the count, and where trustworthy paper ballots exist there is a direct way to test it: count the paper independently. In Georgia, the state conducted a full manual tally of the presidential contest as part of its audit because the margin was close. The state’s November 19 announcement said the hand tally confirmed that Joe Biden had won Georgia. It also said some counties had failed to upload all their initial results and corrected those omissions. Those missing results mattered and had to be included. They did not change the winner.

A risk-limiting audit need not always count every ballot. It calls for examining randomly selected ballots and adding to the sample when the evidence is not strong enough to confirm the reported outcome. If necessary, it escalates to a full hand count. In this context, a five-percent risk limit means that if the reported outcome is wrong, the procedure has at most a five-percent chance of failing to correct it. It does not mean that five percent of ballots are checked, or that there is a five-percent chance the election was stolen. The test also depends on preserving the ballots and knowing which set of ballots the audit is supposed to cover. It checks whether those ballots support the outcome; it is not a general test of whether every eligible person was able to vote.

Court cases asked other questions. In the Pennsylvania case, the Trump campaign appealed the denial of its request to amend its complaint. The Third Circuit examined the proposed claims about unequal treatment and election procedures. It stressed that the campaign’s lawyer had said the case was not a fraud case. The court found the allegations insufficient and refused to disturb the lower court’s ruling. Calling that decision a completed trial of every fraud allegation overstates what it decided. Calling it a refusal to consider whether the proposed case had substance understates what the judges did.

A separate lawsuit tested statements aired about Dominion Voting Systems. In March 2023, a Delaware judge ruled that the challenged statements about Dominion and the election were false. The ruling covered specific broadcasts; it was not a national ballot audit. It left open whether Fox had acted with the level of fault the law requires for liability. Fox agreed to pay $787.5 million to settle the case in April, so a jury never decided that question. By the time Congress met on January 6, 2021, versions of the broader stolen-election story had already become part of [[qanon]] and other movements. The House select committee later documented efforts to pressure state and federal officials, including Vice President Mike Pence, to alter or obstruct the certification process. Political action based on an allegation can have serious consequences even when the evidence does not support the allegation itself.

In Georgia, the audit found mistakes that officials could correct from existing records. In North Carolina’s Ninth District, counting the available ballots again could not repair what the board had found. Those cases called for different remedies. Neither called for assuming that the people who made the first count had the last word.
