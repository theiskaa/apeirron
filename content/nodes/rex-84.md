On 13 July 1987, Representative Jack Brooks tried to ask Oliver North about a subject beyond the arms sales and Contra resupply operation occupying the Iran-Contra hearings. Had North worked on plans to keep the government operating after a major disaster? North’s lawyer intervened. Senator Daniel Inouye, chairing the hearing, asked Brooks to leave a sensitive, classified subject alone.

Brooks explained his concern. He had read newspaper accounts of a contingency plan that would suspend the Constitution. Inouye suggested that an executive session could be arranged if the committees wanted to pursue it. North supplied no answer in that exchange.

The [printed transcript](https://archive.org/download/Iran-ContraHearingsTestimonyTranscripts/1987-irn-0005_North-2-McFarlane.pdf), on page 122, also preserves what followed. North’s lawyer, Brendan Sullivan, called the inferences ridiculous. Inouye replied that the committee would decide that. Brooks returned to Congress’s constitutional responsibilities. The hearing moved on without North explaining that work.

That exchange has become the usual introduction to Rex 84, a federal readiness exercise conducted three years earlier. Legislators investigating secret operations had encountered another area of executive activity they could not discuss openly. Brooks’s question did not name Rex 84, and North never reached an answer about the plans.

Released FEMA records place the exercise within a recurring readiness program. Emergency preparation, proposals for extraordinary powers, actual detention authority and rumors about camps have accumulated around it until they often appear to be one program. The records show how officials argued about responsibilities, defended their agencies and tried to explain who would be in charge.

## Keeping a government alive

A state cannot respond effectively to an attack if the people entitled to make decisions are dead, unreachable or unable to communicate. Officials need ways to establish who is in charge, contact departments, preserve records and perform essential work from somewhere other than their usual offices.

A relocation site therefore needs more than a protected room. Its occupants require communications, usable information, staff and arrangements for replacing people who cannot arrive. Departments also need plans for continuing their own services. The Congressional Research Service distinguishes continuity of government, concerned with government leadership and constitutional survival, from continuity of operations, concerned with keeping an organization’s essential work going. The two can overlap in a major emergency.

For example, an alternate office might let surviving officials communicate while their department restores the records needed to pay benefits or move supplies. An exercise can test those connections by sending simulated reports and asking participants to make decisions. It can reveal that an official lacks a working line or that two departments expect the same transport. The resulting decisions reveal how the arrangements might fail, even though no actual emergency is taking place.

The constitutional difficulty begins with the powers those surviving officials propose to exercise. Preserving the president’s ability to act and granting the president a new power are different things. So are moving officials to safety and moving civilians into custody. Both may involve vehicles, lists, guards and emergency communications, while imposing very different obligations on the people carrying them out.

FEMA inherited this mixture of responsibilities when it was established under Carter. His 1979 Executive Order 12148 transferred civil-defense functions as well as disaster-assistance functions to the agency. It gave FEMA a coordinating role in civil defense and civil emergencies, including natural and nuclear warning systems and preparation for major terrorist incidents. National-security preparation was part of its assignment from the outset, rather than a hidden mission first added by Reagan.

The agency could therefore find itself working beside departments with quite different powers. A single incident could require food distribution, the restoration of utilities, federal protection of buildings and arrests for serious crimes. Several agencies could be present without acquiring one another’s powers. Knowing which agency could issue which order was as important as knowing who had a working radio.

## The file FEMA released

A [2017 response to a Freedom of Information Act request](https://documents.theblackvault.com/documents/fema/2015-FEFO-00367-rex84.pdf) about Rex 84 released thirteen pages of responsive records. The package includes a press position paper, internal correspondence and later explanations of FEMA’s relationship to military civil-disturbance planning. The covering letter says eight pages were released in full and five with personal information withheld under the privacy exemption. The release supplies the agency’s explanations and some of the arguments behind them; the complete exercise script is not among its contents.

The press paper acknowledges a classified exercise called REX-84 ALPHA, involving thirty-two federal departments and agencies, held from 5 to 13 April 1984. NIGHT TRAIN was its companion military exercise. FEMA described such exercises as a recurring opportunity to examine emergency production, transport, economic measures, civil defense, continuity and other national-defense problems.

The agency disputed the accusations about arrests and military intervention. It said REX-84 ALPHA had not simulated military operations in Central America, and that FEMA had neither a program nor the capability to seek out undocumented immigrants, take them into custody or establish detention centers during a crisis. It acknowledged that government plans existed for handling large arrivals of refugees and other migrants, describing coordination over care, processing and protection.

FEMA was defending its responsibilities and reputation. Its account draws a boundary around its own work, leaving questions about other departments and the wider planning process. It does, however, acknowledge both the exercise and government preparations to handle a large arrival of migrants.

Critics and FEMA were arguing about the purposes and control of preparations that both acknowledged existed. The disputed issues included who would control them, whether they contemplated military operations abroad or mass custody at home, and what legal authority would permit the proposed actions. Those questions cannot be answered by the exercise’s existence alone.

## What the newspaper reported

Eight days before Brooks questioned North, Alfonso Chardy’s *Miami Herald* article described officials operating outside ordinary departmental channels. Its account of North’s work with FEMA was part of a larger investigation of the NSC and covert operations. The surviving reproduced text attributes information to officials and congressional investigators and includes a FEMA spokesman acknowledging meetings with North between 1982 and 1984.

Chardy reported something much more serious than a routine relocation drill: proposals for martial law, military administration of state and local government, and a leading role for FEMA during a national crisis. He said the paper had obtained a 1982 memorandum by FEMA official John Brinkerhoff and an August 1984 objection from Attorney General William French Smith.

The passage quoted from Smith concerns FEMA exceeding its coordinating role and the creation of an emergency czar. That was an objection from within Reagan’s own administration. It gives the controversy an institutional basis beyond Brooks’s alarm. The article also acknowledged uncertainty over whether the proposed executive order had been signed and whether any signed version retained the martial-law provisions.

Smith’s intervention makes the fate of the proposal especially important. He was trying to stop or change a measure that other officials wanted. To establish an adopted plan, the crucial documents would include the final version, the approval and any instructions governing its use. The uncertainty over approval was still part of Chardy’s report when the story reached Brooks.

North’s involvement explains why the issue surfaced during Iran-Contra. Brooks had just been discussing a government staff that conducted operations while avoiding ordinary reporting and accountability. The documented concealment in [[iran-contra]] made his question about other secret responsibilities reasonable.

## Reading Giuffrida’s thesis

One source often offered as the intellectual blueprint is Louis Giuffrida’s 1970 Army War College thesis, *National Survival—Racial Imperative*. Giuffrida later directed FEMA. The paper is frequently described as a recommendation to imprison twenty-one million Black Americans, with Japanese American incarceration as its approved model.

The [thesis itself](https://archive.org/details/pdfy-h7zSh86fNBKrtRJU) takes a different course. Its summary calls racial prejudice a learned and dangerous response. Its conclusion constructs a hypothetical national emergency: armed militants seize institutions, violence spreads, white vigilantes attack Black people indiscriminately, rumors multiply and demands for collective detention grow. It likens the rising racial panic to the climate surrounding the removal of Japanese Americans during the Second World War.

Giuffrida then asks how a government could implement an order to remove and detain Black residents from trouble spots. He calls such an order extremely unlikely. Using the wartime Japanese removal rate, he estimates that processing twenty-one million people would take more than fourteen years. The number functions as a calculation of the task’s scale, rather than a target he instructs officials to achieve.

Using militant groups’ own claims of strength, the paper goes on to examine a smaller scenario involving half a million militants. It calculates military-police requirements and camp construction using prisoner-of-war and civilian-internee doctrine. Here domestic political conflict becomes a problem expressed in units, enclosures, processing capacity and construction labor. Classifying someone as a militant would have enormous consequences, while the calculation itself supplies no fair procedure for deciding who belongs in that category.

Giuffrida also refuses to assume that the government would refrain from drastic action simply because calmer judgment might reject it. His discussion distinguishes the wisdom of a measure from its constitutionality and invokes earlier assertions of emergency authority. He treats drastic coercion as a possibility a military planner cannot simply wish away.

The final pages turn to the armed forces’ racial relations and argue for fair treatment, leaders who listen to grievances and action against discrimination, poverty and social isolation. The paper’s stated remedy is broader social reform. He combines that remedy with confidence in military discipline and a willingness to calculate the requirements of detention.

A student thesis also has a different status from an approved government contingency plan. The title page identifies it as research by an individual officer. His later appointment makes his earlier thinking relevant, but it does not establish that every scenario in the paper became FEMA policy. FEMA’s later decisions would show whether his calculations were taken up, modified or set aside.

## The precedent was an injustice

The United States removed and confined people of Japanese ancestry during the Second World War, including American citizens. The Commission on Wartime Relocation and Internment of Civilians later examined the decisions, heard hundreds of witnesses and reviewed the government record.

Its [1982 report](https://www.archives.gov/files/research/japanese-americans/justice-denied/summary.pdf) described exclusion, removal and detention affecting approximately 120,000 people without individual review. It rejected military necessity as the explanation for the central decisions and identified racial prejudice, wartime fear and failed political leadership. The costs included lost property, interrupted careers, stigma and years of confinement.

The legal history was also less uniform than the claim that the Supreme Court simply approved internment. The commission distinguished the Court’s acceptance of wartime exclusion from its rejection of continued detention of admittedly loyal citizens without statutory authority. A ruling on one order did not validate every action the government took against the affected population.

In wartime, officials had treated ancestry as evidence against individuals whose loyalty they had not examined. Public institutions can participate in that mistake rather than correct it quickly. Emergency plans therefore deserve examination for the decisions they make easy: who is categorized, who reviews the categorization, who can order custody, and whether a person has a meaningful route to challenge it. The earlier injustice establishes that these safeguards can fail. It does not identify the contents of a later, uninspected plan.

## Instructions to prepare are not unlimited powers

Executive orders are another part of the Rex 84 story often presented through lists of alarming functions: control of food, transport, communications, labor and finance. The lists cover much of what makes ordinary life possible. Reading the instructions around them changes what those assignments mean.

Nixon’s Executive Order 11490, issued in 1969, consolidated preparedness responsibilities across the government. Section 105 expressly said that the assignments did not authorize implementation of the resulting plans. Implementation required authority supplied by legislation or a presidential order grounded in statutes or the Constitution.

Reagan’s Executive Order 12656 made a similar distinction in 1988. It defined national-security emergencies broadly and directed extensive preparation, but required consistency with the Constitution and laws and preservation of constitutional government. Section 102 said the order itself supplied no authority to execute the plans. It also postdated Rex 84 by four years; it cannot be the instrument that authorized the 1984 exercise.

Take food distribution. Assigning a department to prepare for disrupted production and transport answers who should work out the problem in advance. An actual order compelling delivery, taking property or restricting someone’s movement raises further questions about authority, conditions and remedies. The planning assignment cannot answer all of them simply because food appears in its text.

The same applies to succession. Federal law specifies who may act if both the presidency and vice presidency are vacant or their occupants cannot serve. It includes elected legislative officers and, after them, eligible Cabinet officers appointed with Senate consent. An exercise may need someone to play the president so that other participants can practice receiving decisions. That role is different from the office the participant holds outside the rehearsal. A person helping run an exercise is not necessarily the person legally entitled to succeed. Moving a team underground cannot, by itself, put a private citizen into that line.

None of this guarantees lawful behavior. Officials can exceed an assignment, conceal an instruction or rely on a strained interpretation. But a claim of unlawful power becomes assessable when it identifies that instruction or interpretation and compares it with the law. Merely repeating a preparedness order’s title can hide both its limits and the particular point at which someone might evade them.

## A plan in the safe

The internal correspondence in the FEMA release shows how agencies struggled to explain these boundaries. One staff memorandum discusses inquiries from Representative James Talent about Garden Plot, the military civil-disturbance plan. FEMA personnel were describing their role as minimal, or as no role at all. The writer wondered why, if FEMA had no role, a copy of the plan was locked in an official’s safe.

The internal question exposes the awkwardness of the public wording. Possessing a plan might be necessary for an agency working alongside its users. Calling that situation “no role” invites precisely the question the staff member asked.

Other records in the package supply that answer in more detail. A May 1995 information paper records calls to military officials. It describes Garden Plot as the Defense Department’s civil-disturbance plan, with no assigned lead or supporting role for FEMA. Military authorities would nevertheless contact FEMA personnel if they activated it. FEMA could coordinate recovery after damage sufficiently serious to produce a presidential disaster declaration.

Letters to members of Congress in 1996 distinguished the military’s support to Justice Department law-enforcement operations from FEMA’s parallel disaster work. They used St. Croix after Hurricane Hugo and Los Angeles after the Rodney King trial as examples of operations occurring in the same place with different responsibilities.

The separation could be harder to maintain in the field than to describe in a letter. Concurrent operations can compete for resources, and the public may experience them as a single government presence. The letters still describe coordination among departments with different assignments, rather than FEMA exercising general command over the military. Garden Plot, Rex 84 and continuity planning should be connected through their actual responsibilities, not collapsed into a single name.

## What changed after September 11

Continuity preparations were used in response to a real attack in 2001. In March 2002, George W. Bush publicly defended measures to preserve an operating government if Washington were attacked, explaining that this was one reason the vice president had gone to undisclosed locations. Keeping officials apart could protect the government against the loss of several leaders at once.

That acknowledgement establishes the practical use of continuity arrangements. It does not establish activation of a Rex 84 mass-detention plan or suspension of Congress. The Congressional Research Service’s later discussion of continuity also examined oversight responsibilities and the difficulty of coordinating them across committees. Secrecy and institutional fragmentation were problems to address, rather than evidence that Congress had ceased to have a role.

Later surveillance and detention controversies have their own records. Tim Shorrock’s 2008 reporting described an alleged database called Main Core, drawing on former officials and accounts involving the PROMIS software dispute. Some sources were relaying other people’s knowledge or offering interpretations; the article itself treated important connections as unconfirmed. It identifies a further allegation to investigate. The reported sources do not establish an unbroken detention program running from Rex 84 to 2008. The wider history of [[mass-surveillance]] needs the particulars of each program.

The 2012 defense authorization law is another example. Its detention provisions generated a genuine dispute over the scope of military custody. In [*Hedges v. Obama*](https://law.justia.com/cases/federal/appellate-courts/ca2/12-3176/12-3176-2013-07-17.html), the Second Circuit vacated an injunction in 2013 because the plaintiffs lacked standing: they had not shown the required injury or credible threat linked to the provision they wanted the court to halt. It read section 1021 as leaving existing detention authority over citizens, lawful permanent residents and people captured in the United States unchanged. The court expressly left the scope of that earlier authority unresolved. Section 1022’s military-custody requirement expressly did not extend to citizens.

The court left the plaintiffs’ constitutional claims undecided. Losing a challenge on standing grounds is not the same as a court approving every feared use of a law. Nor does the controversy show that a 1984 exercise had finally become legislation. Each proposed exercise of custody needs its own legal and factual account.

Rex 84 brings these questions together because emergency preparation makes it possible to act quickly, while secrecy makes it harder to inspect what action has been prepared. There are sound reasons to protect a relocation site’s vulnerabilities. The legal identity of the person entitled to command, the conditions for using force and the procedures for challenging detention are a different kind of information. Keeping them understandable is part of preserving constitutional government.

Brooks’s question remains useful for that reason. He was asking what an official had helped prepare and whether it crossed a constitutional boundary. The public exchange did not settle the answer. An adequate answer would have identified North’s assignment, the instructions he helped prepare and the authority claimed for them. The people watching the hearing still had no explanation from the official who had worked on the plans.
