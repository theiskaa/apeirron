In February 1982, Attorney General William French Smith explained why a new agreement with the CIA omitted a requirement to report certain narcotics crimes. He pointed to the agency’s existing cooperation with drug enforcement and said he expected it to continue. A formal obligation had been left out on the strength of an assurance that the work was already being done.

The [letter survives alongside the agreement](https://irp.fas.org/cia/product/cocaine2/13.gif). It raises a question that runs through this history: how did officials treat information about traffickers when those people, or the organizations around them, served another American purpose?

The Contra war in Nicaragua left an unusually substantial record of those decisions: a Senate inquiry, CIA and Justice Department investigations, correspondence, and accounts of particular interventions. Some relationships were tolerated. Some information failed to reach the people who needed it. Some sweeping accusations were not substantiated. Understanding the history requires keeping those outcomes in view together.

## The war created a useful cover

After Nicaragua’s Sandinistas overthrew the Somoza regime in 1979, the Reagan administration supported armed groups seeking to defeat them. The Contras needed transport, supplies, bases, and money. Congressional restrictions and changes in authorized support made the arrangements more complicated. The wider [[iran-contra]] affair concerns the attempt to sustain that war through secret arrangements, including the diversion of proceeds from arms sales to Iran.

A supply network could also be useful to a trafficker. A pilot accustomed to remote airstrips, a company moving cargo, or an armed group controlling territory might provide services that a drug organization needed. The activities could overlap without every participant sharing the same purpose. A trafficker might donate to a political cause because the association offered protection or introductions. A commander might accept help because it kept his forces supplied.

The Senate subcommittee chaired by John Kerry examined these connections in *Drugs, Law Enforcement and Foreign Policy*, a report dated December 1988 and released in 1989. Its [Contra chapter](https://nsarchive2.gwu.edu/NSAEBB/NSAEBB113/north06.pdf) found substantial trafficking connections among supporters and suppliers, and assistance to Contra elements from traffickers. It also described American government payments to companies whose principals were under investigation or indictment for drug offenses.

Those were findings about a network and the handling of known problems within it. They did not establish that the entire war was financed by cocaine, that every supply flight returned with drugs, or that American officials had devised the crack market. The report’s central criticism concerned foreign-policy priorities obstructing narcotics enforcement. That criticism is serious enough without enlarging it into a different finding.

A company on a government contract acquires something beyond the payment. Its aircraft movements and business relationships can appear legitimate, and officials may become reluctant to disrupt a service they need. Whether that reluctance becomes deliberate protection depends on what officials know and what they do next. The records permit that sequence to be examined in particular cases.

## A supplier the files could not find

SETCO, a Honduran aviation company used to support the Contras, shows how the sequence could break down. The Kerry report recorded a humanitarian-assistance payment of roughly $186,000. It cited law-enforcement information connecting the company to trafficker Juan Matta Ballesteros. A carrier associated with serious allegations had entered the supply arrangements funded by the American government.

The CIA inspector general’s [later account of SETCO](https://irp.fas.org/cia/product/cocaine2/pilots.html) adds a revealing administrative failure. When Justice asked the agency for information in 1989, a search produced a response saying there were no records of the company. Yet people in the Contra task force knew SETCO as a supplier. The officer handling the search explained the result as a failure to enter SETCO in the relevant database. A check with the operational desk had not corrected the answer. The person listed as coordinating the response did not remember it, and no signed routing slip was found to establish that he had actually reviewed it.

The report also found no information showing CIA participation in the humanitarian office’s selection of SETCO. And it did not find that CIA had received allegations of SETCO aircraft carrying drugs during the Contra period. The agency knew the company as a supplier. The inquiry did not establish knowledge of a particular cocaine flight.

The failed records search is damaging on its own terms. An investigator asking the right institution could receive an answer that omitted knowledge held elsewhere within it. A negative database result looked like an institutional answer, even though the institution’s operational staff knew more. Information can be lost at that handoff without disappearing from every person’s memory.

The missing routing slip leaves part of the failure unexplained. The answer still left the agency’s operational knowledge of the supplier out of Justice’s reach.

## The commander officials wanted to keep

The case of Juan Ramón Rivas makes the conflict of priorities more explicit. Rivas was an important Contra commander. According to the inspector general’s [Northern Front findings](https://irp.fas.org/cia/product/cocaine2/north.html), information reaching CIA in late 1988 led to an inquiry about an earlier Colombian drug case. Rivas admitted involvement in the drug trade in 1979 and escaping imprisonment.

Officials then debated what that history required. In February 1989 the CIA task force recommended retaining him because removing him would harm the force. Agency lawyer John Rizzo argued that the known conduct was old, that current trafficking had not been established, and that the rules did not require severing the relationship on those facts. The DEA representative reportedly regarded the information as historical and requiring no action.

The eventual result was different from the initial recommendation. State Department officials wanted Rivas separated from the Contras. He announced his resignation in March. A May communication to immigration authorities and the FBI included his admitted drug history; a proposal to ask that his Contra service receive favorable consideration was omitted from the version sent.

Officials assigned different weights to a useful commander’s past, the agency’s rules, political exposure, and the condition of the force. The task force’s preference for retaining him did not prevail. Following that disagreement explains more than treating the American response as a single decision made by a single mind.

## What the reporting agreement left out

The [CIA inspector general’s review of reporting rules](https://irp.fas.org/cia/product/cocaine2/findings.html) describes a distinction that ordinary language easily obscures. An agency employee and a person acting for the agency were not always treated as the same category for reporting crimes.

The 1982 CIA–Justice agreement defined employees more narrowly than the preceding framework. Some people acting on the agency’s behalf therefore fell into the nonemployee category. Potential violations of federal narcotics law were absent from the list of nonemployee crimes that had to be reported to the attorney general. Reporting remained permitted. The exclusion did not make trafficking legal or confer immunity from investigation.

Smith’s accompanying letter acknowledged the omission and invoked cooperation with the DEA as its justification. The gap was therefore recognized, rather than discovered accidentally years later. A revised agreement in 1995 broadened the employee definition to include assets and others acting on an intelligence agency’s behalf, and expressly added narcotics crimes to the reportable nonemployee offenses.

The significance lies in what a formal duty does. It gives an officer a requirement that can be checked and a recipient who can expect information. Discretion leaves another judgment between the information and the referral. In a covert war, that judgment may involve the value of a source, the risk to a relationship, and fear that an investigation will expose an operation.

Cooperation between agencies can be sincere and still fail to cover a particular person. Passing strategic information about drug routes to the DEA is also different from referring an allegation about a useful contact to Justice for possible prosecution. One activity does not guarantee the other. The arrangement relied on officials to make that transition where the written obligation did not compel it.

## An intervention involving seized money

The California investigations contain an instance in which CIA contact with prosecutors is documented. In the 1983 “Frogman” cocaine case, money was seized from Julio Zavala. Letters subsequently claimed that some of it belonged to Contra organizations. About $36,800 was returned in 1984.

The CIA inspector general’s [account of the intervention](https://irp.fas.org/cia/product/cocaine/findings2.html) found that the agency contacted prosecutors to protect what it believed was a Contra support interest. An internal cable credited the CIA request with securing the return. Prosecutors later gave a different explanation: they said the decision rested on practical and evidentiary considerations, rather than agency pressure. Zavala was convicted; the intervention did not make the prosecution disappear.

The cable and the prosecutors’ recollections disagree about the agency’s influence. A cable can exaggerate an office’s success; a later recollection can minimize outside pressure. The contact itself is firmer than either account of its decisive effect.

That is enough to establish something important. An intelligence interest entered a decision concerning money seized from a drug defendant. It does not establish that the CIA owned the cocaine, directed the trafficking, or secured an acquittal. The inquiry should follow the actual intervention: who requested it, what information was offered, what the prosecutor accepted, and what changed as a result.

Zavala’s conviction and the return of the money belong in the same account. Neither event makes the other disappear.

## What “Dark Alliance” changed

In August 1996, [[gary-webb]]’s *San Jose Mercury News* series brought the issue to a much larger audience. His [opening article](https://www.narconews.com/darkalliance/drugs/day1main-2.html) followed Norwin Meneses, Danilo Blandón, and Los Angeles dealer Ricky Ross. It claimed a long-running flow of cocaine and millions of dollars to the Contra cause, and connected the network to the emergence of crack in Black neighborhoods.

The wording carried considerable weight. It presented the network as the first cocaine pipeline between Colombia’s cartels and those neighborhoods, and as helping ignite a national crack explosion. These were claims about scale, chronology, and market causation, beyond establishing that a supplier dealt cocaine and supported the Contras. They were open to investigation and criticism.

The [Justice Department inspector general’s inquiry](https://oig.justice.gov/sites/default/files/legacy/special/9712/exsump1.htm) found that Meneses and Blandón were significant traffickers who provided some support to the Contras. It did not substantiate the proposed CIA relationship or explain their treatment by Justice as a consequence of Contra connections. It also rejected a single-supplier account of the crack epidemic. Its remit concerned Justice’s conduct; the CIA inspector general conducted a separate inquiry into the agency.

The CIA inspector general also [reported no evidence of an organizational or employee conspiracy](https://irp.fas.org/cia/news/pr100898.html) to assist trafficking to raise money for the Contras or another purpose. That was its stated conclusion, alongside the case histories of reporting failures and disputed interventions. The agency’s headline and its detailed findings answer different questions: whether it found a trafficking conspiracy, and how officials handled allegations about people and organizations around its war.

Those conclusions do not cancel the wider Contra record. Nor do that record’s failures retroactively prove every claim in Webb’s series. A supplier can be important without being the origin of a national market. A donation can be real without reaching the sum a story reports. A failure to prosecute can arise from poor coordination, protection, insufficient evidence, or a mixture; the explanation has to be established for the case concerned.

The dispute over Webb’s reporting can also crowd out the relationships beyond his three main subjects. Criticism of the series’ account of crack’s origins does not settle what the CIA knew about other traffickers. The larger newspapers’ criticism cannot be converted, without further evidence, into proof that they were acting under an intelligence command. That claim belongs to an inquiry into media influence such as [[operation-mockingbird]], rather than being supplied by the outcome of this controversy alone.

A less familiar event shows a genuine conflict over publication. Justice inspector general Michael Bromwich completed his report in December 1997, but its release was delayed until July 1998. His [epilogue](https://oig.justice.gov/sites/default/files/legacy/special/9712/epilogue.htm) explained that the DEA had reactivated Blandón as an informant. Public discussion of his cooperation could endanger him and the operation. Bromwich objected to the delay and the inadequate consultation about reusing the witness. He said the released report was unchanged.

Blandón’s continuing usefulness to the government had become a reason to postpone the public account of his earlier usefulness. Officials invoked an active investigation and safety; the inspector general invoked the public interest in disclosure. Those competing judgments are visible in the record.

## The earlier argument in Laos

The Contra evidence is sometimes presented as proof of every earlier claim about intelligence and narcotics. Laos has its own contested history. The CIA’s secret war there depended on local forces and extensive air transport, including the agency-owned Air America. Historian Alfred McCoy argued that American alliances and protection enabled the regional opium trade, and that aircraft serving the war transported opium.

McCoy’s [published 1972 correspondence with the CIA](https://www.nybooks.com/articles/1972/09/21/a-correspondence-with-the-cia/) preserves both that argument and the agency’s denial. It also records a publishing dispute. CIA official Cord Meyer sought advance access to the manuscript. Harper & Row ultimately supplied it despite McCoy’s objection, after which the agency challenged the book’s claims. The book was published. The sequence is more specific than saying that the publisher refused the request or that the CIA had no response.

A later history by William Leary, [published by the CIA](https://biotech.law.lsu.edu/cases/nat-sec/Vietnam/air-america.htm), defended Air America against the trafficking charge while acknowledging agency knowledge of the regional trade and the precedence given to fighting the war. McCoy and Leary disagree about a consequential part of the mechanism: the airline’s role. That disagreement cannot be dissolved by calling the history a fully admitted operation.

The comparison with the Contras is strongest at the level of priorities. An agency can know that narcotics circulate among the people and territories on which a war depends and still devote its effort elsewhere. Establishing who carried a particular shipment requires a different kind of evidence. A shared political justification does not establish a shared aircraft, cargo, or chain of command.

## Afghanistan and the cost of competing purposes

Afghanistan supplies a later comparison without requiring a claim that the CIA directed its heroin industry. In its [2018 counternarcotics assessment](https://youarewithinthenorms.com/wp-content/uploads/2025/02/SIGAR-18-52-LL.pdf), the Special Inspector General for Afghanistan Reconstruction reported roughly $8.62 billion allocated from 2002 through 2017. It found no lasting reduction in poppy cultivation or opium production from the counterdrug programs, despite some useful local results and improved counterdrug capacity.

The report described more than unwillingness to act. Security was poor, strategies changed, programs were badly coordinated, and the agencies responsible for drug-control targets lacked the resources and influence held by military and intelligence organizations. Some development projects even increased production. Much of the American effort was real, yet it did not add up to the stated result.

The division of responsibility made a drug-control goal vulnerable to decisions taken elsewhere. The people with a drug-control objective may not control the relationships and resources that determine whether it can be achieved. Another office can have a reason to preserve a partner, continue a campaign, or postpone a disruptive investigation. No single official has to announce that trafficking is desirable for the resulting policy to tolerate it.

But failure and design remain different explanations. An increase in opium during an occupation does not, by itself, identify the people directing that increase or establish an American intention to supply a foreign drug market. Afghanistan’s farmers, traders, officials, and insurgents had interests of their own. An account that turns all of them into instruments of one intelligence service grants that service a control it has not demonstrated.

The related history of [[operation-cyclone]] concerns a different period and set of relationships. Similar problems of reliance on local allies make comparison worthwhile. They do not justify moving findings from one decade into another without tracing the people, decisions, and transactions between them.

## Complicity has to be described

The records establish more than an unfortunate coincidence between covert wars and drug trafficking. They show a recognized reporting gap, a failure to retrieve relevant supplier information, arguments for retaining a commander after learning of his drug history, and an intelligence intervention over seized money. These are decisions and failures for which institutions can be held responsible.

They establish less than a single centrally managed narcotics operation extending from Marseille to Los Angeles to Helmand. Different wars produced different arrangements, and different agencies sometimes acted at cross-purposes. Treating those differences as mere disguises makes it harder to explain why one relationship was preserved, another ended, and another was not properly examined.

The broader question belongs beside [[control-systems]] and [[shadow-elite]]: how can a government demand strict enforcement while leaving important relationships subject to exceptions? The useful answer begins with the exceptions’ actual terms. Who can decline a referral? Who decides that a contact remains useful? Who receives an incomplete answer? Who can inspect and challenge the decision?

Smith’s 1982 letter placed confidence in continuing cooperation where a reporting requirement might have stood. The later record shows why the distinction mattered. Once a government relies on someone for a mission, their other activities can become a problem it prefers to manage within the relationship. The point at which that preference overrides an investigation is where the history becomes specific enough to judge.
