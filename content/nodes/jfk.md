On November 24, 1963, Dallas police were moving Lee Harvey Oswald through the basement of their headquarters when Jack Ruby stepped forward and shot him. The transfer was being televised. Two days after President John F. Kennedy's assassination, the man accused of killing him was murdered in police custody, in front of officers, reporters, and a national audience.

Oswald would never face a trial. There would be no defense team testing the prosecution's witnesses in court, no account from him under examination, and no jury verdict. The government would have to explain both Kennedy's death and its failure to keep the suspect alive. It is difficult to imagine a worse beginning for an investigation that depended on public confidence.

The case against Oswald was substantial. The investigations also had serious failures, including the withholding of information about CIA plots against Fidel Castro. These facts have often been made to compete: either Oswald fired the shots and the authorities deserve belief, or the authorities concealed things and Oswald must have been framed. Yet a rifle can be connected to Oswald while an agency conceals information about a different part of the inquiry. The conflict becomes clearer when the shooting evidence is followed alongside the decisions about what investigators were allowed to know.

## From Elm Street to a presidential commission

Kennedy was shot around 12:30 p.m. on November 22 as his motorcade passed through Dealey Plaza. He and Jacqueline Kennedy occupied the back seat of an open limousine. Texas governor John Connally, who was also wounded, sat ahead of the president. The Texas School Book Depository overlooked the route from behind; a grassy slope and wooden fence stood ahead and to the limousine's right.

Witnesses heard shots and tried to locate their source. Police searched the Depository, where Oswald worked, and found a rifle on the sixth floor. Later that afternoon Oswald was arrested at the Texas Theatre. Police accused him of killing patrolman J. D. Tippit as well as the president. He denied responsibility.

President Lyndon Johnson created the Warren Commission on November 29, a week after the assassination. Chief Justice Earl Warren chaired it. Its members included legislators, former CIA director Allen Dulles, and John McCloy, a former senior government official and banker. In September 1964 it presented its conclusion: Oswald had fired the shots that killed Kennedy, and it had found no evidence that either he or Ruby belonged to a conspiracy to assassinate the president.

The commission assembled a large record of testimony and exhibits. It also relied heavily on the FBI and CIA for investigations into matters that could expose those agencies' own mistakes or secret activities. Dulles's presence created a particularly difficult problem: the former head of an agency was helping assess an investigation that depended on that agency's disclosures.

The resulting report contains a much less settled record than its concluding pages suggest. Witnesses sometimes disagreed with the reconstruction. Later investigators accepted much of the weapons evidence while criticizing the search for accomplices.

## The evidence connecting Oswald to the shooting

The rifle found in the Depository was a 6.5-millimeter Mannlicher-Carcano bearing serial number C2766. Investigators traced its sale to a mail order under the name A. Hidell, sent to a Dallas post-office box rented by Oswald. Handwriting examiners attributed the order and accompanying writing to him. Photographs showed him holding a rifle; Marina Oswald testified about his possession of one. A palmprint lifted from the recovered weapon was identified as his.

The rifle was also connected to the shooting through firearms examination. The FBI and later House investigators attributed the three cartridge cases found by the sixth-floor window to it, along with CE 399, the bullet found on a stretcher at Parkland Hospital, and two fragments recovered from the limousine. Marks left by a weapon on ammunition supplied a link distinct from the evidence that Oswald owned it. Those findings connected a particular rifle to the scene and recovered projectiles; identifying the person who fired it still required the other evidence.

The [Warren report's fourth chapter](https://www.archives.gov/research/jfk/warren-commission-report/chapter-4.html) lays out these links separately. Their cumulative force matters. A claim that the rifle was planted has to explain its relationship to Oswald before the assassination, not merely identify a disputed recollection about its discovery afterward.

Other parts of the reconstruction were less tidy. Oswald's colleague Buell Wesley Frazier drove him to work that morning and saw a long package, which Oswald described as curtain rods. The commission argued that the package contained the disassembled rifle. But Frazier's estimate of the package's size and the way he remembered Oswald carrying it created a difficulty: his description suggested something shorter than the rifle could have been when disassembled. The commission preferred the physical paper-bag evidence and other circumstances over that estimate.

Frazier had not testified that he drove Oswald and his rifle to work. The commission reached that conclusion by discounting part of what its own witness remembered.

Howard Brennan, who saw a man firing from a Depository window, presented another difficulty. Brennan did not positively identify Oswald at the initial lineup. He later explained his hesitation and said Oswald was the man he had seen. The commission accepted the identification with qualifications. His initial hesitation would have given a defense lawyer an obvious line of questioning.

Oswald's movements, the weapon evidence, and the Tippit shooting formed additional parts of the case. The commission treated his departure from the building, possession of a revolver, resistance at arrest, and statements it considered false as supporting evidence. Those circumstances cannot substitute for proving who fired at Kennedy, but neither can a reconstruction reasonably leave them out.

In 1979 the House Select Committee on Assassinations, or HSCA, also concluded that Oswald fired from the sixth-floor window and that his shots struck Kennedy from behind. Its later finding of probable conspiracy did not exonerate him. This is often lost when the two investigations are presented as giving opposite answers to everything.

Oswald's guilt was never established at trial. The surviving record gives historians substantial grounds to connect him to the attack, alongside testimony whose weaknesses a defense could have pressed.

## What the single-bullet theory asks us to accept

One of the central problems is the relationship between Kennedy's first wound and Connally's injuries. Investigators had to fit the wounds, the men's reactions, the rifle's operation, and the motorcade's changing position into a sequence lasting only seconds.

The single-bullet theory holds that one bullet entered Kennedy's upper back, left through the front of his neck, and then struck Connally, passing through his chest and injuring his right wrist and left thigh. A separate bullet caused Kennedy's fatal head wound. The theory links wounds that would otherwise require additional shots within a very short interval.

The familiar phrase “magic bullet” invites a particular picture: two men sitting upright, one directly behind the other, while a bullet turns in midair to find the next wound. Connally occupied a jump seat that placed him lower and farther inward than Kennedy, and the men were turning. The Sixth Floor Museum's [catalog description](https://emuseum.jfk.org/objects/11856/image-of-the-kennedys-and-governor-connally-in-the-limousine) discusses the seating difference and the misleading impression given by some photographs. A useful reconstruction has to place bodies and wounds in that actual arrangement. Drawing a line through two seated silhouettes chosen for convenience can make a possible trajectory appear impossible.

The recovered bullet, Commission Exhibit 399, presents another objection. It looks remarkably intact in commonly reproduced side views, given the damage attributed to it. Critics have argued that a projectile which passed through those tissues and struck bone should have been far more deformed. The later House committee consulted wound-ballistics expertise and accepted that a bullet of this type could produce the injuries without showing more deformation than CE 399 did. The disagreement concerns what this particular ammunition could do as it lost speed and changed orientation, rather than how damaged a bullet ought to look in the abstract.

Connally's recollection remained important. He believed he had been struck by a different shot from the one that first hit Kennedy. Investigators had to compare that perception with film timing and the possible delay between an injury and a visible reaction. A witness may remember the order of sounds and sensations sincerely without being able to time them to a fraction of a second. Equally, a reconstruction should explain why it departs from the wounded man's account.

The Warren Commission described the evidence that one bullet injured both men as persuasive, while acknowledging differences of opinion among its members. Its report did not determine which of the three shots it believed were fired had missed. Accounts that specify an exact sequence and interval more confidently than the report itself create a certainty the evidence does not supply.

One detail supporting the account was the oval shape of the entrance wound in Connally's back. The House panel considered that consistent with a bullet beginning to yaw, or turn away from flying point-first, after passing through an intervening object. The proposed sequence thus had to account for the condition of the bullet as it reached Connally as well as the positions of the two men. This helped explain why the panel regarded passage through Kennedy as plausible.

The [HSCA's medical and trajectory discussion](https://www.archives.gov/research/jfk/select-committee-report/part-1a.html) returned to these problems. Its experts judged the single-bullet account feasible. Body positions, wound locations, and reaction times remained sources of uncertainty. Neither a diagram that places the two men conveniently in line nor one that makes the bullet turn corners can replace a reconstruction constrained by the film and medical record.

## The film and the autopsy

Abraham Zapruder's home movie records Kennedy's fatal injury and subsequent movement. Watching the president's head and upper body move backward is disturbing, and for many viewers it appears to show a shot from the front. The film has become a kind of private demonstration: once someone has seen it, an official explanation can feel like a demand to disbelieve their own eyes.

But a wounded body is not a rigid object on a frictionless surface. The direction of its later movement cannot by itself identify a bullet's entry point. The House committee considered possible muscular responses to brain injury and concluded that backward movement was compatible with a shot from behind. Its medical panel based its findings on wound evidence, including autopsy photographs and X-rays, rather than treating the visible recoil as a sufficient trajectory measurement.

The autopsy record gives critics stronger grounds for concern than a simple appeal to the film. Kennedy's body was taken from Dallas to Bethesda Naval Hospital, where the examination occurred under extraordinary pressure. Later investigators identified defects in the examination and its documentation. James Humes, the physician who led it, destroyed original notes. Descriptions of the wounds, photographs, diagrams, and later testimony have produced disputes that a more systematic initial examination might have prevented.

The Warren Commission did not inspect the autopsy photographs and X-rays. According to the House committee's account, concern about the Kennedy family's privacy, together with the commission's commitment to publish the evidence it examined, contributed to that decision. It relied instead on testimony about material its members and staff had not themselves reviewed.

This was an avoidable weakness. Sensitive medical evidence could have been examined under arrangements protecting privacy. A commission charged with determining the direction and number of shots needed independent scrutiny of the most relevant physical record.

Later panels did examine it. The HSCA employed forensic pathologists and specialists who checked whether the photographs and X-rays depicted Kennedy and showed signs of alteration. They reported no evidence of alteration. The pathology panel concluded that Kennedy had been struck by two bullets entering from behind. There were disagreements within the panel, notably involving Cyril Wecht, and differences from the original autopsy's placement of the head entry wound.

These later examinations complicate a familiar accusation that every official conclusion merely repeated the first autopsy. They also leave a troubling sequence: crucial material was initially withheld from the commission's own examination, and later experts had to reconstruct an event from records that should have been better from the start. Defects in that process are part of the history even where later reviewers agree on the direction of the shots.

## Why the House committee found probable conspiracy

The HSCA's conclusion is frequently quoted as though it established a second assassin through several independent kinds of physical evidence. Its decisive additional evidence was an audio recording.

A Dallas police radio channel had recorded sounds from an open microphone. Analysts working for the committee identified impulses they interpreted as gunshots. By comparing their patterns with test shots and echoes in Dealey Plaza, they inferred a shot from the grassy-knoll area in addition to shots from the Depository. If correct, that would imply two gunmen even though the medical evidence indicated that the bullets striking Kennedy came from behind: the second gunman could have missed.

This proposal gave scientific form to a longstanding suspicion. Witnesses had reported shots from different directions, and people had run toward the knoll. The acoustic analysis seemed to offer something more discriminating than impressions formed amid gunfire and echoes.

It also depended on several conditions. The recording had to contain the sounds of the assassination, the microphone had to be in a suitable location, and the apparent matches had to be unlikely to arise from other noise. An impressive match to a test pattern means little if the supposed gunfire occurred at a different time.

A National Research Council committee reexamined the evidence and rejected the acoustic basis for a second gunman in 1982. Its analysis placed the relevant impulses roughly a minute after the assassination. Synchronization between the police channels was central to that result. The disagreement therefore reached the identity of the recorded event, not merely a small adjustment to the estimated likelihood of a shot.

The NRC committee's [published conclusion](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/6750789/) was that the acoustic data did not support a second gunman. Subsequent technical exchanges continued, but the original HSCA finding cannot responsibly be presented as uncontested scientific confirmation of conspiracy.

Elsewhere, the committee identified weaknesses in protection, information sharing, and the search for accomplices. Its [summary](https://www.archives.gov/research/jfk/select-committee-report/summary.html) judged the earlier inquiry into Oswald's responsibility much more favorably than the inquiry into possible conspiracy. Its criticism of the earlier conspiracy inquiry had a documentary basis that survived the acoustic dispute. Its positive claim of another gunman had lost its principal scientific support.

## Oswald before Dallas

Oswald had lived in the Soviet Union, returned to the United States with his Russian wife, promoted the pro-Castro Fair Play for Cuba Committee in New Orleans, and visited Soviet and Cuban diplomatic facilities in Mexico City shortly before the assassination. These were substantial leads in a Cold War murder inquiry. They also made him a subject of intelligence interest before Kennedy died.

His return from the Soviet Union is sometimes described as inexplicably easy for a man who had renounced American citizenship. He had announced that intention, but had not completed formal renunciation. His citizenship therefore had a different legal status from the one the abbreviated story assumes. The [Warren Commission's conspiracy chapter](https://www.archives.gov/research/jfk/warren-commission-report/chapter-6.html) also records FBI contacts with him after his return. He was not simply welcomed home without official attention.

Investigators then had to establish what that attention amounted to. An intelligence service can hold a file on someone it distrusts, receives reports about, considers recruiting, or employs. These relationships are not interchangeable. A file can show what an office knew about him without showing that it controlled his activities.

Mexico City presents a harder documentary problem. Surveillance reports, cables, accounts of intercepted communications, and later statements have not always fitted together comfortably. Investigators had to determine who was observed, what was said, which records reached headquarters, and what was retained. Confusion about a photograph or recording can change the interpretation of a lead. It does not automatically tell us whether the cause was misidentification, poor handling, concealment, or an impersonation.

The House committee examined a concrete conflict concerning a purported recording. An FBI memorandum had suggested that agents familiar with Oswald listened to a voice that did not sound like him. The committee's subsequent interviews and file review did not substantiate that account: the relevant agents said they had not heard such a recording, and the committee concluded that the memorandum was mistaken. That weakened a proposed impersonation argument.

Other evidence supported Oswald's presence at the Cuban consulate. Silvia Duran identified him as the applicant she had assisted; the committee also examined the visa application and had its signatures assessed by handwriting experts. Those findings did not resolve every question about the surveillance system. They did give investigators a basis for placing Oswald there independent of the disputed recording.

## What the CIA withheld

The most consequential omission was the CIA's failure to tell the Warren Commission about its plots against Castro, including dealings with organized-crime figures. The [House committee's account of investigative failures](https://www.archives.gov/research/jfk/select-committee-report/part-1d.html) explains why that omission mattered.

Commission investigators were examining possible Cuban and underworld connections without knowing that the CIA itself had brought those worlds together in assassination planning. A lead that seemed remote within the information supplied to them might have looked considerably more significant had they known the operational background. Several former commission officials later told the House committee that disclosure would have changed their approach and opened additional lines of investigation.

Richard Helms explained the CIA's posture in terms of responding to specific requests. That placed the commission in an impossible position: to ask precisely about a secret activity, its staff first needed to know the activity existed. The agency could appear responsive while preserving control over the scope of the inquiry.

Compartmentalization added another obstacle. The CIA official serving as a working-level contact with the commission did not necessarily know what other parts of his agency were doing. An investigator could receive an honestly limited answer from one official while senior officials withheld the knowledge that made the answer inadequate.

The withheld information changed the possibilities investigators would have pursued. Whether one of those paths led to Dallas would then depend on tracing participants, assistance, or instructions. The Castro plots made that investigation necessary; their concealment made it harder.

The [[bay-of-pigs]] disaster supplies further context for the antagonism between Kennedy and parts of the intelligence and exile worlds. It explains why researchers pursue those networks. The number of angry or threatened interests, however, cannot select which person acted. Organized crime, anti-Castro militants, intelligence officials, and military leaders did not become one organization simply because accounts can assign each a motive.

Ruby's killing of Oswald sharpened the concern. His nightclub business and contacts gave investigators an obvious reason to examine underworld connections. At the same time, the circumstances of his access exposed a serious failure of police security. A claim that he was ordered to silence Oswald requires a link between the act everyone could see and the people alleged to have commissioned it. The government had lost the chance to test Oswald's account in court regardless of whether Ruby had received an order.

Poor security can explain how Ruby reached the prisoner, while leaving his purpose open to investigation. Conversely, evidence of Ruby's associations cannot by itself show that a police officer deliberately let him through. Responsibility for the security failure could be assigned without first solving every question about his motive.

## Vietnam and the temptation to reason backward

Kennedy's death preceded an enormous expansion of the American war in Vietnam. It is reasonable to ask how his policies might have differed from Johnson's. It is much harder to infer the cause of the assassination from the war that followed.

National Security Action Memorandum 263, issued in October 1963, approved recommendations that included plans to withdraw one thousand American military personnel by the end of the year. The underlying McNamara-Taylor report envisaged training Vietnamese personnel to take over essential functions by the end of 1965, allowing the bulk of American personnel to withdraw. These plans rested on judgments about progress in South Vietnam and the transfer of responsibilities. How Kennedy would have responded had those expectations failed remains a historical counterfactual.

Johnson's NSAM 273, issued on November 26, did not simply cancel withdrawal. Its second paragraph expressly retained the withdrawal objectives of the October 2 White House statement. Other provisions concerned increased activity and planning, and subsequent policy led toward escalation. But the two memoranda do not support a neat story in which a withdrawal order was formally reversed four days after Kennedy's death.

The [November memorandum](https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1961-63v04/d331) also developed from discussions held before Kennedy died. The actual sequence is more complicated than a dead president's policy being replaced by its opposite. Later profits, later decisions, and an institution's capacity for violence can guide an investigation. They cannot establish that its members arranged this killing.

## What the records can still change

The 1992 JFK Records Act created a new process for collecting and disclosing assassination records. The Assassination Records Review Board pursued access and preservation; it was not a new criminal commission charged with identifying the killer. It brought records from scattered agencies into a collection that researchers could use and challenged continued withholding.

The release history has also moved beyond the familiar account of files remaining classified through 2024. The National Archives' [release page](https://www.archives.gov/research/jfk/release-2025) states that records in the collection previously withheld for classification were released under the March 2025 directive. It lists further publication, including a January 2026 release. The same page distinguishes restrictions concerning court seals, grand-jury secrecy, tax information, and privately donated material. A statement about classification is therefore not a promise that every imaginable record survives and is freely accessible under every legal category.

Newly accessible records deserve examination by file and claim. Some illuminate intelligence practices or resolve the history of a communication without identifying another shooter. Others may alter an account of what an agency knew. Announcing either complete vindication or a solved conspiracy before doing that work repeats the old demand that an archive deliver a single verdict.

The evidence connecting Oswald to the shooting remains substantial. The withholding of relevant information and weaknesses in the conspiracy inquiry remain part of the case against the investigation's completeness. They concern identifiable decisions and omissions, including the CIA's control over disclosure of the Castro plots.

What deserves continued attention is specific: an inadequately pursued lead, a discrepancy between a cable and testimony, a decision about what the commission would be allowed to know. The public was entitled to an investigation with access to the relevant evidence. It did not receive that in 1964. Any later claim to have solved the murder owes the public an account that can withstand the examination the original investigation failed to complete.
