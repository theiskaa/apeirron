At lunch at Chequers on 16 December 1984, Mikhail Gorbachev talked about food packaging. He had visited the engineering company John Brown that morning, and some of its proposals interested him. The Soviet Union was losing food because it lacked the equipment to process, package and preserve it. Margaret Thatcher asked whether private enterprise would handle such problems better. Gorbachev defended the Soviet system.

The [British record](https://www.margaretthatcher.org/document/233558) follows their argument through production incentives, central planning, emigration and the miners’ strike then taking place in Britain. Thatcher pressed him about political prisoners. He insisted that Soviet laws governed the matter. They disagreed, sometimes sharply, and still wanted to develop relations between their countries. The participants defended their systems, looked for advantages and found areas where agreement might be possible.

A more dramatic account makes this meeting the public beginning of a secret recruitment story. Thatcher recognized a usable Soviet leader; Western services cultivated him; his reforms dismantled the state they had failed to defeat militarily. In this account, his welcome abroad explains the disappearance of his country. “Operation Glasnost” is an informal name for that claim. The documents examined here do not identify a confirmed intelligence program bearing that title.

Western interference supplies the theory with a substantial history to draw upon. American strategy included military pressure, economic restrictions, political influence and assistance to Soviet opponents. The difficulty lies in connecting those activities to the decisions that broke the Union apart. Were Gorbachev’s policies directed from abroad? Did Western governments want the particular dissolution that occurred? And how much authority did anyone still possess over the outcome in 1991?

Near the end, private records show foreign leaders seeking influence while Soviet leaders fought over institutions and badly misjudged what their partners would do next.

## What Washington wanted to change

Reagan’s National Security Decision Directive 75, signed in January 1983, states the American ambitions plainly. It set out three linked aims: resist Soviet expansion, encourage changes within the Soviet system, and negotiate agreements that served American interests. Its preferred internal changes included greater political and economic pluralism and a reduction in the governing elite’s privileged power.

Washington wanted to change how its adversary was governed. The directive called for an ideological offensive, stronger broadcasts into the Soviet bloc, support for human rights, restrictions on sensitive technology, and pressure on Moscow’s position in Eastern Europe and Afghanistan. It sought to limit the economic benefits the Soviet Union received from relations with the West, particularly where trade could strengthen Soviet military capabilities or create Western dependence on Soviet energy.

The Afghan war gives one concrete example. American weapons and money, distributed principally through Pakistan, helped an existing resistance continue fighting Soviet forces and their Afghan allies. The assistance increased the costs of intervention. Its effects depended on Pakistani choices, competing Afghan organizations and Soviet decisions about the war. The history developed in [[operation-cyclone]] includes withdrawal discussions before the arrival of Stinger missiles. It also includes a Soviet-backed Afghan government that survived the Soviet troop withdrawal. A successful pressure policy could change an adversary’s choices without determining every later event.

NSDD 75 also prescribed negotiation and exchanges. Cultural, educational and scientific contacts were useful when they encouraged the kind of gradual change Washington wanted. Military strength and economic restrictions were supposed to improve the terms on which the United States negotiated. The document anticipated a prolonged contest, uncertainty over Soviet leadership and the need to avoid both empty confrontation and expectations of a quick breakthrough.

Pressure and negotiation could serve the same purpose. Officials could want a less threatening, less authoritarian Soviet Union and still bargain with its government. They could want to weaken its control over Eastern Europe while preferring a stable government in Moscow that could implement an arms agreement. Their objectives also left room for disagreement about how much pressure was useful and what should follow success.

The directive establishes a policy of pressure and intended political change. It leaves a substantial gap between those aims and a controlled dissolution carried out by a recruited Soviet leader. The examined records do not supply the instructions or agency relationship that would connect the American policy to that further claim.

## The visitor who argued back

At Chequers, the future of that contest was still open. Gorbachev was visiting before he became Soviet leader in 1985. Thatcher’s interest in him gave him access and recognition. At such meetings, officials learn what a counterpart wants, test arguments and discover which concessions might produce a response. Each government was testing the prospects for a more useful relationship.

But the confidential British account hardly reads like a convert accepting instructions. Gorbachev asserted the superiority of the Soviet system. He acknowledged economic problems while arguing that the revolution and postwar reconstruction demonstrated what that system could achieve. When Thatcher challenged central planning, he defended its role and described reforms that would give enterprises and regional authorities more independence while retaining central control over important decisions.

The argument over emigration was less comfortable. Thatcher wanted to know what she could tell the press about people prevented from leaving the Soviet Union. Gorbachev eventually suggested she say that he had added nothing to the existing Soviet position. In the discussion of the miners’ strike, each accused the other’s political world of tolerating or using coercion. The approved British record retained these disagreements alongside the expressions of interest in cooperation.

The recorder heard two committed political opponents finding a conversation useful. Gorbachev’s willingness to talk fits his stated belief that reform and accommodation could strengthen his country. Understanding the failure means following what happened to the arrangements he pursued.

The same problem applies to foreign praise. Western leaders had ample reason to welcome reductions in confrontation and changes in Eastern Europe. Their enthusiasm reveals their preferences. It does not tell us whether the person receiving it shared all those preferences, understood their consequences or acted under their direction. Recognition abroad could strengthen his standing without giving his admirers control over his decisions.

## Reform loosened several kinds of power

Glasnost meant openness. In practice it widened the space for public criticism, historical disclosure and argument about how the country was governed. Perestroika meant restructuring: an effort to change institutions and economic management that developed into a struggle over much more than factory efficiency. Neither word described a single settled program carried intact from 1985 to 1991.

The way the country was governed made political reform especially hazardous. The USSR contained fifteen union republics, including Russia and Ukraine. The Communist Party’s hierarchy helped hold together institutions that formally belonged to different levels of government. Once party authority weakened, it became much harder to assume that a decision made at the center would automatically command obedience throughout the republics.

Gorbachev described this difficulty directly to Secretary of State James Baker in February 1990. The party was agreeing to give up its constitutional monopoly, and authority was shifting toward representative and economic institutions. He wanted stronger presidential powers while the republics demanded a new division of responsibilities. Competing authorities were acquiring a claim to govern before they had settled how to divide power. In Madrid the following year, he recalled how the 1989 elections had brought forward people who were now leading figures in the republics.

Economic reform raised similar disputes. More freedom for enterprises required decisions about prices, ownership, supply obligations, credit and social protection. A government could announce a market direction without having functioning markets or a clear answer to who would cover a loss. Meanwhile, citizens still needed bread, fuel and wages. Waiting for a constitutional settlement was itself costly.

Gorbachev was relying on central authority to hold the country together while dismantling much of the party structure through which it had been exercised. By 1991, Boris Yeltsin, president of Russia, had his own political base and institutions. Russia was the largest republic within the USSR; its leadership could weaken the Union government by claiming control of resources and functions previously exercised at Union level. Ukrainian leaders had their own constituents, independence movement and bargaining position. The relationship between Russia and the Soviet center was therefore a conflict inside the state, rather than a simple contest between Moscow and foreign capitals.

The opening also did not make every Soviet response peaceful. In January 1991, Soviet military intervention in Lithuania brought loss of life and an American condemnation. Asked whether Gorbachev had directly ordered the crackdown, Bush said he did not know. His answer left personal responsibility for that decision unresolved; the use of force itself was plain. The republics faced an uncertain mixture of negotiation, coercion and a weakening central command.

## A coup changed the bargaining

The August 1991 coup attempted to remove Gorbachev and halt the political course associated with him. He was cut off at his residence in Crimea while the plotters claimed authority in Moscow. Resistance centered on Yeltsin and the Russian parliament, with public opposition and divisions over the use of force helping defeat the attempt.

In his telephone conversation with Bush on 21 August, Gorbachev described being blockaded by land and sea and having regained presidential powers only shortly before the call. He credited Yeltsin and other republican leaders with resisting the coup. Bush expressed relief at his safety and said the United States had supported both him and Yeltsin. Gorbachev was describing his recovery from an attempt to remove him and acknowledging the politicians who had resisted it.

His return did not restore his previous position. The center’s institutions had supplied the attempted seizure of power; the republican institutions had supplied much of the successful resistance. Leaders who had defended constitutional government could now claim greater authority than the president who had needed their help.

The prospect of a renewed Union survived, but its terms became harder to settle. A union treaty would have to specify which powers the republics retained and which they delegated to a common government. Gorbachev wanted a state capable of acting, rather than an association whose members could disregard its decisions. Other leaders wanted common services without recreating the center they distrusted. Their need for one another did not produce agreement about who should govern. The new Union proposed by Gorbachev would have differed considerably from the state that existed before reform.

## The Union Washington hoped would survive

Before the coup, Bush had already stated a preference for a negotiated relationship between the center and the republics. In his August address in Kyiv, he supported Gorbachev’s reforms, warned against ethnic nationalism and spoke favorably of the proposed arrangements for greater republican autonomy within a continuing Union. He also spoke to an audience whose aspirations did not necessarily stop at the autonomy he favored. American preferences could frustrate independence movements as well as encourage them.

There were practical reasons for that position. Breaking up a nuclear superpower raised questions about military command, treaty obligations and who could make an agreement stick. A common economy also meant that a factory’s fuel, parts and customers could lie across several prospective state borders. Independence might answer the question of political sovereignty while leaving the daily operation of those systems unsettled. Preserving common institutions offered Washington continuity, even as it sought deep changes in the way those institutions worked.

On 5 September, National Security Adviser Brent Scowcroft sent Bush a [private assessment](https://nsarchive.gwu.edu/document/27265-document-2-memorandum-scowcroft-president-developments-ussr-september-5-1991) of the situation. He expected a new, voluntary Union, with greatly reduced central authority. Defense and foreign affairs were particularly important to the United States: Washington wanted someone who could control armed forces and carry out international commitments.

The memo also described Yeltsin’s conduct after the coup as alarming to other republics. His attempts to place Russians in Union institutions, together with warnings concerning borders if republics departed, had given Gorbachev a constituency among leaders worried about Russian dominance. Scowcroft thought the two men would continue cooperating while maneuvering against each other. Keeping them in that relationship served American interests.

He was wrong about a consequential part of the future. He considered uncontrolled disintegration an unlikely outcome and expected Ukraine to remain in the Union. Three months before the breakup, the president’s adviser was still expecting a settlement that would keep Ukraine inside.

At the same time, Scowcroft saw an opportunity for influence. Republics seeking economic models and external assistance would deal with Western governments and financial institutions. He urged engagement with the IMF and World Bank. The same memorandum that favored cooperation between Gorbachev and Yeltsin welcomed the opening for Western influence. Stabilization and advantage belonged to the same policy.

Baker’s conversation with Alexander Yakovlev on 13 September makes the economic conditions clearer. According to Yakovlev’s record, Baker wanted a prompt agreement among the republics and a credible move toward a market economy, using international financial institutions’ recommendations. Larger assistance depended on that direction and on clarification of political authority. He repeatedly warned that delay could make disintegration irreversible.

The conditions gave outsiders leverage and exposed Baker’s advice to criticism on several grounds: its economic assumptions, the speed it demanded and the amount of help on offer. But Baker was urging agreements that would preserve common economic arrangements, and he feared what would follow their failure. He was seeking economic change within arrangements he hoped would hold the republics together.

The distinction was still present at a private dinner in Madrid on 29 October. Gorbachev explained his determination to preserve a renewed Union, with common armed forces, an economic space and essential infrastructure. Bush asked about its prospects and Yeltsin’s intentions. Spain’s leaders also favored preserving a workable Union. This was a conversation about whether Gorbachev could achieve his objective, with considerable anxiety about the alternative.

Gorbachev had reason to put a hopeful face on his prospects in Madrid. Even allowing for that, the September memorandum, Yakovlev’s notes and the Madrid discussion agree on a practical concern: an orderly, reformed Union would solve problems that a sudden breakup would create. That concern appears in records made for different participants, rather than only in a public declaration of goodwill.

## Banks, timber and bread

In the Soviet State Council, even an agreement to cooperate quickly ran into arguments about who would have to do what. At the [meeting on 11 October](https://nsarchive.gwu.edu/document/27268-document-5-new-minutes-ussr-state-council), leaders debated an economic agreement intended to preserve cooperation among the republics. The disputes concerned institutions people would have to obey the following morning.

Uzbekistan’s Islam Karimov asked whether freeing prices would leave poorer republics permanently disadvantaged. Grigory Yavlinsky, presenting the agreement, argued that preserving the existing arrangements would be disastrous. He explained the difference between a general framework for trade and government promises to exchange specified quantities of timber and bricks. Enterprises given freedom to sell their output could frustrate the very deliveries governments had guaranteed.

Yeltsin said Russia would sign, but wanted supporting agreements before ratification and opposed financing bodies whose functions Russia had not delegated to the center. Ukraine’s Leonid Kravchuk also objected to centralizing authority. Kazakhstan’s Nursultan Nazarbayev pressed for speed: factories were stopping, infrastructure crossed republican boundaries, and even independent states would have to negotiate arrangements for using it.

They had reasons to cooperate and reasons to resist the proposed terms. Every delay worsened shortages; every grant of authority risked reestablishing a power a republic wanted to escape. An agreement could be approved in principle while arguments over its implementation deprived it of force. Signing and ratification were different hurdles: Yeltsin wanted the supporting arrangements settled before ratification, while Gorbachev wanted signatures promptly and the details completed between signature and ratification. Nazarbayev feared that the time needed to finish the documents would finish off the economy first.

By 4 November, Gorbachev was complaining that even the economic agreement already signed was proving difficult to implement. Yeltsin’s announced price reforms had created immediate fears about supplies. Gorbachev reported a sharp increase in bread purchases in Moscow and said shops were holding goods back in anticipation of higher prices. In the same meeting, the Ukrainian representative complained that mines had stopped for lack of timber Russia had failed to send.

Gorbachev and the Ukrainian representative were describing problems they wanted the others to help solve: frightened buyers, missing supplies and institutions unable to secure cooperation. The minutes also preserve an awkward exchange in which Yeltsin repeatedly asks to proceed with the agenda while Gorbachev tries to force a wider discussion. Gorbachev could convene the leaders. He could no longer make them treat his priorities as commands.

By this stage, the republics’ governments and enterprises could frustrate Gorbachev’s plans without waiting for his permission. The state was coming apart partly through decisions about what would no longer be paid for, delivered or obeyed. Those decisions had identifiable Soviet participants and immediate domestic stakes.

## The call before Gorbachev was told

Ukraine’s referendum on 1 December supplied a decisive public choice. More than 90 percent of those voting supported independence. Its parliament reported the result to other countries as confirmation of the independence declaration adopted in August. Gorbachev’s hope that Ukraine would stay in a renewed Union had not survived the vote.

On 8 December, the leaders of Russia, Ukraine and Belarus signed the agreement establishing the Commonwealth of Independent States. Yeltsin called Bush to explain what they had done. The [American memorandum](https://nsarchive.gwu.edu/document/27272-document-9-memorandum-telephone-conversation-bush-yeltsin-december-8-1991) records him describing negotiations for a new Union treaty as exhausted and promising continuity in international obligations, debt and control of nuclear weapons.

Yeltsin also told Bush that Gorbachev did not yet know the results. He wanted American understanding and was explaining an accomplished agreement. Bush said his government would study the text, asked about the center’s likely reaction and emphasized a peaceful process.

Yeltsin wanted Washington informed and reassured immediately. He emphasized that he was speaking before Gorbachev had been informed. The sequence in the memorandum runs from the republican leaders’ agreement to American consideration of its terms. The proposed common state had lost the support of the leaders of three of its founding republics, while its president was being informed after they had acted.

When Gorbachev resigned on 25 December, he said he had supported republican sovereignty within a preserved union state and opposed the course of dissolution. That was his account of his record, delivered when he could no longer determine the settlement. His defense of himself should be read alongside the earlier arguments in which he was still trying, unsuccessfully, to obtain agreement.

## Grievances with their own evidence

Two later arguments often draw this history back toward a single planned betrayal. One concerns NATO; the other concerns the influence and conduct of Western economic advisers. Both deserve examination in their own right.

In February 1990, Baker discussed German unification with Gorbachev using assurances against an eastward extension of NATO’s military jurisdiction. The Soviet conversation record contains those assurances. A British record of Foreign Minister Hans-Dietrich Genscher’s discussion with Douglas Hurd also explicitly considered Poland, so the broader concern about Eastern Europe cannot simply be erased from the diplomatic history.

The final German settlement contained written provisions. Article 5 set restrictions during and after Soviet troop withdrawal, including a bar on stationing or deploying foreign forces and nuclear weapons in the former East German territory after withdrawal. Article 6 preserved united Germany’s right to belong to alliances. It did not create a general treaty prohibition on all future NATO membership farther east. The relationship between the wider reassurances and the narrower provisions that became binding remains central to the dispute about what Soviet leaders had been led to expect. A promise can create a political expectation without appearing in the final treaty; the final treaty can also preserve rights that an earlier assurance appeared to limit. Assessing that dispute requires both records.

Gorbachev’s bargaining can be criticized without assuming he worked for the other side. He might have accepted inadequate guarantees, misjudged future governments or overestimated the security that cooperation would bring. Those criticisms concern choices we can inspect in diplomatic records.

The economic advisers’ record also contains more than retrospective suspicion. In its fiscal 2005 recovery summary, the U.S. Justice Department reported $30.5 million recovered from Harvard University and its agents in connection with a USAID agreement to advise Russia’s market transition. USAID’s September 2005 account described the prosecutors’ allegation: advisers Andrei Shleifer and Jonathan Hay had made prohibited investments in areas where they were advising Russian officials and used their influence to advance private financial interests. The civil settlement concerned identifiable people, grant conditions and undisclosed investments. It cannot, by itself, establish who directed Gorbachev’s reforms years earlier.

The later conduct deserves its own account of who advised whom, what financial interests they held and which safeguards failed. Using it to explain an earlier collapse skips those questions and the intervening years.

The available record supports a consequential Western role: pressure against Soviet power, encouragement of reform and efforts to shape the terms of economic change. It also shows the Bush administration trying to preserve cooperation between leaders whose struggle was dismantling the state. Gorbachev helped create political freedoms and rival authorities he could not ultimately reconcile with a functioning Union. Other Soviet leaders made choices he opposed. The most revealing failure was already visible in those council meetings: everyone needed the railways and deliveries to keep working, but they could no longer agree on a government with the authority to ensure that they did.
