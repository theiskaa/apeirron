During one weekend in March 2015, Lord & Taylor arranged for fifty fashion influencers to post photographs of themselves in the same paisley dress. They could style it individually, but the company supplied the dress, paid for the posts and approved them in advance. According to the [Federal Trade Commission](https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2016/03/lord-taylor-settles-ftc-charges-it-deceived-consumers-through-paid-article-online-fashion-magazine), the posts omitted the financial arrangement. What looked like a collection of personal recommendations had a common client.

An influencer might have liked the dress. A reader deciding how much weight to give a recommendation was missing a fact about why it appeared. The campaign borrowed the credibility of personal taste while withholding the transaction behind it.

The campaign is a small example of what is often called “invisible control”: influence exercised through arrangements the audience cannot readily see. The phrase can make persuasion sound more certain than it is. A sponsor can arrange for a message to appear, surround it with apparent approval and choose the messenger. Getting someone to accept it is a further task.

The effort can take several forms. Paying for a recommendation is deliberate coordination. A newsroom’s dependence on cheap, accessible sources can produce a bias without anyone buying a particular article. A feed can alter what people encounter while leaving some of their political views unchanged. Each arrangement gives its organizers an advantage, but the advantage works differently.

## Fifty versions of one advertisement

The [FTC’s complaint](https://www.ftc.gov/system/files/documents/cases/160315lordandtaylcmpt.pdf) describes the dress campaign as part of the launch of Design Lab, a clothing line aimed at younger women. Influencers received between $1,000 and $4,000 each, as well as the garment. Their contracts required the company’s Instagram designation and the campaign hashtag. The posts were checked before publication; payment disclosures were absent.

The instructions left the influencers room to make the photographs look personal. The sponsor controlled the product, timing and required identification. Their different styles helped the campaign look like separate expressions of taste.

The promotion also extended into *Nylon*. Lord & Taylor reviewed and approved a paid article and an Instagram placement that did not disclose the commercial arrangement. The [agency’s announcement](https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2016/03/lord-taylor-settles-ftc-charges-it-deceived-consumers-through-paid-article-online-fashion-magazine) reported that the influencers’ posts reached 11.4 million individual Instagram users over two days and that the dress sold out. Those figures describe the campaign’s reach and a sales outcome; they do not tell us which buyers would have purchased without it.

The complaint alleged deceptive representations and omissions. Lord & Taylor settled, and the FTC [approved a final consent order](https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2016/05/ftc-approves-final-lord-taylor-order-prohibiting-deceptive-advertising-techniques) in May 2016. The order prohibited presenting paid advertising as independent opinion and required disclosure of material connections with endorsers. The order addressed how the campaign was presented. It did not measure the response of each person who encountered it.

The concealed sponsorship changed what the apparent enthusiasm meant. Seeing several apparently independent recommendations may reasonably count for more than seeing the same advertisement several times. When the recommendations come from one sponsor, the reader has been encouraged to count one campaign as many separate judgments.

Disclosure would not require the reader to dislike the dress or distrust everything the influencer says. It would supply the missing context. A paid recommendation can still contain a sensible observation. Concealing the payment asks the reader to extend a kind of trust the sponsor has not earned.

The retailer had purchased a way into relationships it did not own. The sponsor arranged the circumstances under which the garment appeared desirable and popular, then let the photographs carry the recommendation.

## The world we cannot inspect

Walter Lippmann’s *Public Opinion*, published in 1922, begins from a difficulty that advertising exploits but did not create. Most of the world on which our decisions depend lies beyond direct experience. We cannot personally inspect a war, a national economy or the conditions inside every institution we vote to support. We act on accounts of those things.

Lippmann called attention to the difference between the environment and the pictures people form of it. A picture can lag behind events, omit a decisive fact or organize true details into a misleading impression. The consequences of acting on it occur in the world outside the picture. Confidence does not make the two correspond.

His problem was broader than lying. An honest observer also has limited access and attention. Reporting requires selection, names and categories. Readers bring expectations that help them make sense of what they encounter, sometimes so quickly that they miss whatever does not fit. We need a manageable account even when we know how much it must leave out.

In chapter 24, [Lippmann distinguishes news from truth](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/6456/pg6456.html). News signals an event. Understanding requires discovering relevant facts and setting them in relation. A bankruptcy can become news through a recorded filing. Explaining why the business failed requires a further inquiry into conduct and circumstances. The public event and the adequate explanation arrive by different routes.

An official announcement exposes the gap. The announcement is a reportable event: a minister said something at a certain time. Its claim about conditions in a hospital or a war zone may be much harder to verify. A perfectly accurate account of the minister’s words can leave the public with an inaccurate understanding of the underlying condition. Checking that the minister said it still leaves the condition in the hospital to be established.

An institution responsible for an activity often also supplies the information used to assess it. Its account is accessible, attributable and ready for publication. The people affected may have observations that are difficult to assemble into a comparable report. A journalist with a deadline encounters an inequality in available evidence before deciding whom to believe.

An official can therefore influence coverage by being the easiest source to use. A briefing offers a time, a speaker and a claim that can be quoted without having to establish the underlying facts immediately. Investigating the claim may require travel, interviews or access to records held by the same institution. Repeating the claim may take minutes; checking it may take days.

Treating Lippmann as a teacher of deception leaves out the remedy he proposed. He wanted organized, independent work to make unseen facts intelligible. In the book’s final part, he discusses statistical and research bodies, comparable records and a separation between assembling knowledge and deciding policy. “Intelligence” here includes inquiry and analysis; it is not simply a euphemism for a secret service.

He gives the example of infant-mortality statistics making neglected lives visible to officials and voters. The people concerned had existed before the statistics. A public record could give their situation a presence in political decisions that casual attention had failed to provide.

The proposed cure has its own vulnerability. Better records can expose a condition that officials prefer to ignore. The people deciding what to measure can also shape the terms in which the condition becomes visible. For example, a hospital’s waiting-time figures might reveal delay while leaving the quality of treatment poorly described. Creating a measure does not remove the need to argue about what counts.

Lippmann acknowledged that expert organizations could become bureaucratic burdens and that experts themselves could exercise power. His account leaves a difficult question about who checks them. Comparable records can still make an institution’s conduct easier to challenge. A public that cannot assemble the evidence from scratch may be able to contest a decision once the evidence is available.

## Bernays and the borrowed recommendation

Edward Bernays saw a commercial opportunity in the same dependence on other people’s judgments. In *Propaganda* (1928), he argued for a profession able to shape public opinion through the groups and authorities people already trusted. He defended this work as part of making a complex society function, while describing the public in terms that give an organizer far more agency than the people being organized.

His [opening chapter](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/78634/pg78634-images.html) lists associations, periodicals and overlapping social groups. The variety helps explain how he expected persuasion to spread. A person might hear an idea in one professional or social setting and carry it into several others. Influence could travel through ordinary attachments rather than a single broadcast to an undifferentiated mass.

Chapter 4 makes the technique concrete. Bernays contrasts a direct appeal to buy bacon with an approach through physicians, whose advice can carry authority over eating habits. He also imagines promoting pianos by encouraging the fashion for a music room. Decorators, architects and prominent guests help make such a room desirable; a piano becomes a fitting purchase for a setting the campaign has helped create.

The piano passage is a proposed method, not an independent sales study. The persuasive work begins well before the customer reaches a shop. The organizer does not begin by arguing that a particular instrument has better strings or a lower price. He works on the situation in which buying one will feel appropriate. By then a music room has become something worth wanting, and the piano has a place in it.

The influencer campaign follows a comparable route through a trusted intermediary. A follower is attached to a person’s taste, not to the retailer’s advertising department. Paying that person for an apparently independent recommendation lets the retailer enter the relationship without appearing under its own name as the speaker.

Trusted advice is part of ordinary life. A doctor can explain sound evidence; a decorator can make a useful suggestion. Concealing a commission lets an adviser trade on that everyday trust without giving the audience a fair account of the relationship.

Bernays’s confidence can make the method sound like engineering with predictable results. Yet in the same chapter he admits that human motivation is imperfectly understood and that events outside a campaign can defeat it. A planned aviation spectacle, for instance, might be overshadowed by something more dramatic. A rival event can undo the work of choosing the moment and preparing the audience.

The audience has loyalties, competing needs and other things to do. Even successful persuasion usually works through something the audience already values. A campaign that invokes independence depends on independence being attractive; it does not invent that value from nothing.

What money buys is an advantage in arranging the encounter. Someone can pay for research, repetition, access and a choice of messengers. The recipient may spend only a few seconds on an encounter the sponsor has spent weeks preparing.

## Selection without a secret instruction

A sponsored post gives investigators a contract to look for. Influence can be less explicit. Edward Herman and Noam Chomsky’s *Manufacturing Consent* (1988) proposes that commercial media select and frame news through institutional pressures even when journalists work sincerely.

Their [propaganda model](https://chomsky.info/consent01/) names five filters: ownership and profit incentives, advertising, reliance on powerful sources, organized punitive responses they call flak, and the anticommunist framework of the period. The model explains patterns through resources, routines and incentives, rather than requiring an instruction to every reporter. It is an argument about the system’s selection of news and viewpoints, not a finding that each journalist knowingly lies.

A hypothetical local reporting choice makes the source problem easier to see. A city authority announces a redevelopment scheme. It supplies a timetable, renderings, an economic estimate and a spokesperson who answers the phone. Tenants facing displacement have scattered documents, conflicting schedules and no paid person responsible for responding to the newspaper.

A reporter can accurately quote both a city representative and a tenant while still allowing the city to define the question. If the article asks only whether construction will meet its schedule, the dispute over who should remain in the neighborhood has already been pushed aside. A dissenting quotation does not necessarily change the terms of the story.

This example is an illustration, not evidence that every redevelopment report takes that form. It shows why counting quotations alone would miss part of the proposed bias. To examine an actual outlet, we would need its coverage, source choices, editorial decisions and the stories it failed to pursue.

The model locates some pressures outside the individual journalist’s intention. Its difficulty is specifying how much variation it can explain. Journalists also expose institutions on which they depend; owners and advertisers may disagree; an audience may reward reporting that an official dislikes. A useful account of pressure has to explain those outcomes too.

Advertising creates a related problem at a different stage. Imagine an editor considering two equally costly investigations. One appeals to a readership attractive to the advertisers supporting the publication; the other concerns people with little spending power. No advertiser has to call the editor for the difference to enter a calculation about which work the business can afford. A subscription publication faces a different set of pressures, including the expectations of the people willing to pay for it. Changing the source of revenue changes the dependency; it does not make every editorial choice independent of money.

Assessing the model means comparing the attention given to different subjects and the authority granted to different sources. A costly investigation of an advertiser deserves attention alongside a pattern of favorable coverage. The explanation has to account for both the pressure to conform and the occasions on which journalists resist it.

## Changing the feed, measuring the result

On a digital platform, researchers can sometimes change the order of presentation and compare the results. Such experiments put the connection between exposure and belief to a test that accounts of persuasion often lack.

A study led by Andrew Guess and colleagues, published in 2023, assigned consenting Facebook and Instagram users to reverse-chronological feeds during the 2020 American election period. Compared with the platforms’ default feeds as they operated during that election, the change reduced time spent and activity and altered the content encountered. Yet the researchers reported no significant change in issue polarization, partisan hostility, political knowledge or other key attitudes over the three-month study. The [author-hosted abstract](https://andyguess.com/publication/doi-10-1126-science-abp-9364/) describes both findings.

Time spent and political judgment moved differently in this experiment. Changing what people see is an exercise of power over distribution. Changing their settled judgments is a further achievement. A person may encounter more of a subject, reject a message or retain an earlier view. A measurement of exposure cannot tell us which occurred.

Another experiment reached a different result. In research published in *Nature* in 2026, Germain Gauthier and colleagues studied active American X users in 2023. Participants were asked to use randomly assigned algorithmic or chronological feeds for about seven weeks. Among users previously on chronological feeds, switching to the algorithmic feed shifted some policy and current-event opinions in a conservative direction. Switching the algorithm off for those already using it did not produce a comparable reversal. Neither intervention significantly changed self-reported party identity or partisan hostility.

The [researchers also observed](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-026-10098-2) changes in the accounts people followed. Users who switched the algorithm off continued to follow accounts previously encountered, leaving an earlier influence in place. The authors offer this as a mechanism for the asymmetry, while noting other differences between the groups and the limits of the platform and period studied.

These are different experiments, on different platforms and occasions. Their differences resist a single verdict on algorithms as a whole. Effects depend on what changes, whom it changes for and which outcome is measured. Party identity, opinion about a current event and time spent scrolling are different results.

The persistence question is especially important. Returning a setting to its earlier state need not undo the relationships formed while it was active. That makes a short experiment in turning something off an imperfect mirror of the process by which it entered someone’s life. A longer study could follow the relationships as well as the setting.

There is also a public problem in who can obtain the information needed to test these claims. In its [account of the collaboration](https://medium.com/@2020_election_research_project/us-2020-facebook-instagram-election-study-frequently-asked-questions-faq-266d30cbe95b), the Meta election-study team explains both its reliance on the company and safeguards intended to protect academic control over publication. The team also warned against generalizing its election-period results to an unchanging “standard” algorithm. Cooperation enabled the interventions while requiring conditions and disclosure. The X study was conducted independently, with other limits on observing participants’ compliance. Access to the system and independence from its owner are both valuable, and obtaining one does not automatically provide the other.

## Being able to see the arrangement

An account of a “control system” becomes misleading when it writes the public out of the story. Once every belief is explained as an implanted belief, disagreement becomes evidence that the implant worked. The claim no longer needs to encounter a person who resisted a campaign, a reporter who challenged an employer or an experiment that found a smaller effect than expected.

The more concrete accounts preserve those possibilities. A contract explains who paid for a post. An editorial record can explain why one story was commissioned and another dropped. An experiment can test a change in exposure against a change in an expressed opinion. These records identify decisions someone made and give other people grounds to challenge them.

Recognizing influence also leaves a question about choice. Someone may like a dress after seeing a paid post and still have chosen it freely in an ordinary sense. The objection is that the sponsor has shaped the information used in the choice while making that intervention difficult to recognize. There is no need to declare the buyer mindless to say they were owed a more candid presentation.

The missing disclosure in the dress campaign was modest: the person making the recommendation had been paid. Restoring it would not tell a viewer what to buy. It would let the viewer assess the recommendation with knowledge that the sponsor already possessed.

The same expectation belongs in public life. People will continue to rely on information they cannot produce themselves. The institutions providing it should make their interests and procedures open to examination, and leave room for accounts they did not commission. Independence begins to mean something when a person can find out how a recommendation reached them and hear an alternative before deciding.
