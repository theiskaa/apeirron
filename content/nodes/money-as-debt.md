On 7 August 2013, Richard Werner borrowed €200,000 from a small cooperative bank in Bavaria. He had arranged to examine what the bank did when it credited his account: move money from somewhere else, or create a new deposit.

Bank staff processed the loan while the work was filmed and records were collected. Werner’s account showed a €200,000 deposit and a matching loan obligation. The paper also reports that he transferred the funds to an account at another bank the next day. His [published paper](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.irfa.2014.07.015) interpreted the observation as support for the view that an individual bank creates deposit money when lending. It examined one transaction; it did not demonstrate that a bank could lend without funding, regulation or risk.

The transaction joined two familiar obligations. A bank account is money to its holder and an obligation to the bank. A loan gives the bank a claim on its customer. Those two promises can be created together. The unfamiliar part is that the customer can then use the new deposit to buy something from a person who had nothing to do with the loan.

The capacity to create deposits lets commercial institutions help decide which purchases and projects receive new spending power. It also attracts a much stronger claim: because a bank creates the principal of a loan but not an additional sum for the interest, someone must always default unless borrowing grows forever. The claim can be tested by following what happens after the loan is made.

## A deposit and a loan are different promises

Suppose a bank lends a household £100,000. It records the loan as an asset: a claim to future repayments. It credits the household’s deposit account, recording a liability of the same amount. The customer can spend the deposit; the bank owes the customer that balance under the account’s terms.

The [Bank of England’s explanation of money creation](https://www.bankofengland.co.uk/quarterly-bulletin/2014/q1/money-creation-in-the-modern-economy) describes this process. A new loan need not begin with a saver depositing the same amount so that it can be passed along. The lending operation can itself create the deposit. This differs from an ordinary person lending a friend money by transferring part of an existing account balance.

The household has not become £100,000 wealthier through the bookkeeping alone. It has an asset and a liability. Nor has the bank made £100,000 in profit. The bank’s new asset comes with a matching obligation, and the asset may fail to pay as expected. Interest income is a future possibility, accompanied by costs and risk.

Now suppose the household buys a house. The seller receives the deposit and gives up the property. The buyer has acquired a house and still owes the loan. The bank’s debt to the depositor and the buyer’s debt to the bank are owed by different parties. Saying “the deposit is the loan” obscures that relationship: one can change hands while the other remains with the original borrower.

Lending is not the only operation that can create a deposit. A bank purchasing an asset from a nonbank seller can credit a deposit in payment. A central-bank securities purchase can also create a customer deposit through the seller’s bank. The [Fed’s deposit-growth research](https://www.federalreserve.gov/econres/notes/feds-notes/understanding-bank-deposit-growth-during-the-covid-19-pandemic-20220603.html) distinguishes purchases from bank and nonbank sellers. The origin of a balance cannot always be reduced to one household’s loan.

Not every debt functions as money. A household cannot ordinarily pay a supermarket by handing over another person’s mortgage contract. A deposit is useful because other people accept a payment from it and banks can settle the resulting obligations. The [Bank’s companion introduction](https://www.bankofengland.co.uk/-/media/boe/files/quarterly-bulletin/2014/money-in-the-modern-economy-an-introduction.pdf) distinguishes this widely accepted monetary claim from financial assets more generally.

“Money as debt” brings several meanings of debt together. It can identify the bank’s obligation represented by a deposit, or the borrower’s obligation that helped create it. It can also become a moral judgment about owing money. Fairness depends on the terms and circumstances of the transaction, as well as the obligations it creates.

## Creating the deposit is only the beginning

The bank must be able to honor payments from the new account. If the house seller uses another bank, the buyer’s bank has to settle with it. In the ordinary case, this involves transferring settlement balances through the banking system. Writing a customer’s deposit into existence does not give the originating bank an unlimited supply of the central-bank money used for settlement.

After the payment, the originating bank can have the long-term mortgage asset while the corresponding customer deposit is elsewhere. It needs a way to fund that position. Other deposits, borrowing or other liabilities may help, but their cost and reliability matter. A loan that looked profitable before allowing for funding costs may be unattractive afterward.

The bank remains responsible for its new obligation after the entry has been made. Its customers can move balances away, creditors can demand payment and assets can lose value. Creating the deposit begins a relationship that the bank has to sustain.

Capital supplies a different kind of protection from liquidity. Liquid resources help meet payments as they fall due. Equity—the difference between assets and liabilities—provides a cushion against losses; regulatory capital rules assess loss-absorbing resources using more detailed definitions. A bank can hold a valuable long-term asset yet lack the immediately available means to pay a departing depositor. It can also have ready cash while losses elsewhere leave too little asset value to meet its obligations overall.

Consider a bank with £110 of assets and £100 of liabilities. Its equity is £10. If assets lose £6 of value, the liabilities have not automatically fallen: equity has shrunk to £4. There was no need for £6 to be withdrawn from a particular customer’s account for the owners’ cushion to be reduced. Adding another loan and deposit would not restore the lost equity: it would add a further asset and a further obligation.

Credit can make spending possible before the borrower has earned the money to pay for it. A workshop can obtain equipment and repay from future sales. The danger lies in judging that future. A bank might instead finance a purchase whose apparent safety depends on a property price continuing to rise. The same deposit-creation entry can support very different uses of resources.

The workshop’s loan cannot make a machine, trained worker or quantity of steel appear by bookkeeping. If those resources are available, spending can bring them into use. If suppliers are already fully occupied, additional purchasing power may compete with other orders or raise the price offered. Financial permission to spend and physical capacity to produce are different constraints. A judgment about credit therefore has to examine what the spending can accomplish, not only whether the deposit can be created.

## What a reserve fraction can tell us

The familiar money-multiplier story begins with a deposit, assumes a required reserve fraction and follows successive rounds of lending and redepositing. With a ten-percent requirement, it can produce a neat numerical relationship between reserves and deposits. As an exercise under stated assumptions, the arithmetic is intelligible. As a description of how every modern bank decides to lend, it leaves out too much.

A bank considers expected returns, borrower risk, capital, liquidity, funding and regulation. The central bank’s policies influence those conditions. A fixed multiplication of reserves is not a substitute for following them. Equally, saying that lending creates deposits does not mean the central bank must finance every bank on whatever terms it requests.

The American requirement changed while banks continued making loans. The Federal Reserve [reduced its reserve requirement ratios to zero](https://www.federalreserve.gov/monetarypolicy/reservereq.htm), effective 26 March 2020. The [March 2020 announcement](https://www.federalreserve.gov/newsevents/pressreleases/monetary20200315b.htm) relates this to the ample-reserves approach to monetary policy. Zero required reserves did not mean banks stopped using reserves for settlement or ceased to face other prudential restrictions.

“Fractional-reserve banking” can describe an institution that promises access to deposits without holding every pound or dollar as immediately available central-bank money. It can also suggest the specific multiplier mechanism. Those are different claims. A maturity mismatch can create vulnerability even where a formal reserve fraction is not doing the work the textbook picture assigns to it.

A run is the practical difficulty. Depositors can seek immediate payment while the bank’s assets repay over years. Selling those assets quickly may produce a loss. Waiting may preserve value but fail to meet today’s demand. A deposit contract offering ready access and a loan financing long-lived activity put different timetables on the two sides of the bank.

Deposit insurance and emergency liquidity support address aspects of that vulnerability. In the United States, the [FDIC’s explanation](https://www.fdic.gov/resources/deposit-insurance/understanding-deposit-insurance) distinguishes insured deposits at insured banks from non-deposit investments. Its fund is financed through assessments on insured institutions and interest on invested funds. This arrangement protects covered depositors under specified rules; it is not a promise that every bank, investor or type of liability will be rescued.

## The interest that supposedly cannot be paid

The interest argument begins with correct arithmetic. A bank lending £100 does not necessarily credit the borrower with £110 just because £10 of interest will later be due. From the loan alone, the borrower receives less money than the sum of the scheduled payments.

The conclusion assumes the initial balance must cover all the payments to be made over time, with nothing returning from the bank. Following the payments gives a different result.

For a small example, track the entries associated with one loan. The bank lends £100. The borrower later pays £10 interest, leaving £90 in the borrower’s deposit. The bank then buys £10 of services from the borrower, crediting the account. The borrower again has £100 and uses it to repay the principal.

| Event | Borrower’s deposit | Principal still owed |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| Bank makes the loan | £100 | £100 |
| Borrower pays £10 interest | £90 | £100 |
| Bank buys £10 of services from borrower | £100 | £100 |
| Borrower repays the principal | £0 | £0 |

The interest has been paid, the principal has been repaid, and no second loan was required. The £10 interest payment reduced the deposit the bank owed and increased its income. Paying £10 for the service created a deposit obligation again and used that income. The borrower supplied something in return: the example does not make the cost of credit disappear. Other bank assets, capital, operating costs and taxes are left outside the illustration; its purpose is to test the supposed arithmetic impossibility, not to reproduce a functioning economy in four lines.

The distinction between principal and interest matters in the bank’s books. Repaying principal reduces the loan asset and deposit liability. Interest is income to the bank. Expenditure by the bank can put purchasing power back into other people’s accounts. Treating both payments as if they permanently removed money in exactly the same way makes the imagined shortage look inevitable.

The borrower in this example has a customer: the bank buying the service. If the bank withholds spending, the borrower cannot sell anything, and no other source of money is allowed into this closed example, repayment can fail. Changing those assumptions changes the result. The shortage comes from closing off every way for the borrower to receive the £10 again.

A borrower in an actual economy has to earn or obtain money from other people. Some will fail. A debtor may face harsh terms, a loss of customers or a deadline that arrives before expected income. Those circumstances explain a failure more precisely than saying the money for interest could never exist.

The amount of money at a moment is also different from the value of transactions over a year. The same balance can be transferred repeatedly. A comparison between the money stock today and the sum of payments due across many future years therefore needs a time dimension. A larger total of future obligations is a warning to investigate repayment capacity, not a proof that circulation must stop.

## Repayment, loss and a debt spiral

Repayment and default should not be collapsed into one operation. When a loan principal is repaid from a deposit, the corresponding loan asset and deposit liability can be reduced together. When a bank recognizes that a loan will not be recovered, it has to account for the loss on its asset. That loss does not automatically cancel the deposit now held by the person who sold the borrower a house.

Losses can nevertheless feed into a downturn. A bank facing losses can become less willing or able to lend. Borrowers may repay debt, postpone purchases or be refused refinancing. These processes can reinforce a contraction, but they do so through different entries and decisions. A loss on the bank’s asset can lead to stricter lending decisions, while a borrower’s repayment directly reduces an outstanding balance. The two routes can occur together without becoming the same transaction.

Irving Fisher’s [1933 debt-deflation paper](https://fraser.stlouisfed.org/files/docs/meltzer/fisdeb33.pdf) describes a more serious danger than the missing-interest argument. Heavy indebtedness can prompt distressed sales and debt repayment. Falling prices and weaker activity can then make the remaining obligations harder to bear. Fisher treats the sequence as interacting forces, and explicitly distinguishes cases in which debt reduction becomes self-defeating from milder cases.

For a simplified example, suppose a business owes a fixed nominal debt while the prices it receives and its income fall. Repaying part of the debt need not improve its position as much as expected if the income available to service the remainder falls faster. Other businesses cutting expenditure at the same time can worsen its prospects. An attempt to become safer individually can contribute to a situation in which many debtors become less secure together.

Fisher’s argument depends on the starting conditions: initial leverage, the timing of obligations, prices, income and responses to distress. It does not say that any economy charging interest must expand debt at a particular rate forever. The feedback can intensify distress after people have begun trying to reduce their debts. The dates on which payments fall due and the people receiving income matter as much as the grand total in a debt table.

## Changing who issues the money

Commercial lending gives banks discretion over who receives new purchasing power, subject to their constraints. That makes the selection of borrowers consequential beyond the private contract. Financing equipment that produces more goods and bidding up the price of existing land can have different effects even when each loan is repaid.

The prospect of public support adds another question. A system can make deposits reliable partly by providing insurance and access to emergency arrangements while leaving lending decisions and much of their return in private hands. The value of that public support belongs in an assessment of what banks receive and what they are required to provide in return.

Full-reserve and sovereign-money proposals try to change the bargain by separating the issue of transaction money from the extension of risky credit. In a full-backing arrangement, the balances offered as money would be matched by public money; lending would have to be financed through other funds rather than creating new transaction deposits at the same time. The attraction is that the public’s payment balances would be less exposed to a lender’s loan portfolio.

Jaromir Benes and Michael Kumhof’s [The Chicago Plan Revisited](https://www.imf.org/external/pubs/ft/wp/2012/wp12202.pdf), an IMF working paper published in 2012, models such a separation. The authors report support for claims of reduced debt and greater stability. These are results of their specified model, not observations from a country that implemented the modeled transition. The paper expressly states that it does not represent IMF policy.

Existing deposits and loans would also have to be dealt with in a transition. A household might hold a balance promised on demand while its bank owned a mortgage payable over decades. Moving the household’s money to full public backing would require a corresponding change in the financing of that mortgage asset. The reform’s appeal cannot substitute for an account of who supplies that funding, on what terms, and who bears any loss while the old contracts remain in place.

Moving transaction balances would leave other financing decisions to make. Businesses and households would still want credit. Someone would have to bear the risk of lending, decide its price and supply the funds. If lenders offered substitutes that people treated as money, the boundary of the protected system would become another regulatory problem. Public control over money issuance would also require rules for how the authority exercises that control.

A related sovereign-money initiative reached a Swiss referendum on 10 June 2018. The [official result](https://votations.admin.ch/details/2018-06-10) records its rejection, with 75.72 percent of valid votes cast against it. The question had moved from a reform proposal to a choice on a national ballot. The electorate declined that particular change.

Nor does “debt-free money” have a single accounting meaning. Advocates may mean money whose issue does not simultaneously put a private borrower under a loan obligation. That is different from saying the issuing institution records no liability, or that the money can command real resources without consequences. Moving the issuer does not abolish scarcity, failed investment or disagreement over whose purposes deserve finance.

A bank’s power to issue an accepted promise gives its lending decisions a reach beyond the two parties signing a contract. The obligations run from borrower to bank, from bank to depositor, and through the public arrangements that make the promises trustworthy. Understanding those relationships leaves a substantial choice about how credit should be allocated and whose resources should stand behind it.
