In May 1997, Gary Webb was asked whether his newspaper series about cocaine and the Nicaraguan Contras had reached too far. His answer went the other way. He thought it had covered too little. There were other cities and other people he still wanted to investigate.

By then, the argument over his work had become an argument over whether he could be trusted at all. Three major newspapers had challenged *Dark Alliance*, the series he wrote for the *San Jose Mercury News*. His own executive editor had acknowledged serious shortcomings. Webb was still talking about the next stories; the institutions around him were deciding what to do about the last ones.

Webb brought renewed attention to a real history of drug trafficking around a U.S.-backed war, and his articles made claims their evidence could not fully sustain. The subsequent inquiries enlarged the public record of institutional failure without confirming his account in full. Yet the verdict on the reporter became much easier to circulate than the findings about the people and agencies he investigated.

## The distance between a war and a neighborhood

The Contras were armed opponents of Nicaragua's Sandinista government. Their American backing, and the secret arrangements used to sustain it, had already produced the [[iran-contra]] scandal. Allegations of drug trafficking among Contra supporters also predated Webb. Associated Press reporters Brian Barger and Robert Parry covered them in 1985, and a Senate inquiry subsequently documented trafficking connections in the movement's support networks.

Webb's contribution was to bring that history into a particular American drug market. His August 1996 series followed Norwin Meneses and Oscar Danilo Blandón, Nicaraguans involved in cocaine trafficking, and Ricky Ross, a major Los Angeles dealer who bought cocaine from Blandón. It connected their business to Contra fundraising and to the damage crack cocaine had done in Black neighborhoods.

The difference in treatment was difficult to ignore. A supplier could become useful to federal investigators while the dealer he supplied faced an exceptionally severe punishment. People whose families had lived through the drug war had reason to ask how those decisions were made.

The opening of *Dark Alliance* went beyond reporting that these men had dealt cocaine and supported the Contras. It described millions in drug profits reaching the rebels, called their network the first pipeline between Colombian cartels and Black Los Angeles, and connected its cocaine to the national crack explosion. The first-day article can still be read in a [restored copy of the series](https://www.narconews.com/darkalliance/drugs/day1main-2.html).

The scale of a dealer’s business and his place in a market’s origins are different questions. Revenue from cocaine sales is not automatically money delivered to a rebel organization. And establishing that a supplier supported a CIA-backed force leaves a further question about what the agency knew of his business. The story needed to show how the money and knowledge moved between those people.

The opening gave the reader a continuous path from the guerrilla war to a neighborhood’s destruction. The evidence along that path was less continuous.

## What readers could inspect

The series appeared in print and on the web. Its surviving archive includes links to supporting material, allowing readers to move from an assertion in an article toward the records behind it. That mattered in 1996, when an investigation published by a regional newspaper could reach people well beyond the paper's circulation area.

Readers could check a transcript for the question that preceded an answer. A motion can be read for what a prosecutor actually asked a judge to exclude. An allegation no longer has to travel entirely on a newspaper's authority. The reader gains an opportunity to examine the reporting, although having the file available does not remove the work of interpreting it.

The archive itself had a precarious afterlife. In his introduction to the restored site, its editor Dan Feder describes obtaining a CD-ROM copy through Webb's family after Webb's death. The [restoration](https://www.narconews.com/darkalliance/index-2.html) preserves a contemporary publication while adding an introduction written by people who strongly supported him. Those are different layers of the source. The articles tell us what the newspaper published; the later introduction tells us how the custodians understood its history.

The internet also carried the conclusions beyond their supporting records. A headline, a graphic, or a sentence about the CIA could travel much faster than the distinctions inside a court transcript. Supporters and critics could both circulate an excerpt while asking other people to take its context on trust.

His later account of publication begins with a smaller scene. Visiting friends in Indiana, he opened a laptop in the early hours of August 18 and saw the series online. In the same memoir excerpt, he recalls his editor's congratulations and a $500 bonus. These are Webb's recollections, published in an [excerpt from his book](https://fair.org/extra/taking-a-dive-on-contra-crack/), rather than independent observations of the newsroom. They nevertheless show how he understood the beginning: the paper had proudly put its name behind the work.

## The objections had substance

On October 4, 1996, the *Washington Post* published Roberto Suro and Walter Pincus's examination under the headline *The CIA and Crack: Evidence Is Lacking of Alleged Plot*. Its [full text survives in a reproduction at Duke](https://people.duke.edu/~ldbaker/clippings/cia.html). The headline directed attention toward a plot; the article also acknowledged that Webb had brought a domestic dimension to the older Contra-drug story.

One objection concerned timing. Blandón's accounts of supporting the Contras and supplying Ross did not simply establish a single continuous business whose proceeds financed the war. Another concerned the origins of crack: an expanding market involving multiple suppliers and cities could not be explained just by following one important Los Angeles dealer. The *Post* cited named drug-policy researcher Jonathan Caulkins as well as other sources. Its criticism was more substantial than an unsupported government denial.

It also depended on people whose accounts were difficult to reconcile. Blandón and Ross differed about the scale and timing of the trade. Accepting one witness's statement when it supplied a dramatic connection, while omitting another part that weakened the chronology, could produce a coherent story from an incoherent record. The difficulty should have remained visible in the published account.

The *New York Times* and *Los Angeles Times* followed with their own challenges. The Justice Department's later review describes the *Los Angeles Times* effort as involving fourteen reporters and three editors. Its size helps explain why the response felt overwhelming to Webb. The disputes over testimony, money, and market origins still had to be answered individually.

A newspaper can fairly challenge a competitor and still give readers a misleading sense of what its challenge has settled. Showing that a particular ring did not originate a national epidemic would leave intact an inquiry into the ring's political support and government treatment. Establishing that the CIA had not directed those particular dealers would leave other Contra relationships to investigate. The reporting needed to follow those remaining questions with the same energy it brought to Webb's errors.

Instead, the reporter increasingly supplied the organizing subject. Was he reckless? Had his paper exaggerated? Would his editor stand behind him? These were legitimate newsroom questions, but they offered a manageable substitute for a dispersed history of covert support, trafficking allegations, and enforcement decisions. A dispute over a few articles had a recognizable protagonist and an approaching verdict. The larger history did not.

## Inside the paper

Executive editor Jerry Ceppos published his assessment on May 11, 1997. The original column is quoted and summarized in the [Justice Department inspector general's account](https://oig.justice.gov/sites/default/files/legacy/special/9712/ch01p1.htm). He identified four shortcomings: inadequate treatment of conflicting evidence, failure to identify the Contra-contribution figure as an estimate, oversimplification of the crack epidemic's origins, and imprecise language and graphics.

He retained the conclusion that Contra-associated traffickers had sold substantial amounts of cocaine in Los Angeles and sent some profits to the rebels. The acknowledgment was limited, but it was substantive. Conflicting testimony and an overstated financial estimate affect what a story establishes. Calling the dispute a matter of presentation alone would understate the editor's criticism.

There was also a disagreement about what the series had communicated. Ceppos believed it had implied that this ring was pivotal to the national epidemic. Webb understood the account more narrowly as a description of the ring's considerable damage and its spread through Los Angeles gangs. An editor's responsibility includes that gap between the case a reporter believes he has made and the case a reader reasonably takes away.

The correction belonged to the newspaper as well as to Webb. Editors had approved the prominence, language, and visual presentation. They had published a series whose authority came from the institution's name. If they subsequently found that evidence had been handled inadequately, their task included explaining their own decisions and showing readers which claims survived. A correction that leaves only the reporter's reputation in ruins does not, by itself, explain how the work reached publication.

Webb's recollection sharpens that question. In his book excerpt, he says an early date for the end of Blandón's Contra support had been removed from the series to save space. That recollection describes an editorial cut that later became part of the case against the reporter. Space in a newspaper is finite; the effect of removing an inconvenient date can be considerable.

There was an available next step: publish the conflicting evidence, explain the revised estimate, and continue investigating the remaining allegations. That would have required the institution to sustain a story after its first version had become embarrassing. A correction could have opened a second stage of reporting. In practice, the dispute increasingly closed off that possibility for Webb.

## Webb would not retreat

The [CounterSpin interview recorded days after Ceppos's column](https://fair.org/extra/gary-webb-defends-gary-webb/) gives Webb a voice outside the later arguments over his reputation. Interviewers Laura Flanders and Janine Jackson pressed him on scale, wording, and the editor's response. He continued to defend the importance of the market he had described and the claim that millions had reached the Contras. He referred to additional reporting and to follow-up stories he wanted published.

He also allowed that Ceppos might be acting honorably. That concession sits awkwardly with the later picture of a simple betrayal carried out by people who all knew the story was wholly correct. Webb could resent the consequences of the column while recognizing that his editor might believe it was necessary.

His insistence that the series had been too narrow is revealing. He understood the project as unfinished. Critics were examining the boundaries of what had appeared; Webb was thinking about the territory beyond it. Additional reporting might have strengthened parts of the case, changed others, or opened separate investigations. Its possible value could not repair an unsupported assertion already in print without showing readers the new evidence.

An editor asks for a defensible conclusion; the reporter sees records and witnesses that still lead elsewhere. Every demand to qualify can feel like a demand to stop. Every refusal to qualify can make an editor less willing to fund the next trip. Both people may describe themselves as protecting the investigation while moving toward its end.

Webb was reassigned to a suburban bureau and left the *Mercury News* in December 1997. He expanded his reporting into the 1998 book *Dark Alliance*. The book gave him space to defend the work and describe the conflict; publication did not restore his former position at the paper.

## What the agency welcomed

The CIA also had a role in the public argument. A later account should neither imagine it directing every critical article nor leave it watching silently from a distance.

In 2014, Ryan Devereaux reported on a declassified article from the agency's internal journal, *Studies in Intelligence*. Titled *Managing a Nightmare: CIA Public Affairs and the Drug Conspiracy Story*, it described the agency's handling of the controversy. Devereaux identified its author as Nicholas Dujmovic, who confirmed his authorship. The [report and its quoted passages](https://readersupportednews.org/news-section2/318-66/26093-documents-show-how-the-cia-and-us-press-destroyed-gary-webb) describe established relationships with journalists, circulation of favorable coverage, an effort to discourage one news affiliate from covering the allegations, and an unusual willingness to deny a particular person's agency affiliation to assist a journalist challenging the series.

The reported details describe active efforts to influence coverage. They do not establish that the agency commissioned all three newspapers' investigations or invented the weaknesses those investigations found.

The account also has an institutional point of view. Public-affairs officers assess whether they helped an organization survive damaging publicity. Their measure of success differs from a reporter's obligation to establish what happened. Coverage could be useful to the CIA because it corrected a genuine overstatement, because it diverted attention from a separate failure, or because it did both at once.

The history of [[operation-mockingbird]] makes that relationship worth examining. In this case, an official could supply a checkable fact while setting the terms of the conversation. The official may provide it in the hope that a narrower, answerable allegation will absorb attention that might otherwise fall on harder questions. Independence requires noticing what the source's answer leaves outside the frame, even when the answer itself can be verified.

## The findings that came afterward

The CIA inspector general released the first volume of his investigation on January 29, 1998. It examined the California subjects central to Webb's series. Its [summary](https://irp.fas.org/cia/product/cocaine/summary.html) said the investigation had found no information establishing a CIA relationship with Ross, Blandón, or Meneses. It reported that Blandón and Meneses claimed donations of between $3,000 and $40,000 each, which it had not substantiated. This was far from establishing the millions described in the series. Those conclusions directly challenged important parts of Webb's account. They were findings from the agency’s own investigation, framed around what its search and interviews could establish. An inspector general’s examination can produce important admissions and still leave outsiders dependent on an institution’s access to its records. That limitation accompanies the adverse conclusions as well as the disclosures damaging to the CIA.

The Justice Department's [separate inquiry](https://oig.justice.gov/sites/default/files/legacy/special/9712/exsump1.htm) examined investigations and prosecutions. Its findings included an instructive complication: Blandón had been prosecuted in the United States, served a prison term, and then obtained substantial benefits as a cooperating informant. His immigration treatment included an improperly obtained green card. The review did not attribute that episode to CIA protection.

The disparity was stark. As inspector general Michael Bromwich explained in the report’s epilogue, Blandón’s cooperation helped reduce his sentence to the twenty-eight months he had served by September 1994. He subsequently helped build the case that brought Ross a life sentence in 1996. The supplier had become the government’s witness against his customer. That arrangement gave the series a concrete subject even after the claim of intelligence protection came under challenge.

This left a real enforcement question. A person can receive extraordinary assistance because he is useful to investigators without being a covert intelligence operative. Cooperation can justify different treatment; it can also create incentives to overlook defects and rush arrangements through. The reasons need to be examined in the particular file. Assigning every benefit to the CIA would conceal other officials' decisions rather than explain them.

The second CIA volume, made public on October 8, 1998, examined the wider Contra program. It documented failures in the handling of trafficking allegations and relationships that continued despite them. Its [findings on reporting rules](https://irp.fas.org/cia/product/cocaine2/findings.html) also traced the omission of federal narcotics offenses from the list of crimes requiring reports about certain nonemployees under the 1982 CIA–Justice agreement. That omission was consequential without amounting to immunity from prosecution.

The [agency's release statement](https://irp.fas.org/cia/news/pr100898.html) nevertheless rejected an organizational conspiracy to assist trafficking. The reports therefore gave readers different findings at different levels: unsubstantiated claims about Webb's principal California subjects, alongside serious evidence about other relationships and agency practices. The wider record is developed in [[cia-drugs]].

Calling all this vindication gives Webb credit for conclusions his investigation had not established. Calling it a refutation of the entire subject gives the institutions an acquittal the documents do not supply. The useful result of the controversy was a larger body of evidence. Readers needed to be able to examine those findings without first choosing whether Webb belonged among heroes or frauds.

Nor was every prominent account hostile. In September 1998, *Esquire* published Charles Bowden's *The Pariah*. The [publicly available introduction](https://classic.esquire.com/article/1998/9/1/the-pariah) defended Webb explicitly. Its existence complicates the claim that nobody outside the alternative press reconsidered him. His damaged professional standing nevertheless endured as the subject he pursued yielded further disclosures.

## The colleague behind the reputation

Webb continued working after the *Mercury News*, including in California's legislature. In 2004 he joined the *Sacramento News & Review*. The story that no one ever hired him again erases the work he did and the people who worked beside him.

He was found dead at his Carmichael home on December 10, 2004, aged forty-nine. The coroner ruled suicide. After reports of two gunshot wounds prompted questions, the office issued a statement referring to the evidence at the scene and a note expressing his intention to die. His former wife, Sue Bell, accepted the ruling. These contemporary statements appear in a [reproduction of Sam Stanton's December 15 report for the *Sacramento Bee*](https://www.indybay.org/newsitems/2004/12/16/17100711.php), beneath the host's separate, skeptical commentary.

The unusual injury count became another claim about the CIA. The report does not establish homicide, and turning a family's loss into the final proof of a political theory repeats the habit that has made Webb so difficult to see clearly. His death cannot settle the merits of a newspaper series published eight years earlier. His professional losses deserve attention without treating suicide as their inevitable outcome.

His colleague Kel Munger offers a different memory in [*What killed Gary?*](https://www.newsreview.com/sacramento/content/what-killed-gary/246338/). Their cubicles were beside each other during his last months. She remembers legal pads, pens, hockey, voicemail, and conversations that did not revolve around the famous investigation. After his death, she helped gather the belongings from his desk: awards and magazine covers, but also a Detroit Red Wings pennant and photographs of his motorcycle and children.

Munger also remembers his response when a law-enforcement agency withheld documents from her. He proposed suing for them. Away from the arguments over his most famous work, he was a colleague with practical advice about obtaining a record.

He pursued an important subject, overstated parts of his case, defended himself stubbornly, and helped bring about inquiries that disclosed more than the public had known. His editors and competitors had obligations that went beyond finding his mistakes. The unfinished work was to follow the records after the newspaper argument had acquired its winner and loser.
