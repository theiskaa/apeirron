A forecast can decide whether a power station gets built. It can also decide how much a government borrows, which firms receive the contracts and how much future tax revenue must go toward repayment. The people making that forecast do not bear all the consequences of getting it wrong.

John Perkins made that separation of reward and responsibility the subject of *Confessions of an Economic Hit Man*, first published in 2004. His account accuses development consultants of deliberately encouraging loans that would enrich contractors and leave governments dependent on creditors. Its appeal comes partly from moving financial power into an ordinary workplace: a projection, a meeting, a recommendation signed by someone with professional standing.

The first difficulty is deciding which part of the accusation a record can answer. An infrastructure project can benefit contractors while failing the people expected to pay for it. A creditor can use a financial emergency to demand changes in policy. Neither proposition tells us, by itself, whether a particular consultant knowingly falsified a forecast or whether a subsequent act of violence belonged to the same operation. The accounts can show a creditor’s leverage. Instructions, correspondence and the treatment of adverse findings can help show how that leverage was intended to be used.

## What Perkins asks us to believe

In the publisher’s [promotional text of the book](https://www.bkconnection.com/static/Confessions_of_an_Economic_Hitman_8-18-11-Promotion.pdf), Perkins presents loans as a means of securing political loyalty and enriching engineering firms. He describes instruction by a woman he calls Claudine, deliberate optimism about Indonesia’s electricity demand and escalation to covert violence when persuasion fails. He also says that some conversations combine several exchanges.

Yet his preface rejects the idea that a small conspiracy explains everything. He puts considerable weight on shared beliefs about growth and the rewards for serving them. His account therefore moves between an allegation of intentional deception and an argument about a culture that makes damaging conduct seem worthwhile. A record of deliberate falsification would support the first allegation; evidence that consultants were rewarded for optimism might support the second without establishing the first.

In a [September 2026 essay](https://www.johnperkins.org/blog/the-hit-man-came-home), Perkins again says that loans returned money to American contractors and gave creditors leverage. He also recalls believing, during much of that work, that the infrastructure would end poverty. A person can recognize the incentives of a profession and still be a fallible witness to its concealed purposes. His retrospective explanations need to be compared with records made while decisions were being taken.

The recruitment scene asks the reader to trust a remembered conversation. A loan appraisal can be examined by others. Perkins’s accusation becomes more useful when it sends us to that file: what did the adviser recommend, what did the lender know and how were objections handled?

## A power project, followed through the accounts

Consider an invented example. A state electricity utility borrows $500 million to expand its system, with the government guaranteeing repayment. Part pays foreign suppliers for turbines and control equipment; part pays for construction, local wages, land and connections. Contractors can receive their money as work proceeds, while the borrower remains responsible for debt service for years.

Payments made abroad do not mean the country received nothing. A functioning plant is an asset. The question is whether its benefits justify its costs, who receives those benefits and whether the borrower can meet the payments when due. A plant can produce useful electricity and still be a poor financial bargain.

Suppose the appraisal expects annual receipts of $70 million after operating costs, against annual debt service of $50 million. If receipts instead reach only $35 million, someone must cover the $15 million difference. The utility might increase charges or seek revised terms; if the guarantee is called, the government may have to find money through taxes, other spending cuts or borrowing. These figures are illustrative, not a description of a particular loan.

The utility’s receipts are only part of the benefit, though they are crucial to its ability to pay. A factory may earn more because its power supply becomes reliable. A household may gain hours of lighting. Neither benefit automatically arrives in the electricity utility’s account. An appraisal of social benefits and a forecast of money available for repayment answer different questions. Calling a project productive does not settle whether its debt can be serviced.

Currency can open another gap. If the loan is in dollars but electricity bills are paid in local currency, depreciation raises the local cost of obtaining dollars. Revenue may increase and still fail to keep pace. The same physical plant can become harder to finance without producing less electricity.

The comparison also needs a plausible alternative to the project. Unreliable electricity has costs: factories lose production, households pay for substitutes and new businesses may never open. A country with inadequate power still needs a workable proposal. A smaller plant, repairs to an existing network or staged construction might offer benefits while exposing the public to less debt. The choice is among feasible ways of meeting demand, including the costs of delay.

A serious appraisal would examine those exposures before approval: lower demand, construction delays, cost increases, fuel availability, exchange-rate movements and the public capacity to operate the system. It would also identify who absorbs the losses under each scenario. A precise growth rate cannot compensate for leaving those risks outside the calculation.

## Why an optimistic forecast can win

A project can acquire an implausible forecast because someone lies. It can also acquire one because optimism serves nearly everyone at the approval table.

A contractor wants work. A minister may want an achievement completed within a political career. A utility may want a larger system. A consultant may know that the client prefers expansion. Each can favor assumptions that make the project easier to approve without receiving instructions from a common organizer.

Those incentives become more troublesome when the consultant advising on demand also expects later work from the development it recommends. The question is then how the advice was commissioned, what conflicts were disclosed and whether another evaluator could challenge it. Someone who benefits from the larger project has a reason to prefer the larger forecast; whether that preference became deception still depends on what they knew and did.

Error and misconduct can also coexist. A forecaster might honestly expect rapid industrial growth while suppressing an inconvenient sensitivity test. A minister might believe in the project while accepting inflated costs. A lender might recognize the risk but expect another institution to provide support if the borrower struggles. Responsibility cannot be assigned simply by sorting everyone into believers and conspirators.

Small-looking differences in annual growth become large differences in a long forecast. Take an imaginary system with demand indexed at 100. At four percent growth, demand after ten years would be about 148; at eight percent, about 216. A capacity plan based on the second assumption has to explain where that additional demand will come from. An industrial expansion that has funding, customers and a timetable is a different basis from an aspiration to attract industry once power becomes available.

A forecast can also become circular. The plant is justified because factories will arrive, while the factories are expected because the plant will be built. That may be a reasonable coordinated development plan, but its success then depends on more than the plant. The appraisal needs to expose those dependencies rather than count the expected factories as an independent source of confidence.

The appraisal and later correspondence should show whether objections reached decision-makers, whether alternatives were costed and whether new information changed the recommendation. A failed prediction becomes evidence of deception when records show that someone misrepresented what they knew, rather than merely what they hoped.

## The World Bank and the IMF do different work

The two institutions are often merged into a single creditor in accounts of economic hit men. Their roles overlap, but the difference affects which decisions to investigate.

The World Bank’s [institutional account](https://www.worldbank.org/en/about/history/the-world-bank-group-and-the-imf) distinguishes development finance, policy advice and technical assistance from the IMF’s work on monetary stability and balance-of-payments difficulties. Within the Bank, IBRD and IDA serve different groups of borrowers. Neither institution is interchangeable with a commercial bank, an engineering contractor or the U.S. Treasury.

Nor should a claim that loans inevitably return to American firms be mistaken for a universal procurement rule. The Bank’s [2011 guidelines for goods, works and non-consulting services](https://thedocs.worldbank.org/en/doc/616741467229981357-0290022016/original/ProcurementGuidelinesenglishJan2011.pdf) permitted firms from all countries to compete, subject to stated exceptions and eligibility requirements. Those published rules cannot tell us who actually won a particular contract. They do show why the contract and the applicable rules must be checked rather than assumed from the lender’s address. This later rulebook is not evidence about every procurement decision of the 1970s.

For an infrastructure loan, the important file may contain the demand study, procurement terms, contractor selection and safeguards. For emergency financing, the critical record may instead concern the financing gap, the timing of disbursements and the policy changes required to release them. Treating both as the same transaction conceals where the leverage enters.

## Conditions attached to the next payment

The IMF’s [description of conditionality](https://www.imf.org/en/about/factsheets/sheets/2023/imf-conditionality) makes the connection between money and policy explicit. Most financing is disbursed in installments. Prior actions can be required before approval or a review; numerical performance criteria and structural benchmarks help assess implementation. A missed performance criterion may receive a waiver, while missed benchmarks are considered in the overall review.

For a government waiting for the next payment, a review has immediate consequences. Its need for funding gives the creditor’s assessment weight over choices that would otherwise be decided domestically.

A signature does not eliminate that imbalance. Imagine a government facing a near-term shortage of foreign currency needed for imports and debt payments. It can formally refuse an offer, but refusal may bring immediate disruption. The relevant freedom is the range of alternatives it can actually sustain: another source of financing, a negotiated delay, debt restructuring, restrictions on payments or a larger immediate adjustment. Each can impose costs on different people.

Creditors have constraints of their own. Refusal to agree can leave a lender facing a default or an unresolved crisis, not an effortless victory. A borrower’s alternatives, the creditor’s exposure and the willingness of other institutions to contribute all affect the bargain. Financial dependence narrows choices without giving the creditor complete control over the outcome.

A lender can reasonably ask how additional money will change the borrower’s position. It may say that new money without changes would postpone the same crisis and put additional resources at risk. A borrowing government can have harmful policies, powerful beneficiaries of subsidies or an ineffective tax system. Asking for a plan is not inherently evidence of predation.

The dispute concerns the plan: whether its assumptions are credible, its demands are necessary and the burdens are distributed fairly. Cutting a broad subsidy and protecting households with low incomes can have different effects from simply withdrawing support. Raising revenue from an undertaxed profitable sector differs from collecting more from people already near subsistence. A fiscal target does not explain who will pay to meet it.

## Greece: what the institutional criticism establishes

The IMF’s involvement in Greece supplies a documented example of creditor priorities and uncertain forecasts. It is not a reconstruction of Perkins’s work in developing countries. Its value here is that the decision process can be examined without accepting his private recollections.

The Independent Evaluation Office’s [2016 report](https://ieo.imf.org/-/media/ieo/files/evaluations/completed/07-28-2016-the-imf-and-the-crises-in-greece-ireland-and-portugal/eac-full-report.pdf) found that the IMF approved exceptional financing in 2010 without prior debt restructuring, although Greek sovereign debt could not be deemed sustainable with high probability. The access framework was changed to allow the financing. The evaluation criticized the process, identified political pressures in working with European partners and found overly optimistic growth projections. It also explained how delay allowed private creditors to reduce their exposure before the 2012 restructuring.

The criticism concerns decisions the institution could explain and defend: lending, changing its access rule and postponing a reduction of existing claims. There were alternatives to argue over, with different consequences for the borrower and its creditors.

In a [2015 defense of the IMF’s role](https://www.imf.org/en/blogs/articles/2015/07/10/greece-past-critiques-and-the-path-forward), Olivier Blanchard acknowledged that delayed restructuring let some financing repay creditors and shift debt into official hands. He argued that contagion risk, a lack of protective arrangements and Greece’s large deficit constrained the alternatives. Slower adjustment would have required more financing or debt relief, with political consequences for creditor countries.

The disagreement turns partly on who remained exposed while officials sought to prevent a wider crisis. If new official money pays an existing private creditor, the creditor receives cash and the borrower acquires or retains an obligation to an official lender. The operation can prevent an immediate default while changing who remains exposed to later losses. “Rescue” describes an intended stabilization; it does not identify every beneficiary or settle whether losses should have been recognized sooner.

The documents also reveal disagreement within the institutions involved. That is evidence against treating them as a single actor with a single mind. The record lets a critic ask which argument prevailed, who could veto another approach and whose costs were treated as intolerable.

## Nepal: safeguards that existed on paper

A different failure appears in the Inspection Panel’s [1995 investigation of the proposed Arun III project](https://www.inspectionpanel.org/sites/default/files/ip/PanelCases/1-Investigation%20Report%20%28English%29.pdf) in Nepal. The findings describe shortcomings in resettlement supervision and enforcement of credit-agreement obligations, uncertainty for families affected by an access-road alignment and doubts about the capacity to implement environmental and social measures.

The investigators could compare what had happened with existing safeguards and credit obligations. A family facing uncertainty about its land could be harmed while the institutions continued preparing plans and revising procedures. That harm does not become less material because a contract contains an appropriate clause.

The [president’s review of 2 August 1995](https://documents1.worldbank.org/curated/en/830471468053351887/pdf/781180BR0P010400PUBLIC00A2I0request.pdf) announced that the Bank would not support the project. Its reasons included limited implementation capacity, pressure on other public spending and difficulty completing the financing plan. It also reported substantial local and political support for the project and described the mitigation actions then stipulated as satisfactory. That was an assessment of the revised measures, not a finding that all earlier obligations to affected families had been met.

Local support for development did not settle how it should proceed. People could want electricity and development while disputing the terms, risks or treatment of affected families. The project could have supporters and still exceed the capacity available to deliver it responsibly.

Withdrawal shows that the outcome was contestable. It does not prove that every affected person obtained redress or that safeguards worked well throughout. A review that changes a financing decision and a remedy for an already injured household are different achievements.

## Where the testimony still stands alone

Perkins connects economic persuasion to covert violence. The preface asserts that Ecuador’s Jaime Roldós and Panama’s Omar Torrijos were assassinated after economic pressure failed. That is an allegation about particular deaths and responsibility. The material examined here does not independently substantiate it; loan accounting cannot resolve it.

A failed development project cannot identify who caused a plane crash. Even evidence that an outside government disliked a leader would leave the operational question unresolved: who acted, with what authority and through what means? Repetition in interviews can establish that an allegation has remained consistent; it cannot supply an independent witness or a technical finding.

The same issue arises with Saudi Arabia. The [1979 GAO account of economic cooperation](https://www.gao.gov/assets/id-79-7.pdf) describes Saudi-funded American technical and advisory services. A [separate report](https://www.gao.gov/assets/emd-79-45.pdf) describes the Treasury investment facility and disputes over country-level disclosure. Those arrangements help explain how oil income could generate American contracts and investment. They do not establish Perkins’s personal role in designing them. The [[petrodollar]] essay follows those records in detail.


## Who carries a forecast’s risk

The strongest question raised by the economic-hit-man account concerns decisions made in the name of people who will carry the cost. A minister signs, a consultant forecasts and a contractor builds. Years later, households may face higher charges or reduced public services under commitments they had little opportunity to examine.

Different failures call for different changes. Honest forecasting needs adverse scenarios that can affect approval. Conflicts of interest need disclosure and review by people who do not depend on the project proceeding. Safeguards need implementation and remedies, not only language in a document. An unsustainable debt requires a decision about losses rather than indefinite reliance on a more favorable growth forecast.

None guarantees that a difficult project will succeed. They make particular decisions visible and give people a way to challenge them before the consequences become obligations. A forecast should be judged partly by whether its author showed who would bear the cost of being wrong—and whether that possibility could change the decision.
