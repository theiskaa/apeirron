On the afternoon of August 4, 1964, reports reaching Washington described American destroyers under torpedo attack in the Gulf of Tonkin. Then the commander on the scene sent a warning about the reports themselves. Many of the supposed contacts and torpedoes appeared doubtful. Radar effects and overexcited sonar reporting might account for them. He recommended a complete evaluation before further action.

By that night, President Lyndon Johnson had ordered air strikes against North Vietnam and was telling the public that American ships had suffered another deliberate attack. Three days later, Congress approved a resolution that would become a principal legal basis for expanding the war.

The second attack had not occurred. But that conclusion was not what a person following the stream of reports in Washington could simply read off a single message. Warnings, renewed assurances, disputed sightings, and misleading intelligence arrived through different channels. Officials chose how to weigh them, when to stop checking, and how confidently to describe the result.

A real battle had taken place on August 2. The administration joined it to the reports of August 4 as evidence of repeated aggression. The uncertainties surrounding that second encounter disappeared from the case presented to the public.

## The destroyer and the raids

The destroyer USS *Maddox* was carrying out a DESOTO patrol, an intelligence mission collecting North Vietnamese communications and electronic signals. Its work could reveal how coastal defenses operated: which stations transmitted, where radar coverage existed, and how naval units communicated. It was armed and operating close to a country against which the United States was already supporting covert action.

Those coastal operations, organized under OPLAN 34A, used South Vietnamese forces with American support and direction. They included raids against North Vietnamese installations. Shortly before the first naval clash, raiders had shelled offshore islands. The intelligence patrol and the raids had distinct commands and immediate tasks. That administrative separation did not determine how the destroyer's presence looked to North Vietnamese defenders.

On August 2, North Vietnamese torpedo boats approached the *Maddox*. American interception of their communications helped warn the ship. The destroyer maneuvered away and fired warning rounds as the boats closed, then continued firing. The boats pressed their attacks; aircraft from USS *Ticonderoga* joined the action. All three North Vietnamese boats were damaged, but all eventually reached shore. The *Maddox* sustained a single bullet hole.

The first shots do not by themselves explain how the battle began. The *Maddox* fired warning rounds before the boats discharged their weapons, while intercepts and the boats' approach indicated hostile intent. Merely saying that the destroyer fired first would omit what its crew knew. Merely saying that it repelled an unprovoked attack would omit the raids and the pressure being applied along the coast.

Senior American officials understood that the raids could have a bearing on the clash. In an [August 3 telephone conversation with Johnson](https://nsarchive2.gwu.edu/NSAEBB/NSAEBB132/tapes.htm), Defense Secretary Robert McNamara discussed explaining OPLAN 34A to congressional leaders. He described the recent shelling and the likelihood that the North Vietnamese had connected it with the destroyer's appearance. Johnson directed him toward limited congressional discussions.

The United States reinforced the patrol with USS *Turner Joy* and continued operating in the gulf. Further coastal raids took place. The ships' crews knew they were in a dangerous situation, and Washington had begun considering what response another attack should bring. A second incident would arrive inside those expectations.

## An alert becomes a battle

On August 4, a signals-intelligence unit at Phu Bai reported that North Vietnamese naval operations might be directed against the destroyers. The warning was not a verbatim order to attack them. It was an interpretation of intercepted traffic. That difference became important once the warning helped shape what the ships expected to see.

The destroyers subsequently reported unidentified radar contacts. Sonar reports suggested torpedoes. They maneuvered and fired into the night while carrier aircraft tried to locate the supposed attackers. The reporting described a prolonged engagement, yet the pieces did not fit comfortably together. Aircraft could see the American ships' wakes but could not locate the enemy boats indicated by radar. Reports of torpedoes multiplied without corresponding damage.

Captain John Herrick, the commander aboard the *Maddox*, began to question the account. His message recommended a pause and evaluation. Later messages restored some confidence in an initial attack while continuing to doubt much of the reported action. He suspected that sonar operators had been hearing the destroyer's own propeller noise.

Herrick's warnings were followed by renewed assurances. He did not simply issue a final verdict that nothing had happened and see it deleted. He questioned the reports, sought to reconstruct events, and continued to believe parts of the original account. Washington received reasons for caution as well as statements that could sustain belief in an attack.

Daniel Ellsberg, then working at the Pentagon, [later recalled following these cables](https://archive.pov.org/mostdangerousman/excerpt-ellsberg-memoir/2/?noamp=mobile). He eventually became convinced there had been no second attack. But he wrote that, during the afternoon itself, Herrick's renewed assurances helped persuade him that some attack probably had occurred, while leaving a serious possibility that none had. He was recalling a decision in which doubt persisted alongside a provisional belief in the reports.

The conditions at sea could produce mistakes without requiring the crews to invent an engagement. A warning of imminent attack made ambiguous contacts more alarming. Maneuvering generated noise that could be mistaken for another threat. Responding to a suspected torpedo could make the next uncertain observation seem part of the same battle. Later analysis had to test that story against evidence outside the ships' own reports.

## What Washington decided before it knew

Retaliatory planning began while reports were still arriving. Contemporary telephone records and memoranda show McNamara and other officials examining targets and different degrees of response, including naval bases and petroleum facilities. They were planning an attack on land, beyond the ships' immediate defense.

At the [National Security Council meeting on August 4](https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1964-68v01/d278), Secretary of State Dean Rusk argued for an immediate reaction. CIA Director John McCone described the North Vietnamese as responding defensively to attacks on their islands. The public account omitted that explanation.

USIA director Carl Rowan asked whether the provocation could be established as fact. McNamara answered that certainty would come in the morning and that highly classified information supported the incident. Rowan was asking the central question while officials were deciding to retaliate, and McNamara's answer postponed certainty until morning.

McNamara came to rely on several purported confirmations: reported lights and gunfire, Admiral U.S. Grant Sharp's assessment, and intercepted North Vietnamese communications interpreted as a report of an engagement. Signals intelligence seemed especially persuasive because it appeared to come from the other side. A North Vietnamese report of losses would look like independent corroboration of American observations.

The administration proceeded with Operation Pierce Arrow. Late that night in Washington, Johnson [announced the retaliation](https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1964-68v01/d286) and described repeated attacks as established events. He also emphasized that the United States sought no wider war.

A president can act before every detail of an encounter is known. The issue here was more basic than a missing detail about the number of boats. Officials were retaliating for an attack whose occurrence remained uncertain, then asking Congress to grant authority on the strength of a confident description of it. The account presented as a reason to grant authority concealed the reasons to hesitate.

Defending ships against an immediate threat and deciding to bomb installations ashore did not impose the same deadline. During a genuine attack, a captain could hardly wait for a review in Washington before maneuvering or returning fire. Once the reported engagement had ended, senior officials faced a separate choice about retaliation. Herrick's request for evaluation addressed that choice. Waiting would have risked looking hesitant, but the evidentiary problem concerned whether the event requiring a reprisal had happened at all.

## Reading the North Vietnamese messages again

Robert J. Hanyok, an NSA historian, revisited the signals intelligence in [“Skunks, Bogies, Silent Hounds, and the Flying Fish”](https://www.nsa.gov/portals/75/documents/news-features/declassified-documents/gulf-of-tonkin/articles/release-1/rel1_skunks_bogies.pdf). Written for the agency's classified historical journal in 2001 and released in 2005, it examined material missing from the earlier official account. Its conclusions were sharper than a finding that exhausted officials had misunderstood a confusing night.

The initial warning about preparations for naval operations had been overinterpreted. The intercepted message did not explicitly identify the American destroyers as the target. Other reporting described the North Vietnamese recovering and supplying boats damaged in the August 2 battle. A different American listening station had understood the traffic as replenishment rather than an impending attack.

Those recovery reports should have challenged the first interpretation. Some of the boats supposedly preparing to attack were being towed. Yet summaries supporting an attack did not give that contradictory reporting its proper place. The warning continued to function as if it described a specific plan against the destroyers.

The supposed after-action report had further problems. A field report referred to sacrificing two comrades; the version issued by NSA referred to two ships. Its claim about firing at American aircraft was also different from the stronger claim that aircraft had been shot down. The original decrypted Vietnamese text could not be found, limiting reconstruction of exactly how the wording changed.

Hanyok also concluded that two separate messages had been combined into one translation. One concerned an earlier engagement; the other described observations from August 4. Their merger made a statement that appeared to concern a single recent battle. The translation's release time in Washington helped it look like confirmation of the incident that officials were then trying to verify.

A message passes through several times: its composition, interception, translation, and release. A translation arriving after American ships reported a battle need not describe that battle. If its original timing and context disappear, the order in which Washington receives paperwork can be mistaken for the order of events at sea.

The result supplied two apparently separate points on McNamara's list of confirmations from the same problematic product. Agreement among pieces of evidence is much less persuasive when those pieces derive from one source and inherit its mistakes.

## The communications that were missing

Hanyok's reconstruction did more than discredit the messages cited as proof. It asked what communications an attack would normally have produced.

On August 2, American stations had intercepted tracking information, directions, and communications among North Vietnamese boats and commands. They had observed enough of the real engagement to know something about the arrangements required to conduct it. On August 4, the comparable pattern was absent. The North Vietnamese appeared uncertain about the destroyers' location, while their boats were involved in recovery work and coastal movements.

Silence alone cannot prove that nothing happened. Equipment can fail, messages can be missed, and an opponent can change procedure. But the absence had to be considered alongside the actual intercepts, the condition of the supposed attackers, the aircraft observations, and the ships' contradictory reports. Together, those records supported a different account from the one preserved in the attack summaries.

Hanyok found that nearly ninety percent of the available relevant signals intelligence had been excluded from the later summaries and official chronology. Fragments supporting an attack were promoted while conflicting material was omitted or answered with speculation. His judgment was that the presentation had been deliberately skewed.

He did not find a directive establishing a coordinated fabrication throughout the government. He expressly rejected the claim that the record demonstrated pressure on NSA personnel to deliver the administration's desired story. His reconstruction allowed for people who believed the attack had happened and then selected or reworked the evidence to defend that belief.

An analyst can believe a conclusion and still misrepresent its support. Once contrary information is knowingly withheld, the recipient is denied the means to make an independent judgment. The analyst's conviction has replaced the evidence the recipient was entitled to examine.

The successive products served different purposes. The first warning, the first mistaken interpretation, a later selective summary, and the October chronology were different products. A distortion embedded after the air strikes cannot have caused the earlier decision, although it could protect that decision against subsequent challenge. Hanyok's study traces failures during the crisis and an effort afterward to preserve the accepted account.

## An authorization already under discussion

The request to Congress had a history before August. The administration had been considering a broad resolution on Southeast Asia in June 1964. The relevant papers survive in the State Department's documentary record.

A [June 10 memorandum by McGeorge Bundy](https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1964-68v01/d211) discussed whether to seek general authority, when to approach Congress, and how to answer objections. The proposed replies stressed limited intentions while acknowledging that the resolution would permit force. They also emphasized securing a large majority.

A [June 11 draft, circulated with papers for a June 15 meeting](https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1964-68v01/d214), offered alternative formulations of the authority and its expiration. One termination option depended on a presidential judgment that regional peace was reasonably assured. Another proposed expiration when the next Congress convened in January 1965. Even before Tonkin, the duration of the delegation was a choice available to the drafters.

The advance work shows that broader authority was a policy objective, not an idea suddenly invented in response to gunfire. It does not establish that officials arranged false radar contacts to activate the draft. The papers also contemplated seeking congressional support without a dramatic new incident. The administration was deciding how to obtain flexibility amid a deteriorating situation, and the reported attacks supplied an immediate opportunity.

Congress [passed the resolution on August 7](https://www.archives.gov/milestone-documents/tonkin-gulf-resolution); Johnson signed it on August 10. The House vote was 416 to zero; the Senate vote was 88 to two, with Wayne Morse and Ernest Gruening opposed. Members were approving more than the raids already ordered.

Its first section authorized necessary measures against attacks and further aggression. Its second extended to the defense of Southeast Asian treaty partners and protocol states requesting assistance, with the president determining the necessary steps. That regional commitment could support action far beyond protecting two destroyers. The final text included no fixed calendar expiration. It provided for termination when the president judged peace and security reasonably assured, while also allowing Congress to terminate it earlier by concurrent resolution.

That wording left several different judgments with the president: whether further aggression needed to be prevented, what assistance a regional partner needed, and which measures were necessary. A member of Congress could think the administration's immediate intentions modest and still vote for language permitting much more. Assurances about intentions did not narrow the words enacted into law.

The June expiration alternatives make that difference visible. A January deadline would have required Congress to confront renewal after the election and the start of a new session. A termination tied to presidential judgment could instead allow the same authority to carry forward as circumstances changed. Congress retained the ability to revisit the measure, but the administration would no longer have to obtain a fresh affirmative vote simply because a date had arrived.

The unresolved attack was therefore used to justify a power larger than the immediate response. Congress was voting on future discretion while the public debate centered on whether to stand behind American ships that had supposedly been attacked again. Supporting the ships could be made to stand for supporting the broader delegation.

## What a later inquiry could still recover

Once the resolution had passed, the incident's evidentiary problems did not lose their political importance. They became harder to correct. A government that had retaliated and obtained congressional support had more invested in the account than it had while the first reports were arriving.

The Senate Foreign Relations Committee revisited Tonkin in 1968. The Pentagon Papers later made much more of the administration's earlier planning public. Their disclosure was important, but it was one stage in a history of challenge and disclosure, not the first occasion on which anyone questioned the story.

In January 1972, committee staffer Carl Marcy [wrote to NSA deputy director Louis Tordella](https://nsarchive2.gwu.edu/NSAEBB/NSAEBB132/relea00007.pdf) after inspecting intercept material. He asked Tordella to check a draft memorandum and requested a copy of the document central to the dispute. The surviving correspondence and revisions show arguments about timing, the form of the retained record, and what it could establish. Marcy's draft and the revisions do not supply an agreed confession by Tordella.

Hanyok's much later work added an examination of the larger body of signals reporting. The 2005 release made that reconstruction and many underlying documents public. It did not mean that all knowledge of the second attack's unreliability had remained secret until then. What had been withheld included the means to test how selective the supposedly authoritative intelligence account had been.

The resolution was [terminated by section 12 of legislation approved on January 12, 1971](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/STATUTE-84/pdf/STATUTE-84-Pg2053.pdf), with the effective date tied to the preceding Congress’s final adjournment. The war continued. The authorization had helped legitimate the escalation of 1965, but neither passing nor repealing it mechanically determined every later military decision. Presidents, legislators, and commanders continued making choices with consequences for people in Vietnam and elsewhere.

Tonkin's role in the war was enormous without making it the sole cause of every subsequent death or treating the full course of the war as irrevocably planned in August 1964. The resolution enlarged presidential freedom of action and weakened the demand for a fresh decision as the commitment grew. A limited reprisal and a regional war were different choices, joined by authority broad enough to make the transition easier.

The proposed provocations in [[operation-northwoods]] and later disputes over [[iraq-wmds]] invite comparison. The proposals in one case and intelligence assessments in the other have their own records. At Tonkin, the surviving records connect an intelligence patrol in the setting of covert raids, a real battle, a mistaken second encounter, intelligence fashioned to corroborate it, and a congressional delegation whose scope exceeded the event used to obtain it.

The most damaging omission from the public account was the uncertainty that officials themselves had to confront. Congress did not need the benefit of a historian's full archive to be told that the second attack remained doubtful. It needed that warning while it could still decide how much authority to give. By the time the fuller record was available, an argument about what happened on one night had become an argument about decisions already made in its name.
