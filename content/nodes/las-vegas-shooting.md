On October 1, 2017, Stephen Paddock fired on the Route 91 Harvest music festival from the Mandalay Bay hotel in Las Vegas. The police initially recorded fifty-eight deaths. Two survivors later died of complications from their injuries, bringing the toll to sixty. Hundreds were injured. Paddock died in the hotel. The police could identify the person who carried out the attack, but he left no explanation that investigators could verify. In January 2019 the FBI announced that a panel it had convened had found “no single or clear motivating factor.”

Those conclusions describe two different things. Identifying where the gunfire came from can be done by comparing accounts with evidence recovered at the scene. Explaining why a person chose to kill strangers often requires statements, relationships, or a history that links preparation to a purpose. The Las Vegas record is much stronger on the first question than on the second. It also shows how an early error about what happened to a security guard turned a question of timing into a suspicion about the investigation itself.

## A time in a log

Jesus Campos, a Mandalay Bay security officer, was wounded during the attack. At first the sheriff’s office described his injury as occurring while the concert was already under fire. An October 9 briefing put it several minutes earlier. That changed the question reporters asked. If hotel staff had known for several minutes that someone had been shot, what had they told police before the gunfire at the festival began?

By October 13 the sheriff was distinguishing the time recorded for Campos’s response to a hotel alarm from the time he was shot. The 9:59 entry referred to the alarm response; it did not establish when Campos was wounded. The police now placed the two shootings close together. The [contemporary CNN report](https://www.cnn.com/2017/10/13/us/las-vegas-shooting-investigation/index.html) preserves that correction. The department’s January 2018 preliminary report, drawing on hotel records and witness accounts, also placed Campos’s injury around the time the attack on the crowd began. The change did not prove that every call was made promptly. It removed a specific factual premise for the claim that hotel staff had known of the injury for six minutes before the festival came under fire.

## What fixed the source of the shots

Some people at the concert reported hearing shots from more than one direction. That is testimony about what they heard in the middle of the attack, not a measurement of where each sound began. Buildings can reflect sound; recordings made at different places can capture arrivals and echoes differently. Without separating those possibilities, a second sound that seems to come from another direction is not evidence of a second person firing.

The police case did not depend on recovering a single recording that sounded unambiguous. Evidence from the hotel room and from the concert grounds could be compared directly. Detectives also examined video, DNA evidence and personal devices for signs of an accomplice. The department’s August 2018 report found that Paddock acted alone, and the FBI panel separately reported that it found no evidence of a conspiracy. A suggestion that another person fired on the crowd would have to account for the physical evidence and connect that person to a firing location, not simply explain why a recording sounds unusual.

Surveillance footage has a narrower use than its reputation suggests. A camera can show who walked through a common area and when. It may not show what was inside someone’s luggage, what an employee knew, or what happened outside its field of view. The police reports make use of hotel records and video; they are not a public copy of the entire archive. Those are real limits on independent review. They are not evidence that a particular undisclosed recording shows a second attacker.

## A conclusion about motive, not an explanation

The FBI’s behavioral panel reviewed Paddock’s personal history, his relationships, and material the investigation had gathered. Its published findings, reported by NPR and CNN, did not identify an ideological cause or a clear grievance against the people at the festival. The panel suggested that a desire for notoriety might have been a factor. It could not establish that possibility as the explanation. Describing an attack as planned does not identify the aim the attacker had in mind beyond carrying it out.

The panel’s brief public findings leave limited room for readers to weigh all the material it considered. It is reasonable to ask what records are available to substantiate the assessment. But a record can be missing in more than one sense. If a recording was not released, there may be something an investigator saw that the public cannot inspect. If no reliable statement of motive was ever made, there may be nothing in the file that answers the question. The second problem is not resolved by assuming the first.

## What a settlement did not decide

The civil suits asked a question about the hotel’s responsibilities, not Paddock’s private reasons. In 2018 MGM Resorts went to federal court to seek a declaration that it was protected from liability under the SAFETY Act, a post-September 11 law meant to encourage the use of federally approved security measures by limiting certain liability. MGM argued that federal certification of the festival’s security contractor brought the claims within the law’s protections. The filings named victims, but did not ask them to pay MGM damages. The company’s argument about the law was not a ruling that it applied, and it was not a criminal finding about the motive for the shooting.

In 2020 a judge approved an $800 million settlement. That resolved claims without a trial deciding what a hotel employee should have noticed or how a different decision might have changed the outcome. There was a separate legal response to the equipment used in the attack: a federal rule classified bump stocks under the existing machinegun statute. In *Garland v. Cargill* in 2024, the Supreme Court held that the agency had exceeded its statutory authority. That decision turned on the statute Congress had written, not a finding that the attack was less severe or the policy unnecessary.

The question about Campos had a practical consequence: whether the hotel had time to act on a warning. It could be tested against the log and the other accounts of that night. The question of motive would require evidence of a different kind, and the investigators could not find enough of it. Knowing where that inquiry stops is not a reason to stop asking what the hotel or the police could have done.
