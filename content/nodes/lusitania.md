The *Lusitania* had enough lifeboat places for everyone aboard. That was one of the lessons shipping companies had taken from the *Titanic*. On May 7, 1915, it was not enough. After a German torpedo struck the liner off southern Ireland, she listed so sharply that boats became difficult to board and dangerous to lower. The ship continued moving. Within about eighteen minutes, she was gone.

Roughly twelve hundred people died, including more than a hundred Americans. Survivors and the submarine commander described a second explosion. The liner had been carrying ammunition as well as passengers, and British authorities had known that submarines were operating near her route. These facts made the sinking a dispute about responsibility almost as soon as it became a catastrophe.

There are several responsibilities to examine: Germany’s decision to attack without warning, Cunard’s carriage of passengers and war supplies together, the Admiralty’s precautions, and the political use of the deaths. Following those decisions takes us from the cargo hold and the bridge to the inquiries and diplomatic correspondence.

## Passengers and war supplies

The Cunard liner left New York for Liverpool on May 1. Britain and Germany had been at war since the previous August; the United States remained neutral. The Atlantic crossing was a civilian service within a war economy. People needed passage, Britain needed imports, and American firms were selling supplies to the belligerents.

The ship’s possible military use also preceded the war. A 1903 agreement gave the British government rights to hire or acquire the liner for naval service. A [1913 parliamentary answer](https://hansard.parliament.uk/Commons/1913-03-06/debates/24c8a87a-ef06-4537-bab2-b6d1197903f2/SteamshipCompanies%28ImperialSubsidies%29) publicly described annual payments for these rights over *Lusitania* and her sister, *Mauretania*. These arrangements helped give later German claims about an auxiliary cruiser an appearance of plausibility. A ship designed to permit conversion, however, was not necessarily serving as a warship on a particular voyage. The British inquiry and the later American proceedings found that *Lusitania* was unarmed.

The cargo included rifle cartridges, empty shrapnel shells, and fuses. These were military supplies, carried alongside the belongings and ordinary freight of a passenger crossing.

The cargo was discussed in the published proceedings, long before divers reached the wreck. The [1915 British report](https://www.titanicinquiry.org/Lusitania/Report/Repcargo.php) acknowledged thousands of cases of cartridges and said they appeared in the manifest. In 1918, Judge Julius Mayer’s [American opinion](https://www.rmslusitania.info/primary-docs/mayer-opinion/pages-7-11/) listed 4,200 cartridge cases, as well as the empty shells and fuses.

The explosive properties of that freight were disputed. Mayer accepted testimony that these items could not produce a mass explosion through fire or impact. The British report likewise rejected a cargo explosion. Those are findings made from the evidence available to the proceedings, rather than a modern examination of an intact wreck. Officials acknowledged military cargo while denying that it explained the destruction.

Carrying cartridges, carrying loaded artillery shells, and having guns ready to fire are different conditions. Much of the argument after the sinking moved among them as though each established the others. A cartridge contains propellant, but the presence of cartridges alone does not establish a catastrophic detonation of the hold. An empty shell is an unfinished military article. A liner equipped with gun mountings is not thereby an armed cruiser.

For Cunard and the British government, the civilian service and the military freight were entangled. Passengers were traveling on a vessel contributing to Britain’s supply system. Keeping that service running exposed them to a threat the German government had advertised. The economic and strategic reasons for continuing the crossings did not make their safety a settled matter.

## A warning in the newspaper

Germany had declared the waters around the British Isles a war zone in February. On May 1, the German embassy’s notice appeared in American newspapers near advertisements for Atlantic passage. It warned that British and allied vessels were liable to destruction and that travelers entered the zone at their own risk.

That notice was a real warning of danger. It was also an attempt to establish Germany’s terms for neutral travelers before an attack occurred. A passenger’s knowledge that the voyage was hazardous would later be offered as part of the defense of a submarine that gave the people aboard no chance to leave.

The American government rejected that defense. Its [note of May 13](https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1915Supp/d575) argued that announcing an unlawful act in advance could not excuse committing it. Washington insisted that neutral Americans retained rights when traveling on a belligerent merchant ship. It challenged the method of attack, including the failure to secure the safety of passengers and crew.

The problem was acute for submarines. A surface warship could stop a merchant vessel, inspect it, and make arrangements for the people aboard. A submarine had little room for a captured ship’s company and was vulnerable when surfaced. Britain’s use of neutral flags and instructions to merchant ships to resist submarines increased German concerns about approaching a vessel to search it.

Germany made those practical difficulties part of its case for changing the rules. Washington treated the protection of noncombatants as a limit on the weapon’s use. From that position, the fact that a submarine could not safely perform a particular interception did not entitle it to torpedo the ship with its passengers inside.

There was also a distinction between warning of a dangerous region and warning a particular ship before attacking. The newspaper notice supplied no evacuation time at the moment of danger. On May 7, the people aboard *Lusitania* had seconds between the sighting of a torpedo’s wake and the impact.

## The messages Turner received

Captain William Turner was not told that the sea ahead was safe. The surviving record includes both standing Admiralty advice and messages about recent submarine activity. The standing advice described precautions; the later messages reported sightings.

The general advice recommended giving headlands a wide berth, approaching important landfalls after dark where possible, and using irregular course changes to complicate a submarine’s aim. A submerged submarine was slow. To make a successful attack, its commander had to bring the boat into position and anticipate where the target would be. A fast liner following a steady course was easier to predict than one changing direction.

The [1918 ruling reproduces the warnings](https://www.rmslusitania.info/primary-docs/mayer-opinion/pages-17-23/). On the evening of May 6, *Lusitania* received notice of submarines off Ireland’s south coast. On May 7, an 11:25 a.m. message reported activity in the southern Irish Channel and a last reported position south of Coningbeg Light Vessel. It specifically directed that *Lusitania* receive the information. Another message, received at 12:40 p.m., reported submarines south of Cape Clear, proceeding west when sighted earlier that morning.

Turner had received locations as well as general advice. Those positions came from earlier sightings, leaving him to judge where the submarines might have gone since.

Turner had practical problems to solve. Fog reduced visibility on the morning of May 7. He reduced speed and later increased it to about eighteen knots when conditions improved. He also wanted to time the approach to Liverpool so that the ship could cross the bar at a suitable tide without waiting in exposed waters. Taking a bearing off the Old Head of Kinsale helped establish his position but required maintaining a course while the observations were made.

Turner had to work out his own position while judging a moving threat. Going faster, keeping farther offshore, or zigzagging might have changed the encounter. Each counterfactual alters the positions and timing of both vessels. Turner had to choose before knowing where an attack would come.

There was an earlier speed reduction for which Cunard was responsible. Since late 1914 the company had operated the liner with reduced boiler power to economize on coal and labor. Mayer’s account described an average service speed reduced from about twenty-four to twenty-one knots. That arrangement preceded the final crossing; it was different from Turner’s further adjustments for fog and arrival time.

The vessel Cunard sent to sea already had less power available than in her earlier service. Turner’s choices were made within that constraint.

## Eighteen minutes

U-20, commanded by Walther Schwieger, fired the torpedo. In the [diary passage reproduced by the US National Archives](https://unwritten-record.blogs.archives.gov/2016/05/03/aftermath-of-disaster-rms-lusitania-in-photographs/), Schwieger described a heavy detonation, a tall cloud of smoke, and what he believed was a further explosion. He suggested possible causes—boiler, coal, or powder—without knowing which applied.

He was watching through a periscope, unable to inspect the holds or machinery. His report supports the occurrence of a further violent event. It does not identify its material cause.

The immediate human problem was the ship’s list and the short time remaining. On the high, port side, boats swung inward. Getting them past the rail was difficult; lowering them could drag them against the riveted hull. On the starboard side, boats swung away from the deck, leaving a gap passengers had to cross. The ship’s continued forward movement created further danger as boats reached the sea.

Mayer’s ruling describes Turner ordering the engines astern in an effort to stop that movement. The machinery did not respond as required. An order to lower boats could therefore become hazardous before it could be completed. Some boats reached the water; others tipped or spilled their occupants. Collapsible boats cut free from the deck provided flotation for some people who survived the sinking itself.

An evacuation plan assumes time to bring people to stations, organize them, and carry out successive operations. The disaster compressed those tasks into minutes on a deck changing its angle. The nominal number of lifeboat places could not measure how many people could actually reach safety.

The inquiries heard conflicting accounts of what particular crew members had done. Some survivors believed that incompetence had cost lives. Mayer generally defended the crew, finding that they had worked courageously under extreme conditions. His judgment cannot give every individual action a complete history, but its account makes the physical obstacles clear. A boat could be available and still be almost impossible to launch safely.

## What the explosions can tell us

The second explosion is often treated as the discovery that overturns the contemporary explanation. It was already central to the contemporary arguments. The disagreement concerned what it meant.

The British inquiry concluded that two torpedoes had struck. Mayer also favored two, weighing conflicting witnesses and reasoning that another torpedo best explained the further explosion. Modern accounts generally identify a single torpedo. The inquiries preserved testimony that would later be used to challenge their own reconstruction.

In Mayer’s explanation, the exclusion of explosive cargo helped make a second torpedo seem necessary. Yet a damaged ship contains boilers, steam systems, fuel, bulkheads, and enclosed spaces. The range of possible secondary failures is wider than a choice between another torpedo and an ammunition detonation. Determining which occurred requires information about the location and sequence of the damage.

An ammunition explanation has its own physical sequence to establish: ignition or detonation of particular material, damage to the surrounding structure, and resulting flooding. The listed military cargo identifies things to investigate, while leaving their behavior during the attack unresolved.

Mayer’s analysis also considered the torpedo damage sufficient to sink the liner through flooding of boiler rooms and adjoining coal bunkers. These side bunkers counted among the compartments whose flooding affected her ability to remain afloat. Keeping two main rooms isolated would not save her if the same damage opened an additional adjoining space to the sea.

The arrangement also helps explain why flooding and the ability to use the boats belong in the same account. Water entering along one side changes the ship’s balance. Even before the hull disappears, the resulting angle can defeat equipment intended to work from an upright deck. A question about the loss of the vessel therefore becomes a question about which escape routes remained usable.

Mayer explicitly judged the damage from one torpedo sufficient, despite accepting a second hit elsewhere. That was his explanation from the testimony, rather than a settled modern forensic result.

The surviving testimony gives us a badly damaged ship, rapid flooding, loss of effective control, and a severe list. The exact contribution of a secondary explosion remains a more difficult technical question. It need not be solved before asking who ordered the torpedo fired or what precautions had been taken for the people aboard.

## The inquiry and its limits

Lord Mersey’s British investigation opened in June and issued its report in July 1915. Part of the evidence was heard in private. The published report said this included Admiralty advice about avoiding submarines and information furnished to Turner. Mersey declined to reproduce the detail on grounds of public interest.

He praised the Admiralty’s efforts. He also found that Turner had not followed all the advice given to him. But he expressly declined to attribute negligence or incompetence to the captain. The report’s [formal findings](https://www.titanicinquiry.org/Lusitania/Report/RepFindings.php) likewise answered no when asked whether blame attached to Turner or the owners.

Historian Diana Preston has described [Admiralty hostility toward Turner](https://booknotes.c-span.org/Watch/170043-1), including Fisher’s desire to see him found guilty. The report did not deliver the verdict Fisher wanted. The attempt to blame Turner and Mersey’s refusal to do so are both part of this history.

The American proceedings followed claims against Cunard by survivors and families. Mayer’s 1918 decision examined navigation, the cargo, drills, open portholes, and the handling of boats. He found no negligence warranting liability. The claims against the owner shaped what the proceeding had to decide. It did not examine every intelligence decision in London.

Both accounts were written during the war. Their condemnation of Germany was emphatic, and some of their physical conclusions remain contestable. The British proceedings also limited public access to important evidence.

Preston’s criticism goes further. In the same interview, she described finding an intercepted German message reporting that one torpedo had sunk the liner. She argued that British officials knew this and tried to preserve a multiple-torpedo explanation because it weakened Germany’s case about exploding ammunition. That is her account of evidence she examined, and it concerns the management of the explanation after the attack. It sits alongside her rejection of a deliberate British plan to have the ship sunk.

## Did Britain deliberately expose the liner?

Britain’s strategic interest in American involvement was real. Churchill’s February 12 letter to Walter Runciman is particularly uncomfortable. As reproduced and discussed by historian [Cameron Hazlehurst](https://winstonchurchill.hillsdale.edu/lusitania-sinking-1915/#comments), it urged attracting neutral shipping partly in the hope of involving the United States in conflict with Germany. The exchange concerned insurance terms for neutral trade under the threatened submarine campaign.

Churchill saw a possible political advantage in danger to neutral commerce. His letter concerned neutral shipping, while *Lusitania* was a British liner. It establishes a political calculation without identifying an operational decision to expose this particular ship.

The liner had no naval escort when attacked. Claims that HMS *Juno* was assigned to meet her and then deliberately withdrawn have become central to the allegation. The alleged rendezvous orders and the reconstruction of a decisive Admiralty meeting are themselves disputed; Richard Langworth’s account on the same site challenges the documentation offered by Colin Simpson. An unescorted crossing is established more securely than the claimed secret instruction that made it a sacrifice.

Preston, explaining her own archival research, judged the Admiralty complacent and preoccupied with the Dardanelles campaign, rather than engaged in a plan to have the liner sunk. That explanation leaves serious failures of attention and protection to examine. The warnings received by Turner also show some action by the authorities, however inadequate it proved.

The Royal Navy’s achievements elsewhere tell us little about whether these particular arrangements were adequate. Large organizations can possess good intelligence, allocate resources badly, communicate imperfectly, and overestimate a ship’s safety at the same time. Establishing a deliberate sacrifice would require evidence connecting those failures to an intention that the attack occur. The political benefits that followed cannot fill in that missing decision.

## The United States stayed out of the war

The diplomatic exchange after the sinking gives a clearer view of the immediate political result. Germany’s [May 28 reply](https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1915Supp/d615) alleged masked guns, Canadian troops, and an ammunition explosion. The American [June 9 note](https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1915Supp/d632) rejected the claims about the ship’s armed and military status. It also argued that contraband did not excuse the method used to kill the people aboard.

Secretary of State [William Jennings Bryan resigned](https://history.state.gov/departmenthistory/people/bryan-william-jennings) during this dispute. His objection concerned the risk that the administration’s approach would lead to war. He wanted greater restraint toward Germany and objections to British violations of neutral rights as well. Outrage had not produced an agreed course of action inside the administration.

American trade and finance increasingly favored the Allies. By the time of American entry, bankers had lent them more than $2 billion, according to the National Archives’ historical account. Creditors had money at stake in the Allied cause, and American suppliers had orders to fill. These relationships grew during the period of formal neutrality. They form part of the explanation for American alignment with Britain and France, alongside the submarine dispute and the later decision to enter the war.

The United States remained outside the war through the rest of 1915 and all of 1916. Germany made concessions over submarine warfare before reversing course in 1917. In his [April 2 war address](https://www.archives.gov/milestone-documents/address-to-congress-declaration-of-war-against-germany), Wilson made the renewed unrestricted campaign central to his case. Congress declared war on April 6. The intervening decisions included another change in German policy and an American judgment that armed neutrality could no longer work.

The memory of *Lusitania* supplied powerful imagery. A surviving [British replica of Karl Goetz’s medal](https://www.awm.gov.au/collection/C1178043) shows how the sinking was made into propaganda. Goetz’s original criticized Cunard and the traffic in war supplies. The British packaging presented the medal as evidence of German encouragement of atrocity. Its imagery and accompanying explanation directed the viewer toward a different object of blame.

The replica preserved the original’s erroneous date, May 5, two days before the actual sinking. Goetz had made the medal afterward, but the date could be presented as evidence of advance preparation. On the reverse, a skeleton sold passage at the ticket office. In the British box, a privately produced German satire became an apparent endorsement of mass killing. The object changed political use without needing a completely new image.

Officials and propagandists kept the sinking available as an argument for the Allied cause. Two years of further choices separate the torpedo from the declaration.

The people trying to get into lifeboats had no control over those later uses of their deaths. They depended on decisions made by the shipping company, their captain, British authorities, and a German commander. The failures of protection warrant scrutiny alongside the decision to attack a passenger ship without warning.
