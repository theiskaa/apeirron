The most carefully imagined witness in Operation Northwoods was someone who would not know they were a witness to a deception.

In one proposal, a civilian-looking aircraft would transmit a distress call over Cuba, report an attack by Cuban fighters, and then be destroyed. The aircraft would be an unmanned substitute. A separate plane carrying selected passengers under aliases would already have diverted to safety. Radio stations that heard the distress call could tell the United States what appeared to have happened. The American government would receive the account from outsiders rather than have to introduce it itself.

That was the intended advantage: an invented attack acquiring the credibility of an independently reported event. The [March 13, 1962, memorandum](https://nsarchive2.gwu.edu/news/20010430/northwoods.pdf) proposed several ways to create incidents that could justify American military intervention in Cuba. General Lyman Lemnitzer forwarded it to Defense Secretary Robert McNamara on behalf of the Joint Chiefs of Staff.

The proposals reached the defense secretary as recommendations for further planning. They did not become an implemented Northwoods campaign. The document shows senior military officials considering how to make the public and other governments believe that Cuba had supplied a reason for war. Some suggestions depended on simulated deaths. Others contemplated actual harm.

## A justification in search of an incident

Northwoods emerged from the effort to remove Fidel Castro's government after the failed Bay of Pigs invasion of April 1961. Kennedy had inherited that operation, approved it, and then faced its defeat. The administration continued trying to destabilize Castro through Operation Mongoose, a program involving intelligence collection, political pressure, covert activity, and planning for possible military intervention.

Brigadier General Edward Lansdale coordinated the Cuba project. A March 5 request asked the Defense Department's representative, General William Craig, for brief descriptions of pretexts that could justify intervention. The Joint Chiefs' papers answered that request. They were part of a policy process involving civilian officials, intelligence officers, and military planners, rather than an isolated document written outside government channels.

The assignment asked which incidents would make intervention justifiable. It began with the desired response and worked back toward the event that could justify it. The planners were being asked to supply reasons for a decision they wanted to enable.

The annex included a different route to a pretext: provoke a real Cuban reaction. Harassment and deception could persuade the Cubans that invasion was imminent. American forces would be positioned to move quickly from exercise to intervention if the response provided the justification. The apparent defensive act would then be an event Washington had helped bring about.

That option relied on the target making a consequential decision under a false impression. A Cuban commander seeing what looked like an impending attack might respond for reasons intelligible to his own side. The resulting clash could be presented abroad as Cuban aggression, with the deliberately created threat absent from the account. Fabricating an incident and manipulating the circumstances of a real incident were different ways to reach the proposed result.

They also carried different risks. A drone scenario required concealment of the substitution. Provoking armed forces introduced people who did not know the script and could use real weapons in unexpected ways. The annex's emphasis on readiness for immediate intervention allowed an escalation to be seized as an opportunity rather than treated as a failure of the deception.

The planners also wanted to influence international opinion. An American invasion could appear as a response to a dangerous Cuban government, with other countries and the United Nations receiving evidence of Cuban aggression. Northwoods sought to improve the apparent case for intervention as well as create anger at home.

There was a strategic condition attached. The paper assumed reasonable confidence that intervention would not directly bring the Soviet Union into the conflict. It noted the absence of a formal Soviet commitment to defend Cuba and urged action within the coming months. This was March 1962, before the missile confrontation that autumn. The recommendations were being made within a particular assessment of escalation risk, not as a timeless instruction to stage any atrocity that might be useful.

The planners weighed the risk of Soviet intervention while treating deception of the public as a method to develop.

## What the Chiefs endorsed

The familiar Northwoods release is a fifteen-page packet containing covering papers, recommendations, proposed memoranda, and an annex of suggested pretexts. The papers have different functions: they introduce an assignment, explain assumptions, recommend forwarding proposals, and describe possible actions.

Lemnitzer's covering memorandum called the submission preliminary and suitable for planning. It anticipated contributions from other agencies, a more developed schedule, and consideration of individual projects case by case. It also recommended that the Joint Chiefs receive responsibility for both overt and covert military aspects of the eventual plan.

The Chiefs were endorsing submission of these ideas for further development. They were not issuing a final operational command to units to begin bombing cities or sinking vessels. The packet even recommended withholding the paper from several categories of commanders and overseas officers.

Lemnitzer signed for the Joint Chiefs, and the memorandum described the recommendations as theirs. Its purpose was to offer American-created pretexts for American use.

Further planning would have required selecting projects, resolving practical problems, and securing additional decisions. The surviving paper identifies what the Chiefs were willing to place before those decision-makers.

## The base and the ship

Several suggestions centered on the American naval base at Guantanamo Bay. Friendly Cubans could impersonate hostile forces. Planners could arrange disturbances, fires, explosions, or staged captures so that the base appeared to have suffered coordinated Cuban aggression. One suggestion involved sinking a ship near the harbor and holding funerals for fictitious victims.

The sequence then moved from the apparent attack to an American response: operations to secure the base's water and power, attacks on positions said to threaten it, and larger military action. A limited protective operation would provide a route toward intervention. The allegations about what Cuba had done would be created as part of preparing that route.

The ship proposals included a reference to “Remember the Maine,” invoking the political force of the 1898 explosion in Havana harbor. The document considered blowing up an American ship at Guantanamo and blaming Cuba. It separately described destroying an unmanned vessel in Cuban waters, conducting a rescue for its nonexistent crew, and using published casualty lists to generate indignation.

The unmanned option anticipated how observers might interpret the explosion. Cuban aircraft or ships might approach simply to investigate a vessel. Their presence could then be used to make an attack by Cuba seem plausible. An observer would see Cuban forces near an explosion without seeing the American decision that caused it.

The reference to casualty lists sits within that unmanned-vessel scenario. It cannot fairly be quoted as if those words alone were an instruction to kill a particular American crew. The paper did contemplate a destructive deception, including a ship explosion and invented deaths. Its intended casualties were fictitious; the anger they were meant to produce was expected to be real.

The rescue operation would add another layer of corroboration. People could see American forces attempting to help, while newspapers could print names and authorities could speak about losses. Several apparently different facts would have one concealed author. Their agreement would be manufactured in advance.

## The refugees were not all imaginary

Other proposals crossed directly from simulated evidence to potential victims. The memorandum suggested a campaign attributed to Communist Cuba in Miami, elsewhere in Florida, and Washington. Cuban refugees seeking safety in the United States were among the people to be frightened and harmed.

The proposal to sink a boatload of Cubans traveling toward Florida included the phrase “real or simulated.” Here the writers left open an actual sinking as well as a simulation. The paper also contemplated attacks on refugees' lives, including wounds intended to receive publicity, alongside bomb explosions and prepared documents implicating Cuba.

The people singled out had fled the government that planners wanted to blame. An injury to them would be doubly useful in the proposed account: proof of Castro's reach and a reason the United States should act. Their vulnerability was being considered as a political resource.

Some measures could produce genuine witnesses to genuine fear or injury while preserving a false account of responsibility. A wounded refugee need not knowingly participate in the deception. Nor would a journalist need instructions to recognize an apparent attack on people who had sought refuge. The planners were considering how to supply an event from which others could draw the intended conclusion.

The packet did not resolve every detail of those suggestions. It did not name the refugees, specify which locations would be selected, or record approval for carrying out the real sinking. Further planners would have had to choose between suggestions that already included actual violence.

The proposed campaign also extended beyond the United States. Actions in another Caribbean country could be made to look Cuban-backed; aircraft, weapons, messages, or incendiaries could help create the appearance. The geographical spread served the desired picture of Cuba as an international threat rather than a country in a bilateral dispute with Washington.

## Two aircraft, two different deceptions

The civil-airliner proposal developed the mechanics of false corroboration in particular detail. It began with an aircraft belonging to a CIA-associated company and a matching plane prepared at Eglin Air Force Base. Selected passengers would board under prepared aliases. The planes would take off on schedules permitting a rendezvous south of Florida.

The passenger-carrying aircraft would then descend and divert to an auxiliary field. The unmanned aircraft would continue the filed route over Cuba, transmit a distress message claiming an attack by Cuban fighters, and be destroyed by radio signal. Outside monitoring stations would receive the message and relay the apparent event.

This was not a proposal to put unsuspecting college students on an airliner and kill them. A student holiday was suggested as a plausible public identity for a charter. The operational description used selected passengers and aliases, with their evacuation arranged. It did not say that passengers would disappear into indefinite custody or that their families would be told they had died.

The substitution was intended to make the destroyed object match the public identity of a passenger aircraft. The distress call would provide an accusation. External reception would make that accusation look independently discovered. Each part answered a credibility problem that might otherwise arise if the United States simply announced that Cuba had shot down a plane.

Another proposal concerned an American military aircraft. A pilot flying at the back of a formation would report an attack by Cuban fighters, then fly low to a secure base. The plane would receive a different identifying number; the pilot, who had used an alias, would resume his real identity. Aircraft parts and a parachute would be placed offshore for searchers to find.

The plan depended partly on the other pilots not knowing the whole arrangement. They could truthfully report what they had heard and what they believed had happened. Search crews could recover genuine aircraft components. Neither observation would establish the claimed Cuban attack, but together they could seem to confirm it.

The military-pilot scenario explicitly provided for the pilot's return. There is no need to add his murder or a fatal ejection to make the document alarming. Its distinctive feature is that people performing normal duties could become reliable witnesses to selected parts of an invented event.

The aircraft suggestions were not identical, either. The airliner scenario used an unmanned replacement and external radio reception; the military version hid a surviving pilot and introduced physical wreckage. Both sought an apparent loss, but through different people, records, and observations. The writers were thinking about how a story would survive verification.

## Kennedy's decision and its limits

The proposals did not become an implemented Northwoods campaign. The surviving record is less explicit about Kennedy's personal response to this particular packet.

A contemporary record of the [March 16 discussion of Mongoose](https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1961-63v10/d314) is important. The guidelines assumed that final success against Castro would require decisive American intervention. They nevertheless restricted the immediate phase to intelligence and other activity short of provoking a situation that would demand such intervention. Further review would precede the next phase.

U. Alexis Johnson's covering note recorded Kennedy's skepticism that circumstances would arise in the foreseeable future to justify overt American military action. It explicitly stated that no decision approving the use of American forces was expressed or implied. Contingency planning would continue. The apparent certainty of the written assumption and the caution in the presidential note belong together: the same file records both an expectation about eventual military requirements and a refusal to authorize their use then.

The note records Kennedy’s position on intervention; it does not describe him reading and vetoing each suggested incident. The written planning assumption cannot be substituted for a presidential decision to invade.

The broader effort against Castro continued. Mongoose involved civilian authorization and supervision, with the attorney general participating in the oversight group. Rejecting overt intervention at that point did not mean abandoning covert pressure or bringing every dispute over Cuba policy to a close. Civilian opposition to immediate invasion coexisted with civilian direction of covert pressure.

Lemnitzer's subsequent career also resists a simple punishment narrative. Maxwell Taylor succeeded him as chairman later in 1962, but Lemnitzer went on to command American forces in Europe and serve as NATO's supreme allied commander. In a [July 1962 press conference](https://www.historycentral.com/JFK/Press/7.23.62.html), Kennedy publicly defended his choice of Lemnitzer for European command and described him as well equipped for it.

Public praise does not disclose every private disagreement. It does establish that the man who forwarded Northwoods was entrusted with another major command. The available records do not support treating that appointment as proof that he was removed from power for this document, or that his European service itself proves he later executed its proposals there.

## How the paper became public

Northwoods reached a broad audience decades after the period in which it could have affected policy. Its release grew out of the search for records surrounding Kennedy's assassination, including the wider history of Cuba operations.

The Assassination Records Review Board's [November 18, 1997, announcement and selected-document list](https://www.archives.gov/files/research/jfk/releases/2025/0318/104-10333-10014.pdf) identified the Northwoods papers among military records being opened. The selection included documents from several dates, not just the March 13 memorandum. A larger [National Archives record case](https://www.archives.gov/files/research/jfk/releases/2018/202-10002-10104.pdf) preserves Northwoods material within the files of Cuba policy and intervention planning.

A paper's inclusion in the Kennedy assassination collection does not establish a connection to the murder. The board was seeking historical context as well as direct evidence. Its release expanded what people could know about policy arguments and covert planning during Kennedy's presidency.

James Bamford brought the proposals to wider notice in his 2001 book *Body of Secrets*. The National Security Archive [posted the memorandum that April](https://nsarchive2.gwu.edu/news/20010430/), and its later documentary account identifies Bamford as the source of its copy. The declassification and the later publication helped the document reach different audiences four years apart.

The surviving paperwork gives the proposals a definite scope. The pages that specify safe diversion of a plane sit in the same packet as the suggestion of a real refugee-boat sinking. The intended survival of some participants and contemplated harm to others were parts of the same submission.

## What Northwoods changes

Northwoods defeats an argument based on institutional reputation: senior American military officials did formally propose manufacturing incidents to help justify a war. A person cannot dismiss that fact by assuming such proposals would never reach a defense secretary.

It does not shift the burden of establishing who caused a different event. An unexecuted proposal from 1962 supplies no missing participant, communication, or physical evidence for a later attack. The [[gulf-of-tonkin]] record, for example, involves a real first engagement, mistaken reports of a second, and subsequent intelligence distortion. Calling it Northwoods in action erases the decisions and evidence particular to Tonkin. Claims about [[nine-eleven]] require the same attention to their own records.

Nor does the lack of implementation make this merely an imaginative exercise with no institutional significance. Officials had been asked for usable pretexts. The Joint Chiefs supplied recommendations and sought a role in developing the resulting operations. Formal approval at one stage had carried the proposal toward decisions that could have made it real. Further authority was still required.

The consent the plan sought would have depended on a false description of danger. People might reasonably support rescuing a crew, protecting refugees, or answering an attack on a civilian aircraft. Northwoods proposed creating those reasons for support while hiding their source. It would let the government invoke judgments that citizens had been denied an honest chance to make.

The most revealing witnesses in the aircraft proposals were therefore the ones expected to remain sincere. Pilots, search crews, and radio operators could report what they had actually encountered. Their honesty would help conceal the planning behind it. The document's authors understood that an official assertion was more persuasive when other people appeared to have arrived at it independently. They proposed arranging the evidence those people would see.
