The first attempt to remove Mohammad Mossadegh failed. On the night of August 15–16, 1953, the attempt to deliver the Shah’s dismissal decree to the Iranian prime minister ended with the officer carrying it under arrest. Other parts of the military plan broke down. The Shah fled the country. Within days, officials in Washington were considering how to end the operation without further exposing the United States.

On August 19, Mossadegh’s government fell. Demonstrators and military units took control of Tehran’s streets, communications, and government offices. General Fazlollah Zahedi, the replacement supported by American and British intelligence, emerged to claim power. The CIA station that had argued against abandoning the effort received congratulations from headquarters.

Operation Ajax is unusually well documented for a covert overthrow. The records establish American and British planning, financing, propaganda, and work with Iranian participants. They also show disagreement, lost control, and surprise at the outcome. The intervention worked through Iranian officers, politicians, and organizers whose interests sometimes aligned with the foreign plan and sometimes pulled it apart.

The oil dispute brought Britain into that crisis. Fear of communist expansion helped bring the Eisenhower administration into the operation. The resulting government gave Western companies renewed access to Iranian oil and gave the United States a much larger role in Iran. It also narrowed the space in which Iranians could challenge their rulers.

## What nationalization was meant to change

In 1951, Iran’s parliament voted to nationalize the oil industry. Mossadegh, a leading advocate of that decision, became prime minister with parliamentary backing and appointment by Shah Mohammad Reza Pahlavi. His authority came through a constitutional system in which parliament, ministers, and the monarchy competed over the right to govern.

The Anglo-Iranian Oil Company operated a vast industry, including the refinery at Abadan, while the British government held a controlling interest in the company. For Britain, the dispute concerned state revenue, commercial power, and a strategic source of fuel. For Iranian nationalists, foreign control of the industry made political independence incomplete.

Mossadegh explained that argument [at the United Nations in October 1951](https://www.mohammadmossadegh.com/news/united-nations/october-15-1951.pdf). He described a country with valuable oil resources whose people remained poor and whose industry still depended on foreign expertise. He accused the company of denying Iran the opportunity to check its accounts. More income mattered, but so did the ability to decide how the industry would operate and how its earnings would be used.

A better percentage alone could not settle the quarrel. A state might receive more money while a foreign company retained the decisions that made the money possible: production, refining, access to buyers, and the accounting on which payments depended. Nationalization sought ownership and authority as well as revenue.

Compensation and future sales remained negotiable. Mossadegh told the Security Council that Iran would reopen talks on both. The disagreement was over what compensation would cover, who would determine it, and whether a settlement would restore foreign control under another name. Britain defended the company’s contractual rights; Iran defended its right to change the arrangement.

The industry’s disruption put pressure on Mossadegh. Iran could take possession of installations more easily than it could replace the international system that moved and sold their output. The major oil companies had other supplies, resisted handling disputed Iranian oil, and could make a new buyer’s access to markets difficult. The loss of large-scale oil sales deprived the government of revenue while increasing the political cost of waiting.

Mossadegh acknowledged that pressure. In the same UN address in which he defended nationalization, he warned that continued delay in production would worsen Iran’s economic position and paralyze its finances and administration. His refusal to surrender control was not a belief that the interruption of sales was harmless.

The United States initially tried to mediate. By 1953, the administration’s refusal to supply a financial escape from the dispute became part of the pressure. Eisenhower’s June message to Mossadegh said substantial additional assistance was unavailable while an oil settlement remained possible. The CIA’s later history described public American statements about aid as contributions to the campaign against him.

## The coalition was coming apart

Mossadegh’s position had weakened inside Iran. The coalition that brought him to power contained people who agreed on oil and disagreed on much else: nationalists, politicians with their own followings, religious figures, and supporters with different views of the monarchy and social change. Former allies became opponents. Ayatollah Abol-Qasem Kashani was among the prominent figures who broke with him.

The Shah had his own reason to resist a prime minister trying to limit the court and control the armed forces. Officers feared the loss of position as the government changed commands. Conservative interests opposed Mossadegh for reasons that did not have to be invented in London or Washington. Foreign intelligence could recruit within those disputes.

Mossadegh also took measures that strained his parliamentary claim. He had obtained authority to govern by decree. In August 1953, he used a referendum to seek dissolution of the Majlis, the elected lower house, after withdrawing supporters had helped deprive it of a quorum. The voting procedure made a voter’s choice visible and exposed opponents to intimidation. A contemporary American intelligence draft criticized both the method and the possibility of a fraudulent count.

Mossadegh’s use of these powers weakened the parliamentary constraints on his own government and supplied his enemies with arguments against it. The foreign operation entered a constitutional struggle already deteriorating into coercion.

Communism was another pressure. The Tudeh Party had disciplined organizers and a visible presence in demonstrations. Its strength worried Americans, Iranian royalists, and religious opponents of the left. Mossadegh could benefit from its support against other enemies while still regarding it as a threat to his own position.

The intelligence picture was more cautious than the claim that Iran stood immediately before a communist takeover. An [August 12 draft estimate](https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1951-54Iran/d259) expected Mossadegh probably to remain in power through the end of 1953. It judged Tudeh unlikely to seize control on its own initiative within that period and expected Mossadegh to resist it. The draft nevertheless warned of longer-term growth and an opportunity if a violent struggle or succession crisis broke the existing government apart.

Washington was choosing to remove a nationalist government because its continued difficulties might eventually benefit communists. That was a forecast about political deterioration. The August draft anticipated a danger that might grow or find an opening in a crisis, rather than a takeover already at hand.

## Turning opposition into an operation

The joint operation was known to the CIA as TPAJAX. Donald Wilber, an American planner, worked with the British intelligence officer Norman Darbyshire on the plan. Britain contributed existing relationships, including the Rashidian network; the CIA supplied money, personnel, communications, and access to American authority. Kermit Roosevelt took charge of the operation’s final phases in Tehran.

The intention was explicit. Mossadegh would be removed, the Shah’s position restored, and Zahedi brought in as prime minister. The successor was expected to suppress the communist movement and reach an oil settlement. These aims appear together in the CIA’s internal history. A government willing to settle the oil dispute was supposed to restore economic stability, which in turn would help contain communism.

Approval developed over months. An April authorization covered financial support under unusually restricted procedures. Roosevelt asked to dispense with normal written receipts from recipients, citing secrecy. The operational plan was developed further in June, with final authorization in July. A March 1954 CIA summary and a chronology cited by State Department historians both record Eisenhower’s approval on July 11; the historians found no surviving written authorization from the President himself.

The Shah was essential to the preferred plan but reluctant to commit. Royal decrees would dismiss Mossadegh and appoint Zahedi, giving officers a reason to act in the monarch’s name. The plan prescribed pressure through people the Shah knew, including his twin sister Princess Ashraf and General H. Norman Schwarzkopf, who had previously served in Iran. Roosevelt also met him. A monarch whose name was meant to unite the operation first had to be persuaded that the two foreign powers would stand behind it.

The written plan anticipated using the Shah even if he failed to cooperate willingly. It also allowed for modifications by Zahedi and his officers. Foreign planners could offer resources and pressure; Iranian participants controlled access to the forces needed to carry the plan out.

The planners wanted to capture the institutions through which orders became effective: army headquarters, military radio, Radio Tehran, police and gendarmerie headquarters, and postal and telephone facilities. Arrests would remove ministers, loyal officers, editors, and communist organizers. The purpose was to make resistance difficult before opponents could understand what had happened and assemble an answer.

Taking a headquarters required more than occupying its building. Officers elsewhere needed to receive orders they would recognize as authoritative. In the first attempt, a commander backed away when he found loyal troops waiting at the chief of staff’s office; without that position, the plotters could not use the command radio network. The problem reappeared in reverse after the successful overthrow: once the new chief of staff took office, the embassy reported, surrounding units began taking his orders. A new command could turn officers’ obedience to the military hierarchy into acceptance of the new government.

## Making the overthrow look necessary

The propaganda plan targeted Mossadegh’s standing among different audiences. He would be portrayed as favoring Tudeh and the Soviet Union, threatening Islam, weakening the army, and deliberately ruining the economy. Articles and leaflets would turn these claims into apparently separate expressions of alarm. Forged material would supply supposed evidence of a secret agreement with communists.

Some accusations took advantage of real disputes. Others fabricated the relationship they claimed to expose. A reader already anxious about Tudeh might be persuaded by a document that appeared to confirm the prime minister had secretly promised it support. The forgery could make an uncertain political relationship look like a settled conspiracy.

The campaign also manufactured evidence of communist hostility to religion. Wilber’s history reports threatening messages and telephone calls made in Tudeh’s name, along with one of several planned sham bombings of religious leaders’ homes. The intended audience was the religious leadership and its followers. Fear generated by an action attributed to communists would become another reason to turn against the government.

The operation could then use the fear it had helped create as an argument that Mossadegh could not protect religious life. The plan proposed more staged attacks than the later history reports carrying out.

Wilber also described articles planted in major American publications and then reproduced in Iran. A foreign report could return with the apparent authority of independent observation. The operation had an international publicity component, although the surviving description does not identify every unfavorable Western article as an intelligence product.

The London draft estimated $285,000 to implement its plan, divided between the two services. That was a prospective budget. A [March 1954 CIA summary](https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1951-54Iran/d363) reported a grand total of $5.33 million, including money supplied afterward for the new government’s urgent expenses; its individual component amounts remain redacted in the published document. Wilber’s history separately identifies an emergency grant of $5 million. These records distinguish the cost of organizing the overthrow from the money needed to keep its beneficiaries in office. The payroll of the new government was an immediate obligation of the operation’s success.

## The decree that failed

The first attempt depended on coordinated military action on the night of August 15–16. Colonel Nematollah Nassiri, commander of the Imperial Guard, went to Mossadegh with the royal dismissal decree. Forces were supposed to neutralize the government’s command structure and allow Zahedi to take over.

Mossadegh’s side had been warned. His chief of staff, General Taqi Riahi, had time to prepare loyal commanders. Nassiri encountered superior forces and was arrested. Other intended actions did not happen, and the plotters failed to gain the military communications they needed. A CIA station report described Iranian commanders cutting the Americans out of final military preparations, followed by confusion over who was acting and whether to hide or continue.

The Shah fled to Baghdad and then Rome. Zahedi remained in hiding. The arrest of Nassiri left the government able to present the episode as a defeated coup, while the station urged that the royal decrees be publicized as proof that Mossadegh was defying lawful dismissal.

The royal decrees offered a constitutional argument. Article 46 of the supplementary fundamental laws assigned appointment and dismissal of ministers to royal decree. Other provisions made ministers responsible to parliament and required countersignature for royal acts. The practical relationship between royal prerogative and parliamentary government was contested, especially after Mossadegh’s referendum against the Majlis.

A dismissal decree could therefore persuade officers and civilians who accepted the Shah’s authority. That did not make the associated covert military operation an ordinary transfer of office. Foreign planners had arranged pressure on the monarch, the removal of the government, and the seizure of institutions before the decree became a public justification. The constitutional dispute helped the operation recruit support; it did not account for the operation’s existence.

## Three days in which the answer changed

Washington now contemplated a retreat. An August 18 [CIA headquarters cable](https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1951-54Iran/d278) described the State Department’s tentative judgment that the operation had failed and that further action might compromise future relations with Mossadegh. The British also declined to continue without fresh grounds for confidence. In the absence of strong contrary recommendations from Tehran, operations were to stop.

The station argued the other way. Its messages stressed the army’s loyalty to the Shah, the continued usefulness of the decrees, and the danger of abandoning the monarch. On August 19 it complained about the lack of support and guidance. After the failure, the station still wanted a reversal that Washington doubted it could achieve.

The political situation outside the station was also changing. Demonstrators attacked royal symbols after the Shah’s departure. Tudeh’s visibility and demands alarmed opponents. Mossadegh ordered demonstrations stopped, and security forces confronted Tudeh supporters on August 18. This weakened the possibility of a common street response when royalists mobilized the next day.

On August 19, pro-Shah demonstrators gathered in the bazaar area and moved through Tehran. Some security personnel joined them; others declined to use force against them. Crowds attacked newspaper offices and seized communications facilities. The radio station was particularly important: control of it allowed the victors to announce that power had changed hands while that change was still being fought over.

Military units and tanks gave the demonstrators the means to defeat resistance and occupy the government’s centers of power. Mossadegh’s residence was attacked and overrun. Zahedi appeared at Radio Tehran to assert his authority. The station urgently requested $5 million to help the new government meet its payroll.

The victors offered different accounts of how the movement had grown. The embassy described a planned royalist demonstration that grew far beyond its beginnings, while admitting its account was preliminary. A station cable called the movement a popular uprising and also said work with the military had paid off. Wilber’s subsequent history credited political-action assets with contributing to its start.

These accounts show how organization and an expanding crowd could operate together. Money and contacts could put people in motion; royal loyalty, fear of communism, anger at the government, and the conduct of security forces could draw others in. Once troops and communications shifted, people had to decide which authority was likely to survive. Foreign intervention was consequential without determining every person’s decision.

## What the new government secured

Headquarters congratulated Roosevelt and the Tehran personnel on the successful overthrow. The argument over ending the operation gave way to claims of achievement. Zahedi took office, and the Shah returned with a stronger position. Mossadegh was tried and sentenced to three years in solitary confinement. In December, the Shah told Ambassador Loy Henderson he was pleased the sentence had been limited: a longer term could win Mossadegh public sympathy. The Shah also wanted to retain the possibility of commuting it to banishment. Even after removing his opponent, he had to reckon with the political authority Mossadegh might still possess.

The costs were visible well before the revolution of 1979. A December 1954 American intelligence estimate described martial law, censorship, and prosecution or repression of opponents. It also identified the United States as the new government’s chief financial backer and a major influence, while noting that many Iranians held it responsible for Mossadegh’s fall. The same assessment warned of continuing instability, economic grievances, and the dangers of an entrenched ruling group refusing to share power.

The oil settlement also required work after the overthrow. In 1954, an international consortium replaced the old company’s monopoly. The British company received 40 percent participation, five American companies together received 40 percent, Shell 14 percent, and the French company CFP 6 percent. American firms obtained a substantial position in an industry previously dominated by Britain.

The agreement recognized Iranian legal title to the former Anglo-Iranian properties. Operating companies controlled the major producing and refining activities, with Iran nominating two of their seven directors. Iran was to receive half the consortium’s net profits. Ownership, operational control, and income were thus divided rather than simply restored to their old form.

That arrangement addressed the need for revenue while leaving much of the original demand for control unsatisfied. Mossadegh’s dispute had concerned authority over the industry as well as the size of a payment. The post-coup agreement improved the revenue terms while limiting Iran’s role in management.

The settlement made clear who gained access to the industry. Oil settlement, protection of Western interests, anticommunism, and support for a preferred government had all been explicit parts of the policy. A January 1954 American policy paper explicitly called for avoiding a settlement that damaged the wider oil business or encouraged other states to imitate nationalization.

## A success recorded by its authors

Knowledge of the coup spread long before officials opened its records. Iranians attributed responsibility to foreign powers; participants spoke and wrote about it; Kermit Roosevelt published a memoir in 1979. Official acknowledgment and access to the records lagged behind that public knowledge.

Wilber wrote his operational history in March 1954. The substantial text that became public in 2000 reached *The New York Times* through a leak, not a comprehensive CIA declassification in response to the newspaper’s request. In 2013, the National Security Archive published material from another internal history, *The Battle for Iran*, with the agency’s direction of the overthrow stated openly. The State Department’s 2017 retrospective documentary volume supplied further records, including the cables surrounding the failed first attempt.

The earlier official diplomatic volume, published in 1989, had omitted documentation of the CIA’s role. The 2017 preface acknowledged that deficiency and explained the controversy that helped bring a stronger statutory requirement for an accurate diplomatic record. An official history had described the diplomatic dispute while leaving out the intervention that changed its outcome.

Wilber had helped plan the operation, and his history sought lessons for future operations. Contemporary cables are less tidy: the station lost access to some military preparations, headquarters considered stopping, and victory arrived amid reports of surprise and incomplete information. The success could later be presented as more controllable than it had felt at the time.

Operation Ajax removed Mossadegh and secured a government more acceptable to Washington and London. It did not settle the struggle over foreign influence, political authority, or who could claim to speak for Iran. The records show that American officials recognized some of those problems soon afterward. Their decision was to support the new arrangement while hoping that aid, oil income, and repression of its enemies would make it durable. The people excluded by that arrangement had lost the government through which they had tried to change it.
