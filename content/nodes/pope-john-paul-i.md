On September 27, 1978, John Paul I began his general audience with a prayer his mother had taught him. He proposed to explain it a phrase at a time, as a parish catechist might. By the end, he was discussing poverty between nations, the limits of private property and the waste of an arms race.

Albino Luciani was finding a public voice that could carry a demanding thought through a familiar thing. The prayer's instruction to love one's neighbor led beyond personal kindness to the conditions under which other people lived.

He is understood to have died the next evening; his body was found the following morning. The discovery of his body on September 29, barely a month after his election, turned that unfinished pontificate into a story about what might have been prevented. Later accounts alleged murder or criticized the care he received, while Church researchers defended a natural death. A disputed account of who entered his bedroom became more widely known than the words he had spoken two days earlier.

The announcement and the decisions that followed his death deserve examination alongside the life that had ended. The surviving public record offers more than a smiling face onto which hopes of a different Church can be projected.

## The work of a month

Luciani was elected on August 26, 1978, following the death of Paul VI. Before becoming pope he had been Patriarch of Venice. At his first Angelus, on August 27, he explained his double name by recalling John XXIII and Paul VI and acknowledging his debt to them. He presented himself as someone taking up their service, conscious of what he lacked.

His chosen name was a debt acknowledged to two predecessors, even as he adopted a more personal manner of speaking. He publicly placed himself in a line of continuity.

His September 27 audience shows what his manner could do. He began with the experience of learning to pray, then explained love through movement toward the thing loved. His examples included a Venetian bell tower and the journeys he had enjoyed reading about as a boy. When he turned to neighbors, he brought the discussion into the unequal distribution of necessities, citing Paul VI's *Populorum Progressio*.

The domestic beginning led the listener toward a larger obligation. Love of a neighbor could not end at a national border, or be satisfied by keeping what one did not need while others lacked necessities.

This was religious teaching with social consequences. It reveals more than the pleasantness recalled by the nickname “the smiling pope.” His appeal made claims on the conduct of people and institutions, even if a short audience could not specify every policy those claims required.

The speech begins where a listener can follow. Fulfilling its demands is harder than recognizing its opening prayer.

An earlier audience, on September 6, offers a smaller example. Luciani recalled visiting an elderly woman who was housed, fed and warm, but unhappy because her family did not come to see her. In his account, practical provision had left a human need unmet. He used the encounter to explain what care required beyond supplying necessities.

He notices a part of the woman's life that a checklist of provisions would miss. A room can be warm without its occupant feeling loved. For his listeners, the demand is uncomfortable precisely because it is familiar: the next act may be a visit, rather than an expression of agreement with a principle.

The addresses show the claims he made and how he tried to make them intelligible. They leave open how he would have handled resistant officials, legal obstacles or a financial institution. Turning pastoral sensitivity into proof of a specific administrative program skips that unfinished work.

Luciani had little time to turn priorities into appointments, administrative changes or sustained teaching as pope. The foundation's diary records a working schedule of audiences and meetings, including his confirmation of Jean Villot as Secretary of State. Several futures remained possible. None can be treated as a decision already carried out.

## The announcement and the household

The first public account can still be read. *L'Osservatore Romano*'s English edition of October 5, 1978 reproduced a notice saying that the private secretary, not finding the pope in his chapel at about 5:30 a.m., looked in the bedroom and found him dead. The light was on; he appeared to have been reading. The physician was reported to have placed death around eleven the previous evening and attributed it to acute coronary thrombosis, a clot affecting the heart's blood supply.

The notice does not name *The Imitation of Christ*. That book has become part of many retellings, but it should not be placed in the original announcement and then exposed as a false detail the announcement did not contain.

The notice also matters for what it marks as approximate. It reports a presumed time of death, rather than a witnessed event at eleven o'clock. A person reading at that hour and a doctor later estimating that hour are different sources of information. Repeating the estimate without its basis can make a reconstruction seem more exact than the original report allowed.

The identity of the finder presents a more substantial discrepancy. The John Paul I Vatican Foundation's later diary names Sisters Vincenza Taffarel and Margherita Marin as discovering the body at about 5:20. In the same account, Dr. Renato Buzzonetti confirmed death at six; the public radio announcement followed at 7:30. Preservation of the body began that evening.

The foundation is associated with Luciani's cause for canonization. Its diary is a later institutional reconstruction. Even so, its naming of the sisters directly conflicts with the first notice's account of the discovery. The discrepancy cannot be dismissed as the invention of an outside critic.

In reporting on the beatification in 2022, the Associated Press interviewed Stefania Falasca, the cause's deputy postulator. Its account also describes the sisters being told not to say that they had found him, out of concern about how a woman entering the papal bedroom would be perceived. That explanation is reported testimony about the household's response. It establishes neither the cause of death nor the intentions of every official who conveyed the first account.

It does identify a failure of judgment. The people whose work placed them closest to the event were less convenient to name than a secretary. Concern about appearances displaced an account of what they said they had actually done.

An announcement can respect privacy while accurately reporting the details it chooses to give. Substituting a more acceptable witness weakens confidence in the rest of the account and makes later correction harder. Readers then have to ask whether a new detail comes from a better source or another adjustment to the story.

The discrepancy makes it necessary to seek the witnesses' accounts, compare their dates and preserve their differences. It cannot identify a larger crime on its own. The false account creates a question about the announcement. Evidence about the death must still answer a different question.

## A diagnosis without an autopsy

No autopsy was performed. That absence limits how confidently an exact cause can be reconstructed, especially when subsequent accounts dispute symptoms, medication or timing. It does not turn poisoning into the default explanation.

The initial notice gives a clinical attribution of coronary thrombosis. A doctor confirming that someone is dead and a doctor establishing why the death occurred are performing different tasks. The first can be certain while the second remains an inference from the available circumstances and medical history.

At a 2022 Vatican press conference, Falasca described doctors' reports and medical records collected during the canonization inquiry. According to the official report of her remarks, the physicians had not considered an autopsy necessary. She presented a heart attack as the explanation and described chest discomfort before Luciani retired. This was the position of the researcher working on the cause, based on records she had examined. It was not the result of a postmortem examination performed in 1978.

Claims of neglect need examination too. John Cornwell reached a natural-death conclusion in *A Thief in the Night*, published in 1989, while criticizing the care and support Luciani received. In a direct interview with the Associated Press in 2022, he again described inadequate help and attention to the pope's health. The criticism remained part of his interpretation, not an agreed finding shared by all later researchers.

Knowledge of the death changes how an earlier symptom looks. To establish preventable neglect would require following what a particular person observed, what was communicated, what advice was available and what action could reasonably have been taken. A later account of chest pain cannot by itself answer all those questions.

A remembered farewell or a normal day's work cannot certify the absence of illness. Such recollections can help reconstruct a person's evening without deciding the medical issue. Their value should not depend on forcing them to favor either a murder narrative or a picture of inevitable natural death.

Other sources of knowledge survive the missing autopsy. Documents, testimony, a medical history and the chronology of the household remain open to examination. Nor could an autopsy by itself have identified who arranged a death, even if it had supplied decisive evidence about its physical cause.

## The threatened reformer

David Yallop's *In God's Name*, first published in 1984, gave the murder account its best-known form. The publisher's description presents a web of Church corruption, Masonic and criminal connections, and the later death of banker Roberto Calvi. The framing makes Luciani's death the center of a wider financial story.

The account is compelling partly because it gives the lost future a shape. A new pope could alter appointments, order an inquiry or end a relationship that benefited others. If someone stood to lose greatly, the death of a reforming pope could look like the removal of a threat.

But a threatened-reformer account needs more than the possibility of reform and the existence of people with reasons to resist it. It needs to establish what Luciani had decided, who knew of that decision and how the alleged threat became an act against him. These are historical links, not details supplied automatically by the character of the institution.

His public teaching gives reasons to take his concern for justice seriously. It does not establish a final-night order to dismiss particular bankers. A conversation with Villot belongs in the chronology. Its occurrence does not disclose every subject discussed or prove that a resulting decision triggered a killing.

Later financial revelations supply important context. [[p2-banco-ambrosiano]] examines the lodge's documented organization, Ambrosiano's foreign lending and the IOR's disputed obligations. It also follows the distinction between the later finding that Calvi was murdered and the acquittals of those charged with his killing. Those records deserve to be read for what they establish.

They cannot be worked backward as if Calvi's death in 1982 completed a proof of Luciani's murder in 1978. A demonstrable crime in a related milieu can justify further inquiry. Connecting it to another death still requires evidence of that connection.

An assassination theory can overstate its case while the Vatican's financial problems remain serious and its first announcement remains inaccurate. An institution can deserve serious criticism without being guilty of the particular killing alleged against it.

## What beatification changed

Pope Francis beatified John Paul I on September 4, 2022. In the homily published by the Holy See, he emphasized service, humility and a faith expressed without self-importance. He also returned to Luciani's words from the September 27 audience.

Beatification belongs to the Church's recognition of a person's holiness. It is not a criminal judgment acquitting every official who dealt with the person, and it is not a substitute for identifying the cause of death. Calling it the Church's most formal declaration that no hidden crime occurred asks the ceremony to answer a question it was not designed to decide.

The historical work associated with the cause gathered testimony and documents and gave the sisters' accounts a public place. That material should be evaluated through its provenance and consistency. Rejecting everything because it was collected within the Church would discard evidence; accepting every conclusion because of the institution's authority would stop evaluating it.

The records examined here do not establish murder. The clinical account and later research favor natural death, although they cannot supply the postmortem examination that was not performed. The first announcement's treatment of the witnesses remains a failure that does not need a poisoner to make it consequential.

Luciani also left something less dependent on reconstructing a private room: words he had chosen to speak in public. In the September 27 audience, the prayer learned from his mother became an argument about how people treated one another and what they owed strangers. Whatever might have followed, that was already part of his pontificate. It deserves a place in his history that an imagined future cannot occupy.
