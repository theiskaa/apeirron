The jury’s verdict was unlawful killing. When the inquest into Diana’s death ended in April 2008, those words seemed to offer a different answer from the familiar account of a car crash. But the jury specified what it meant: grossly negligent driving by Henri Paul and by the vehicles following his Mercedes.

It was a finding of grave responsibility for a fatal pursuit. It was not a finding that the royal family or an intelligence service had arranged an assassination. The difference matters throughout this case, where evidence of dangerous conduct, missing information, and genuine deception has repeatedly been assembled into a much larger story.

Diana, Dodi Fayed, and Paul died after the Mercedes struck a pillar in the Alma underpass in Paris on August 31, 1997. Bodyguard Trevor Rees-Jones survived with severe injuries. None of the four was wearing a seatbelt. Diana was thirty-six.

Mohamed Al-Fayed alleged that Prince Philip had ordered MI6 to kill his son and Diana. He believed they intended to marry and that she was pregnant, placing an impending change in the royal family at the center of his proposed motive. The allegation brought the couple’s relationship, her earlier fears, and the conduct of intelligence services into a prolonged inquiry. The evidence that emerged supports a collision involving high speed, an impaired driver, and pursuit, while exposing shortcomings in parts of the investigation.

## A plan made late that evening

Diana and Dodi had arrived in Paris after a holiday in the Mediterranean. His father, Mohamed Al-Fayed, owned the Ritz, where they spent the evening. Photographers followed their movements, and a plan developed to leave through the rear of the hotel in a different car from the one ordinarily used for the couple.

Paul, the Ritz’s acting head of security, had gone off duty earlier and was called back. He drove the Mercedes S280. Rees-Jones sat in front; Diana and Dodi were in the rear. They left through the rue Cambon exit shortly after midnight, intending to return to the Fayed apartment near the Champs-Élysées. The car traveled along the embankment before entering the underpass.

According to the reconstruction evidence reviewed by the coroner, the Mercedes was traveling at about 65 miles an hour when it struck the pillar, roughly twice the road’s speed limit. At that speed, a short urban sequence of a bend, merging traffic, a descent, a wall, and exposed pillars left little room for an error to remain harmless.

The speed estimate came from reconstructing the damage and the collision, including comparison with manufacturer crash tests. It was not a reading preserved on the speedometer. Paget records that photographs showed the instruments at zero after impact. Examiners also inspected the vehicle’s mechanical systems and found no defect or interference that could account for loss of control. The damage therefore provided a way to check witnesses’ impressions of the car’s speed.

The timing of the departure plan became important to the conspiracy allegation. The [coroner’s ruling on possible verdicts](https://static1.squarespace.com/static/5b283d0ffcf7fd4e0101bd20/t/5b84d15caa4a992b1db9d9b0/1535431005698/Diana%2C%2BPrincess%2Bof%2BWales%2Band%2BDodi%2BAl%2BFayed.pdf) records that Paul learned about using a third car and the rear exit at around 10.30 p.m. The plan had been devised by Dodi and communicated through a Ritz colleague. The coroner noted that this account was supported by CCTV and accepted by the parties to the inquest.

A staged ambush would consequently require information about a late change of car, departure point, and route. Paul had been away from the hotel between roughly 7 and 10 p.m., but that gap preceded the new plan. It could not by itself establish that he had passed on arrangements he did not yet know.

The coroner also addressed a shorter gap in the later CCTV coverage during which a phone call was theoretically possible. There was no record or witness connecting that gap to a communication about the new arrangements. The late decision left the proposed ambush dependent on another unestablished event: someone passing on the route in time to organize it.

## The collision with another car was real

Paint transfer and broken light components supported a glancing collision between the right front of the Mercedes and the left rear of a slower-moving Uno, near the entrance to the underpass. The identity of that car and its driver was never established.

The coroner treated this contact as a significant contributor to the loss of control. Reconstruction experts disagreed about its exact effect on the Mercedes’ subsequent course, but they agreed that the contact occurred. The reconstruction had to include a car whose driver remained unknown.

At the inquest, Al-Fayed’s counsel, Michael Mansfield, did not argue that the Fiat’s driver was part of the plot. His proposed reconstruction concerned another vehicle ahead and a motorcycle behind, with a deliberate bright light disorienting Paul. The untraced Fiat and the proposed blocking vehicle therefore occupied different roles in his argument.

Witnesses described cars and motorcycles from different positions, some on the opposite carriageway. One witness used the language of blocking, yet had also said he could not tell whether a car was being driven slowly on purpose and had seen no dangerous maneuver by it. Another car’s occupants said the Mercedes was already out of control behind them and that no vehicle lay between them.

There were reports of bright lights. Some witnesses understood them as camera flashes. One acknowledged that a light could have come from the Mercedes’ headlights as it swung around after control was lost. François Levistre’s account of a blinding flash and motorcycle occupants making signals was more suggestive of an operation, but the coroner identified inconsistencies in his descriptions, difficulties with his claimed view, and a later unsupported account involving bullet casings.

The inquiry compared when people looked, where they were, what they originally said, and whether another account supported the distinctive part of their observation. A flash seen after a car has begun to spin cannot explain the initial loss of control. An unidentified car ahead of a fast-moving Mercedes need not have been deliberately restricting its speed.

The Uno’s driver could have supplied another account of those seconds. Without it, the paint and debris establish contact while leaving that person’s conduct and intentions less clear.

## What the tests can tell us about Henri Paul

Paul did not appear visibly drunk in the Ritz footage. The [Operation Paget report](https://downloads.bbc.co.uk/news/nol/shared/bsp/hi/pdfs/14_12_06_diana_report.pdf), the Metropolitan Police investigation published in 2006, explicitly acknowledged his apparently capable movements. The footage is part of the case against a simplified picture of him as incapable of functioning. It gives us movements to observe, rather than a measurement of blood alcohol. A person can walk, converse, and perform familiar movements while having impaired judgment and driving ability. The relevant question is whether the laboratory results were reliable and belonged to Paul.

Those questions were examined through more than the first blood sample. Tests reported alcohol concentrations around 1.74 grams per liter, over three times the French driving limit. Blood collected during a second examination several days later produced a similar result. Alcohol was also found in other samples from the original examination, including fluid from the eye and urine. The interpretation rested on the pattern of findings, not just a single disputed bottle.

There were genuine problems with the record. Paget’s own forensic adviser, Richard Shepherd, criticized the lack of clarity in sampling, labeling, and documentation. Blood described as cardiac blood had apparently been collected from the chest cavity. The difference could affect the interpretation of contamination and some test results. A document that should have clarified the sample sites was missing from its expected place in the French dossier and had been replaced by an unrelated document.

Carbon monoxide findings require particular care. An unusually high reading of 20.7 percent carboxyhemoglobin—the proportion of hemoglobin bound to carbon monoxide—came from a chest-cavity sample. The report considered that site vulnerable to contamination and did not regard the figure as a dependable measurement of Paul’s condition before death. A lower but still unusual reading came from another sampling site. Toxicologist Robert Forrest discussed possible contributions from smoking and changes or interference after death, while acknowledging that the discrepancy could not be convincingly explained if both readings were accepted as accurate measurements of the original state.

The discrepancy remained an analytical difficulty. Using it to predict how Paul should have looked in the hotel assumes that the disputed measurement accurately represented his condition before death; that was precisely the assumption the experts were questioning.

DNA testing supplied evidence about identity, with an important limit. Retained blood samples were matched to Paul through comparison with his mother. Some samples used in the original toxicology had already been consumed and could not themselves be DNA-tested. The genetic evidence establishes the identity of the retained material, with less direct reach over samples no longer available.

The later directly collected blood, retained samples, compatible findings across different materials, and prescription history supported the conclusion that the toxicology concerned Paul. His doctor had prescribed drugs found in the samples. The investigators did not rely solely on a mortuary label to connect them to him.

The inquest ultimately included impairment of judgment through alcohol among the factors contributing to the crash. That judgment concerns his ability to undertake this drive, where the speed and pressure demanded rapid decisions. Visible coordination in a hotel corridor and the absence of every possible sign of long-term alcohol misuse would not establish that ability.

## The intelligence allegation changed as it was examined

Former MI6 officer Richard Tomlinson supplied a possible link between intelligence methods and the crash. He described a proposal to assassinate a Serbian leader through a staged car accident involving a tunnel and a powerful flash. If that account were reliable, the resemblance to the alleged Paris ambush would merit attention.

Paget’s report records an officer acknowledging that he had written an assassination proposal concerning a particular extremist leader, for discussion by his superiors. He disputed Tomlinson’s identification of the target, the date, and the operational details. Other officers supported that account. The distinction between a proposal and an authorized operation remained important even within the intelligence evidence.

The investigation also compared Tomlinson’s descriptions from before and after Diana’s death. His later accounts contained details absent from the earlier ones. When questioned, he accepted that he might have combined his knowledge of technical capabilities with his memory of the proposal. The apparent match to the tunnel therefore depended on a recollection whose content had changed after the event it was being used to explain.

Tomlinson also suggested that Paul had been an intelligence source. Paget records that he had not actually seen Paul’s name in an MI6 file. His account concerned someone in a hotel security role and an inference about Paul’s suitability. That is a weaker claim than identifying a named person in a contemporary service record.

Paul’s money was another issue. He possessed cash and had income that could not be fully explained through his recorded salary. Friends and relatives described tips and money advanced for wealthy hotel guests, and the investigation considered those explanations. The coroner nevertheless acknowledged unexplained income. The existence of that money was a reason to investigate its source; identifying it as payment for the fatal journey required another connection. Some unexplained income preceded Diana and Dodi’s relationship.

There are limits to an investigation that depends partly on intelligence services describing their own records. Interviewing officers and reviewing files does not make every possibility independently observable. But in this case the evidence against the specific allegations included changes in the principal accuser’s account and the timing of the journey’s arrangements, not only institutional denials. Those difficulties remain even if one starts with little confidence in MI6.

## Diana’s fears were part of the evidence

Diana had spoken of the possibility of being harmed in a car. Lord Mishcon, her legal adviser, recorded an October 1995 meeting at which she said unnamed reliable sources had warned her of efforts to get rid of her or leave her injured and discredited. A prepared brake failure was among the possibilities she described, with an expectation of action by April 1996—more than a year before the Paris crash. The note was Mishcon’s record of their conversation, not a second letter written by Diana.

Mishcon brought it to senior Metropolitan Police officers in September 1997. They agreed to revisit it if the crash appeared suspicious. That decision deserves scrutiny: an early judgment that a death was accidental helped determine whether a prior warning would be treated as relevant. The warning later became part of the evidence examined by Paget and the inquest.

The separate note made public by Paul Burrell described fears of a car accident involving brake failure and head injury. Its date was uncertain. Burrell initially placed it in 1996 and then thought 1995 more likely. Paget reported doubts and disagreements among people who knew Diana about the document and its meaning. The two records have different authors, different histories of disclosure, and, in Burrell’s case, an uncertain date. They record fears whose source was never established.

Her fear should not be reduced to a diagnosis applied at a distance. There were real reasons for distrust in her surroundings, including press intrusion and information supplied to her about people close to her. The [Dyson investigation, published in 2021](https://downloads.bbc.co.uk/aboutthebbc/reports/reports/dyson-report-20-may-21.pdf), found that Martin Bashir used fabricated bank statements to deceive Earl Spencer and secure an introduction to Diana before the *Panorama* interview. The documents purported to show payments involving people in their circle. Dyson also criticized the BBC’s subsequent inquiry and handling of the affair.

Dyson’s findings give her distrust a context of actual deception, followed by institutional failure. They leave the source of the warning recorded by Mishcon unidentified, and the proposed connection between the warning and the Paris journey unestablished.

The warning also specified a mechanism that the crash investigation did not establish. The Mercedes was examined, and the account of the crash turned on speed, contact with another car, loss of control, and the pillar. Treating any fatal road collision as fulfillment of a prediction about brake sabotage removes the part of the prediction that could distinguish it from another kind of crash.

## Treatment, embalming, and the pregnancy claim

Diana survived the impact and received treatment from an off-duty doctor, rescuers, and a medical team. The interval between the crash, around 12.22 a.m., and her arrival at the Pitié-Salpêtrière Hospital, around 2.06 a.m., has often been presented as an inexplicable drive of nearly two hours. Much of that interval was spent extracting and treating her at the scene.

The medical account in Paget describes deterioration during her removal from the car, cardiac arrest and resuscitation, and unstable blood pressure. The French service brought a doctor and intensive treatment to the roadside. During transport, the doctor ordered the ambulance to stop when her pressure fell again, so that he could reassess and stabilize her. The hospital had been selected by other coordinating doctors for its facilities and available specialists.

That chronology explains why dividing a few miles by the total elapsed time produces a misleading picture. It does not settle every clinical question about the relative merits of roadside treatment and faster transport. Reviewers considered the injury exceptionally difficult to survive and found no evidence of a deliberate effort to withhold care. The injury ultimately identified involved a tear in the left pulmonary vein and adjacent heart tissue, with severe internal bleeding.

Early accounts also varied over her level of consciousness. Paget recorded rescuers describing movement and speech before she deteriorated. A later report that she had spoken was therefore not a new contradiction of an official position that she had been continuously unconscious. A patient speaking after a collision can still have a grave internal injury.

After her death, a French pathologist made an external examination of her body. It was then partially embalmed in Paris before being returned to Britain. The preparation took place before the full British post-mortem examination, and questions were raised about authorization and possible effects on evidence. Paget traced the decision through hospital, police, consular, and embalming personnel. The embalmer described relying on a medical certificate, police authorization, and agreement from the British consul-general. He considered treatment necessary to make her presentable for family viewing in a hot hospital room. The report defended the legality of the process while recording that some authorizations were verbal and that Al-Fayed was challenging it in France. Those are identifiable decisions and a contested procedure, rather than an order traced to a royal household.

The pregnancy question also had evidence independent of the embalmed body. No pregnancy test had been performed during the emergency treatment in France. Later investigators tested blood from the Mercedes’ carpet, identified as Diana’s and deposited before hospital transfusions. They did not detect HCG, a hormone used in pregnancy testing. The scientist’s interpretation was cautious: the result weighed against a pregnancy of several weeks but carried less certainty as the possible conception date approached the time of death.

A negative result in an old stain is not an absolute exclusion of every earliest-stage possibility. Combined with the post-mortem findings and other evidence, it provided no support for Al-Fayed’s asserted pregnancy. The claim that embalming removed the entire subject from scientific examination is therefore wrong.

Dodi did purchase a ring. Paget accepted that he might have intended to propose, while finding the weight of evidence against an agreed engagement. An intended proposal, an accepted proposal, and a known pregnancy would have different implications for the alleged motive. A purchased ring cannot establish all three.

## What the jury decided

The French inquiry, Operation Paget, and the British inquest were separate processes. Paget investigated allegations of a conspiracy; the inquest heard evidence publicly and considered how Diana and Dodi died. The police report supplied evidence for scrutiny rather than replacing the jury’s task.

The coroner declined to leave a staged-crash verdict to the jury because he found no evidential basis on which it could safely be returned. Al-Fayed’s representatives argued for it. The inquest had spent months receiving conspiracy-related material before he made that decision about which conclusions the evidence could sustain.

The parties also disagreed about the paparazzi. The Metropolitan Police opposed leaving unlawful killing on the basis of the pursuing drivers’ conduct; the Ritz argued that it should be available. The coroner allowed that route. He identified evidence of close following, acceleration to keep up, and a race-like journey through a hazardous city environment.

On April 7, 2008, nine jurors agreed that the deaths resulted from grossly negligent driving by Paul and the following vehicles. The contributing role of the missing seatbelts and the impact with the pillar was unanimous. The [verdict transcript](https://toronto.citynews.ca/2008/04/07/final-verdict-on-princess-dis-death-driver-and-paparazzi-the-only-ones-responsible/) records those conclusions separately.

Unlawful killing here meant conduct sufficiently grave to meet the relevant manslaughter standard. It was stronger than a finding of simple misfortune, and it included responsibility beyond Paul. The verdict addressed how the deaths occurred; it was not a criminal conviction of named surviving drivers.

## A public life reduced to its ending

Diana’s public life had purposes beyond her conflict with the royal household. Her [1997 visit to a mine-clearance site in Angola](https://www.halotrust.org/us/news/20-years-after-dianas-visit-landmines-are-still-killing-angolas-children/) helped bring the consequences of landmines before a large public. She used the public attention around her to direct it toward people injured in a war they could not simply leave behind.

The press relationship had a more immediate place in her death. People pursuing a commercially valuable photograph were among the drivers whose conduct the jury found grossly negligent. The commercial pursuit had translated into decisions about speed, distance, and whether to keep following through a dangerous road environment.

The record leaves the Fiat’s driver unidentified and does not make every laboratory discrepancy disappear. It also gives a substantial account of the collision and the actions that made it fatal. The later samples, original statements, timing of the departure, and jury’s words give reasons for the conclusion beyond a general appeal to trust the authorities.

Diana had reasons to distrust people and institutions around her. That truth can coexist with the finding that she died in a collision brought about by dangerous driving. The pursuit, speed, alcohol impairment, and lack of seatbelts describe risks that accumulated during a short journey. Together they were enough to end her life.
