In January 2013, BP Exploration and Production Inc. pleaded guilty to crimes arising from the Deepwater Horizon disaster and was placed on probation. The defendant was a company. The court could order payments and supervision of its future conduct, while the cases against individual employees proceeded separately.

A company able to own property and enter contracts can also be the party that owes compensation or answers a criminal charge. Calling it a legal person makes those obligations possible. It also raises a harder question: what happens when the assets of that person cannot cover the harm?

The workers, shareholders and neighbors of a business stand in different relationships to it. Some can negotiate terms before taking a risk; others encounter the consequences without being asked. In the United States, corporate identity, shareholder liability, directors' duties and constitutional protections shape those relationships through distinct rules. The rules determine how far an obligation reaches beyond the company itself.

## A company can continue when its people leave

Delaware's corporation statute gives a company several familiar capabilities. Its [enumerated powers](https://delcode.delaware.gov/title8/c001/sc02/index.html) include owning property, making contracts, borrowing and suing or being sued in the corporate name. It also permits continuing existence unless the certificate of incorporation sets a limit, while providing for winding up and dissolution.

Continuity allows people to organize work that lasts beyond a particular manager's employment or an investor's involvement. A laboratory does not need to be transferred to a new owner each time a manager leaves. Contracts can continue while the people responsible for carrying them out change. That continuity is sometimes described as immortality. The company can still be dissolved or become insolvent, and the business sustaining it can fail.

The separation also limits which obligations reach the shareholders personally. Delaware's [section 102(b)(6)](https://delcode.delaware.gov/title8/c001/sc01/index.html) provides that shareholders generally are not personally liable for corporate debts, while preserving liability that follows from their own conduct or acts. The certificate of incorporation can also impose personal liability on specified terms.

An investor may lose the whole value of the shares. The company borrowing money does not ordinarily put that investor's home or unrelated savings behind the loan. This makes it easier to contribute to an enterprise whose operations the investor cannot supervise personally.

For someone harmed by the enterprise, the boundary looks different. A lender can investigate a borrower, negotiate security or refuse a loan. A neighbor exposed to a company's pollution may never have had a corresponding opportunity to choose the risk. If the company lacks enough assets to satisfy a valid claim, recognizing its liability does not create the missing money.

A valid claim against an insolvent company can leave someone unpaid. To reach a shareholder or manager personally, there must be another basis for liability, such as that person's own wrongful conduct. Holding shares alone does not establish it.

## The railroad case and an earlier college

Corporate personhood is often traced to a reporter's note in *Santa Clara County v. Southern Pacific Railroad* in 1886. The [published report](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/118/394/) records Chief Justice Morrison Waite stating before argument that the justices regarded the Fourteenth Amendment's equal-protection provision as applying to the corporations involved.

The opinion then resolved the tax dispute on a narrower ground. California's state assessment had included fences that were assessable by local authorities, and the invalid part could not be separated adequately from the whole. That allowed the Court to affirm judgment for the railroads without deciding the other constitutional questions raised in the case.

The preliminary statement and the opinion's reasoning need to be distinguished. The report's unusual treatment of equal protection did not create the whole legal arrangement of a corporation acting as a separate party.

Long before 1886, Chief Justice John Marshall had described the corporation's separate identity in [*Dartmouth College v. Woodward*](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/17/518/), decided in 1819. He explained how continuity allowed property and institutional work to persist through a succession of people. The institution at issue was a college. Incorporation has uses beyond profit-seeking business.

The politically important question concerns the protection being claimed. An entity's ability to hold property does not by itself settle its right to fund an election advertisement. Those are different legal questions, with different arguments about the interests at stake.

## What an obligation to shareholders requires

Joel Bakan's *The Corporation* makes a deliberately provocative claim about institutional character. The [publisher's account of the book](https://www.simonandschuster.com/books/The-Corporation/Joel-Bakan/9781439134948) presents the corporation as legally structured to pursue its economic interest regardless of consequences, comparing its behavior with a psychopathic personality. The associated film helped make that comparison familiar.

Bakan directs attention to incentives that can survive a change of personnel. A decision-maker can be rewarded for saving the company money while costs fall on people who had no say in the decision. If a factory avoids paying for waste treatment, the saving appears in its accounts while downstream damage may appear in a household's water bill, lost income or health. Economists call a cost imposed on others in this way an externality.

The example exposes an incentive while leaving the decision to be investigated: who approved it, what they knew and how affected people can obtain a remedy. Describing the institution as a psychopath cannot substitute for that investigation. The institutional comparison is an argument about design, not a clinical assessment.

The statute also leaves room for purposes beyond an immediate financial return. It expressly allows [donations for public welfare, charitable, scientific and educational purposes](https://delcode.delaware.gov/title8/c001/sc02/index.html). Directors have a legal power to make such donations; another person's benefit is not automatically an unlawful use of company money.

The obligations to investors are nevertheless real. In [*eBay Domestic Holdings v. Newmark*](https://law.justia.com/cases/delaware/court-of-chancery/2010/143440-1.html), decided in September 2010, Delaware's Court of Chancery examined measures adopted by Craig Newmark and chief executive Jim Buckmaster, who together controlled craigslist. eBay owned just over a quarter of the shares and had become a competitor. A rights plan restricted its ability to buy more shares and hampered its ability to sell its stake to an outside buyer. Newmark and Buckmaster defended the plan partly by invoking the protection of craigslist's culture and service-oriented approach.

The court rejected that justification. It found that the controllers had not established the claimed connection between protecting the culture and promoting stockholder value, and had acted to punish eBay for competing. Having chosen a for-profit Delaware corporation and accepted an outside investor, they could not defend the plan through a policy openly rejecting that investor's economic interest. The court rescinded the rights plan and another challenged measure that diluted eBay's stake, while upholding a separate change to the way the board was elected. Its response was to undo particular decisions, rather than direct the company to adopt a complete new business model.

People can value a service because of something its founders do not want to monetize. An outside investor may value an opportunity to monetize it. The controllers' preferences cannot settle the conflict simply because they hold a majority; the rights accompanying the investment matter too.

The judgment required the controllers to respect the minority investor's interests when taking those actions. It left many choices about services, prices and investment to the company. The chosen form and the commitments made to investors constrain the controllers without determining every operational choice in advance.

## A guilty company and separate individual cases

The Deepwater Horizon disaster shows why corporate responsibility and individual responsibility must be followed separately. Eleven workers died after the rig exploded in April 2010. The subsequent proceedings were directed at named legal entities and people, under particular environmental and criminal laws.

On January 29, 2013, BP Exploration and Production Inc. [pleaded guilty to fourteen criminal counts and was sentenced](https://www.justice.gov/archives/opa/pr/bp-exploration-and-production-inc-pleads-guilty-sentencedto-pay-record-4-billion-crimes). The counts included eleven for felony manslaughter, as well as obstruction of Congress and environmental offenses. The resolution required $4 billion in criminal fines and penalties, probation and monitoring.

In that plea, the company admitted that its well-site leaders had observed indications that the well was not secure and had failed to take appropriate steps to prevent the blowout. It also admitted misleading Congress about the oil flow. The accepted plea tied corporate liability to failures at the well and in the response to Congress.

The cases against individuals had different outcomes. The Justice Department's [May 2016 enforcement bulletin](https://www.justice.gov/enrd/page/file/851586/dl) records that supervisor Donald Vidrine received ten months of probation after pleading guilty to a misdemeanor Clean Water Act violation. A jury acquitted supervisor Robert Kaluza of a misdemeanor violation. Former BP vice president David Rainey was acquitted of making false statements after an obstruction count had been dismissed.

The company's admission and an individual's guilt remained separate issues in the proceedings. Vidrine's plea, Kaluza's acquittal and the company's conviction are distinct results. They cannot be replaced by a general claim that limited liability prevented any prosecution of the people involved.

Civil enforcement supplied another route. The [EPA's record of the proceedings](https://www.epa.gov/enforcement/deepwater-horizon-bp-gulf-mexico-oil-spill) describes a $5.5 billion Clean Water Act penalty and provisions for natural-resource damages. It distinguishes those proceedings from private medical and economic-damage claims. The proceedings involved several defendants with different obligations.

The legal consequences included both payments and measures directed at future conduct. Money for restoration could support real work after the damage; monitoring could change how later decisions were made. They addressed different needs. Neither should be counted as a complete recovery of the lives and opportunities lost in the disaster.

## The right to speak, and the means to be heard

Corporate constitutional protections can also change the rules under which the institution is regulated. [*Citizens United v. Federal Election Commission*](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/558/310/), decided in 2010, concerned a nonprofit corporation's film about Hillary Clinton and restrictions on corporate election-related spending.

The majority held that the government could not suppress independent political expenditures on the basis of the speaker's corporate identity. Its argument concerned the protection of political expression, including expression organized through associations. The ruling did not make a company a voter or confer every right a human being possesses.

The Court retained the distinction between independent expenditures and direct contributions to candidates. It also upheld the challenged disclaimer and disclosure requirements as applied to the film and its advertisements. Independent spending, contributions and disclosure remained separate legal questions.

The decision still raises a democratic objection. A legal freedom to speak does not distribute the resources required to reach an audience. An institution with accumulated funds can repeat its message, employ specialists and remain active through changes in personnel. People affected by its operations may have a much harder time making themselves heard.

There is also a serious argument for protecting collective expression. People often need an organization to publish, campaign or make a film together. A rule aimed at powerful businesses can reach smaller associations too. Protecting that activity while limiting the advantages of concentrated economic resources requires choices about the particular kind of spending. The label of corporate personhood leaves those choices unresolved.

## Changing the promise, then asking who can enforce it

The corporation's purpose can itself be altered through law. Delaware's [public benefit corporation provisions](https://delcode.delaware.gov/title8/c001/sc15/index.html) require directors of that form of company to balance shareholders' financial interests, the interests of those materially affected by the company's conduct and the public benefits named in its certificate of incorporation.

The public purpose becomes part of the governing duty. Yet the existence of a public purpose does not automatically give every beneficiary a right to sue its directors. Section 365 denies an automatic duty to a person simply because that person's interests are affected, and section 367 imposes share-ownership conditions on actions to enforce the balancing requirement.

The law can therefore require consideration of affected people without giving each of them control over enforcement. A neighbor without shares does not acquire a balancing-duty claim simply by living beside the company. Other applicable law may supply a different claim, but the public-benefit designation does not itself settle that question.

A duty has practical force through the people able to enforce it. Someone affected by the company may welcome its purpose and still lack a direct means to make the board honor it. Information and access to a remedy determine whether that person can do more than appeal to the board's goodwill.

The business corporation combines capabilities that are useful with boundaries that can be costly. It lets property, work and obligations continue through changes of people. It can also concentrate decisions while dispersing losses. The people outside its contracts are often those with the least opportunity to negotiate how that dispersal works.

Bakan's provocation directs attention toward that institutional design. Its value does not depend on treating a legal fiction as a medical diagnosis. The harder task is to arrange ownership, duties and enforcement so that the people making a decision cannot treat everyone outside the balance sheet as someone else's responsibility.
